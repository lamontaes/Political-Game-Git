# Words reads

**A words change is read as played, in drawn places, on a fresh draw, with the lines quoted, before it goes anywhere.** Tests say a rule holds. The read says what a player gets. On Nov 14 the conversation engine passed 15 of 18 test files and, read as played, had all 18 strangers in three towns say the same two questions.

## What the builder sends

1. The head's full sha and what a player now sees, in one sentence.
2. The gate with the train or main merged in: changed tests on the named seed and on a fresh seed, typecheck, `npm run guards`, `tests/english-parts-banks.test.ts`, `npm run words:rules` twice with a clean tree, `npm run words:composed -- --check`, zero-dice, `release:check --mode pr`. Say which reds are also red on the clean base. Never raise a baseline or lower what a test expects to pass.
3. The lines as played, whole, in order, with who speaks: at least three places drawn from all 56 on a seed nobody chose, the places passed over named, in a file under the shared project folder. For a screen, a picture at 1440 by 900 before and after. The change's own subject must appear: a year of papers with no endorsement in it is not a read of an endorsement brief.
4. Faults the builder saw while reading, left in.

## What the lead does

- Reads every line, not a sample, and counts where a count settles it (how many bodies repeat, how many talks ask a question).
- Rules in numbered rules a builder can check, in a file beside the lines, and says which reds are the branch's.
- Writes the lines itself when a builder lists the piece group, the facts a line may read, who speaks to whom and how many wordings are needed. A subject whose pieces are missing stays unoffered; a stand-in line is never written.
- Posts `Words lead: train-ready #N <full sha>` only after the gate and the read, quoting lines as played. A ruling from the owner that a cleared change breaks withdraws the line the same hour.
- A missing picture never holds words back (owner, Nov 14: "do not let pics hold it back. it can be generated later"). The scene plays on its fallback and is named in the wanted-pictures list.

## Rules 30 to 37: election result stories

30. A race for several seats is one story at each outlet, naming its winners together and the first who missed, with how far back.
31. A name in full once, then the last name; two people with one last name keep full names, in headlines too.
32. One name on the ballot is "ran unopposed" or "elected without opposition": no share, no "beat", no vote total. As many names as seats is unopposed.
33. A whole-number share only in a race for one seat with two or more on the ballot; under half, the field is said ("in a field of four"). Counts in figures.
34. Offices in lower case in a sentence; a district office in a headline is "District 5 commissioner". The body by its name as people say it, from the one name writer.
35. The runner-up comes from that race's own count.
36. Headline by last name; the body opens with the race, the count and the runner-up, turnout last.
37. No two outlets print the same body of one race. Wordings are picked by the outlet's order among those that printed it, and the test checks every place it draws.

## Rules 38 to 49: speeches

38. The belief leads an ask at most once in an address; the other asks come first, fold the belief in or leave it out. No address has two sentences opening "Because I".
39. Years are said once; counts under ten in words.
40. The body is named in the greeting and the first ask, then "this council".
41. The close does not repeat an ask.
42. A measure is named once, then "it".
43. An opponent names the measure plainly or their own way, never in its supporters' words.
44. The condition comes before its answer.
45. A figure is called trouble only where the record says so, is said once, and the line after it says what it means with no number.
46. A small amount is not offered as a remedy; with no record of the ask's size, no amount is said.
47. A bad term always shows, whatever kind the address is.
48. A good term says one good thing, the improved measure nearest the address's subject.
49. One "On {theme}," opener at most; the place word comes from the record ("across Guam", never "the state" in a territory); a person is thanked once.

## Rule 50: dialogue is only dialogue (owner, Nov 14)

"None of those count lines or anything like that should pop up in dialogue. It should literally just be dialogue." Nobody speaks a count, tally or progress of the player's own doing. A clerk says once what the office requires and by when; after that people say "that many". Numbers a player needs live on a screen. A clerk telling you your papers are in, or that three others are running, is talk and stays.

## Rules 51 to 61: conversation

51. A stranger does not know you: no name, one greeting, none of the lines for people who have met. Names come out by being given.
52. Nobody asks what they can see or already know. A stranger opens on why the two are in one place or by saying who they are.
53. One fact once in a talk.
54. The question fits the person's record (someone still in school is not asked where they went).
55. A talk never ends on the player's answer.
56. Six strangers in a day are six talks: what a person raises comes from that person.
57. People at home ask questions, each with a record behind it (the player's day, the calendar, something heard).
58. One opening line carries one thing.
59. A line about the President says which side the President took, then the speaker's view, as two parts (a lead and a stance) so the bank combines them; no line says "he" or "she" for the President unless it reads the record; no stock phrase stands in for the measure.
60. An answer answers the question; with no local link on record the speaker says so or the question is not offered.
61. One person in a room raises the address in an evening.

## Rules 62 to 73: door scenes

62. A scene has a middle: a door with a cause on record gets an exchange about the resident's own matter, with at least two replies that end differently.
63. One button is not a choice. No scene offers a single reply.
64. A name in narration comes from the household record, or nobody is named.
65. He, she or they comes from the person's record.
66. Narration states nothing the record does not hold: no pets, no other people, no objects that claim a fact. The door, the light, the hour, the dwelling kind and what the cause implies are free.
67. No narration and no reply plays twice in one outing; which wording a resident uses comes from that resident.
68. Nobody claims a past that is not on record ("I've been waiting for someone to ask").
69. The office and the day are said as people say them ("running for council", "Election Day").
70. Door endings have their own labels ("Promised you a vote", "Would not sign").
71. Progress is not said aloud ("That's one closer" is a count).
72. What a resident says matters to them is their own strongest question from their views record, with their side and a reason from their life; the player then holds it as a learned view. Stock answers for everyone are a fault.
73. After the opening line the town and the office are each said at most once more in a scene. A line meets the insert guard with a record that belongs in it, or is three words or fewer; never pad with the place name.

## Rules 74 to 81: conversation, second pass

74. A player's reply never says a slot's own name ("How long have you been with work?"); it says "there".
75. A stance must parse with its lead; mark which stances follow which leads in the pieces.
76. A follow-up fits the side: "plan" and "pass it" only when the President is for the measure.
77. One subject once per talk: nobody raises the same question under two headings.
78. A reply answers the line before it, and an opener says the stand, not its history ("always").
79. A figure is said once per talk; after that it is "that" or "the rent".
80. A goodbye is one goodbye: a core that is already a goodbye takes no closer.
81. A modest wording ("just a") fits the job; not for someone who heads a department or a body.

## Rules 82 to 90: the Journal

82. A past paragraph is one stretch of a life (growing up; first work and a partner; the children; the working years; the last few years), headed by its span of years.
83. Dates are said, never printed raw: a past paragraph by its years, a played entry by its day as the game says days.
84. A partner is named as what they are the first time ("my wife, Robin"), from the relationship record.
85. Children are told together in one sentence; a child gets its own sentence only when something else on record happened with it.
86. The town is named at most once per paragraph, and not at all where the chapter heading names it.
87. Nothing trivial (a spell of work under a season folds into the working years) and nothing about someone else alone.
88. Family is said the way people say it ("I had an older brother, Diego"), and a fact the record cannot explain is left out.
89. An event is told with its end: a filing says what it meant, election day is an entry, a race tells its result with the count once.
90. One entry per law that reached the person, by the law's said name, with the one largest change and where they learned of it; no entry for a change they could not notice.

## Rules 91 to 100: the reporter's call, the door, a seat said aloud, and what a reply must do

91. A bill is named once in a scene, by its said name; after that it is "the bill" or "it".
92. A reporter speaks for their own outlet and asks only about what a record put in front of them.
93. "Why?" gets a reason on record. A vote or a no with no reason on record is a fault in the record (a pool item), never something to word around.
94. Each follow-up question is about a new thing from a record, not the first question again.
95. Nobody is offered a lie about a roll that was watched.
96. A room holds only the people the records put there.
97. A stall is said once; a second one ends the call.
98. A count, where one is said at all outside a scene, is said once.
99. A door asks about what the office being run for can touch, and a candidate does not promise to act on a question above the office. The same holds at a desk and for a donor.
100.  A seat is said aloud with the area it covers ("Congress, the 3rd District", "the state Senate in the 17th District"); a statewide seat takes no place at the counter, and a town's own office takes nothing after it at its own counter.

Three rulings that go with these. The rules that a scene names its stake and that a promise names who, what and why judge a scene's path up to its ask, never one line or one pair of lines. An offered reply that nothing later reads is not offered: keep the offer as a fact a later talk brings up, or cut the reply. And before lines are written for a scene kind, it is played with the player in the right seat in three drawn places: a kind that never stages, or whose packet holds no facts, needs a producer or a fact first (skill `scene-producers`).

## Rules 101 to 107: how people talk (owner, Nov 14 2026)

His words: "they shouldn't just say the unemployment rate is 4.6, it should say like, times are tough... Like in normal life, people don't say, oh, the unemployment rate is 4.6%"; "When I give examples, don't just limit it to those examples. I'm giving generalities. Your job is to see the bigger picture and build that as a system"; "Be sure that it's talking in American English... There better not be any British English in this word session."

101. In talk nobody says a rate, a percentage, an index or a statistic about the world. They say how it sits for them, and the record and the speaker's own situation choose the wording on a sliding scale. A figure may be said once where people say figures: a prepared speech, a paper story, a hearing, a reporter's question, and an official about their own act. A sum the speaker pays or is paid is their own fact and may be said once.
102. An example from the owner is a generality: build the system that covers every case like it, and say what you took the general rule to be.
103. A view is said as what it does to the speaker, from their own record, never as a bill's name plus "I'm for it". Where their record holds nothing the question touches, they do not volunteer a view.
104. Fact first, figures when asked. News is told flat and stops; tenure, pay and the employer's whole name come out only when the listener asks, and none is said twice.
105. A name marks attention. A speaker uses the listener's name in at most one line of a talk, where it does work, and never to pass the insert guard. No line explains a feeling ("Thanks for asking.", not "Just knowing you asked helps").
106. A price is never named in the same breath as the vote. A member says the history or what they need and lets it hang. A refusal is a pending fact, a prior word, a smaller offer or a need for cover; yes is said once.
107. Plain American talk an ordinary person understands on first hearing: no British spelling or phrasing, and no insider words of government, finance or the office in a person's mouth. Say what the thing does ("I cut your property taxes."). An insider word may pass between insiders in a chamber or hearing, once.

How soon a speaker's own hard news is told slides with the tie: close, it leads; an acquaintance gives a guarded line and the fact only if asked; a stranger does not volunteer it. A scene is a tree: at least two turns past its ask on two branches that end differently and write back. Guides to how real people talk in twelve settings were written from transcripts on Nov 14 2026 and are kept off main; ask the Words lead for the one for your setting before writing lines.

## Rules 108 to 135: scenes as played (owner, Nov 14 and 15 2026)

His words are kept whole, with his message ids, in the Words lead's feedback file; ask the lead for it. He grades by scene and line number on the Scenes page, and a spoken "good" or "not good" becomes a rule here the same night, because only the 1-to-5 grades spread through the grade run.

108. A question that is offered is answered from a record, or it is not offered. This binds the player's own replies as well as other people's.
109. A pronoun for a person comes from that person's record, never a guess.
110. A scene opens on what just happened, in plain words, from its cause record.
111. A "why" gets a reason or a plain decline, never a restatement.
112. A sharp or refusing reply is earned: by the tie, by a story against the player, or by being pressed twice.
113. No reply contradicts the record unless it is a marked lie.
114. The player always knows which lines are their choices.
115. No "it", "this" or "of it" that points at nothing said.
116. A scene says the specific thing at stake from the record, or it does not play.
117. An oath or ceremony follows its real form.
118. A reply does one clear thing.
119. Replies belong to the person and the moment. Offer many, different in what they do, not in wording.
120. Scenes do not all have one shape (narrator, line, line).
121. A line someone says to themselves is a thing people say ("I need to gather myself.").
122. A bill's sponsor says what the bill does unasked, in a few sentences.
123. Narration makes the setting and the kind of talk plain, and the lines fit it: a floor is not two people alone.
124. An opener fits how the two came to be talking and whether the player knows the person.
125. No reply needs context the player was not given.
126. What happens around the talk follows what was just said.
127. A scene is as long as its occasion: a keynote is 10 to 20 clicks of short sentences, a hallway word is two.
128. Another person's long turn is one block of three or four short sentences, sometimes one sentence alone for effect.
129. The player's own speech is picked one part of four at each click (ruled Oct 14 and 15; `docs/coordination/OWNER-DECISIONS.md`).
130. The player does not cut in on a turn. Another person may cut in on the player, as an event in the scene.
131. A scene has an outcome, or it is a step toward one inside an event under way. Little everyday talk; home goes deep when the subject is heavy.
132. Before a design question or an inference goes to the owner, the whole repository is searched for his ruling, and the question carries the reading line.
133. Rule 129 holds for every speech. The number of clicks follows the occasion and varies; short is allowed and length is never padding.
134. Speeches do not all follow one format. The order and kind of parts differ by occasion, by the speaker's record and by what was picked before.
135. One back-end place accounts for every scene and line, so any line can be found later. A line it cannot list is a fault.

Rulings that go with these. A said name of a subject ({issue}, {said}) goes only after "on" or "about". A put-off that promises a later talk is offered only when the record keeps the ask open and someone raises it again. Every spot where the player answers offers two or three answers that differ; one only for a courtesy. In a first meeting the name is said once, at the introduction, and a stranger at a door never uses the candidate's first name. What the player just said sets a fact, and the next line must answer it. A person remembers what passed with the player: a second ask of the same person opens on the first answer, a third on both, and after a second no the player gets a plain close only. A plea is offered only when the record holds what it leans on. A bribe says what is wanted first and the money second. A news story shows the day it ran. A line taken out of a scene is deleted from the bank, and a played file, sheet or verdict for a head that no longer exists is deleted, never renamed "old" (owner, Nov 15: "don't leave old shit in the files. Delete it.").

## Rules 136 to 157: context, conditions and the opening talk (owner, Nov 15 and 16 2026)

His words are whole on the Story thread, issue #9750 (the CTO's first comment) and in docs/words-lead-handoff-2026-11-16/played-scenes-feedback.md.

136. The narrator says who a person is the first time the player meets them at a story point: full name and what they are to the player or the place, from the record. A first name alone only where the record says the player knows them. One rule in the director, at every level.
137. A line never presumes something the record does not hold. A lie is a reply the player picks, marked as one, and possible at that moment; a line about an earlier act plays only when the record shows it.
138. Every office, department or body a line names is one the place's records hold, by its own name there. Where the place has none, the line says the act, not an office.
139. Replies under one choice are different things to say, each standing on its own; a no says what is refused and why.
140. Plain phrasing people use ("I've been out of work a while, and my savings are almost gone.").
141. The time words at the top of a scene come from the hour the event happens on the calendar; an event with no hour of its own is given one from the record.
142. The Scenes page shows what a player would read: the seat's own title, sample names that are one person, conditions in plain English.
143. People already in the player's life carry context and news: a friend calls after a big event, and the talk is how the player learns about the world. Any major public event can cause such a talk with someone the record ties to the player.
144. Laws and ordinances that can pass are material for scenes at every level.
145. Scenes are conditional by who the player is: sets for a state legislator only, for a legislator or an executive at any level, for a person not in office. The scene record says who it can play for.
146. Sessions may ask the owner questions, after the repository and his rulings are searched (rule 132); a real question left goes in one line with what was found.
147. No chit-chat scene. The coffee is never the point: the friend is deciding whether to run, a protest is on the way, someone from a party committee is met.
148. Directing is narrowing: the director reads what is simulated and what the player is doing and can do, cuts the whole bank of scenes by those conditions, then picks a few.
149. A no is a record: declining takes that scene out, and because the player said no, something else may come later. The game nudges and never says so.
150. Many scenes with real variance: a scene is eligible through its wordings, each wording with its own conditions (requiresFacts, excludesFacts); a new wording is a different condition or thing to say, never a copy.
151. "Tagging" means these conditions, on scenes and on lines.
152. A recorded yes-or-no fact may gate a scene or a line; an amount weights the pick and never gates at a number.
153. Whatever a thread traces by script or by reading code, dev mode in the game must show; a PR that comes out of a trace adds the missing dev-mode view.
154. One path: every talk, the opening one included, is built by the same engine under the same rules; a fix is proved on the opening talk of a new life as well as on the scene it was made for.
155. A reply that says nothing ("Oh, my.") is not a reply.
156. Someone in the player's house does not open with "Did you hear about" a story that has nothing to do with them; a story is raised only with a recorded tie to the speaker or the player.
157. The Elections page shows what the player can run for, in the office's own name, one entry per office, not one per seat.

Rulings that go with these (owner, Nov 16, on the Story thread): the Nov 14 excuse for a fixed line "the world places" is withdrawn ("No. Count them again and rewrite them with inserts"; "either that or remove them and start fresh"); every line of dialogue or narration is registered in one place so there are no leftovers; dev mode traces and forces every scene and shows why each played and why each reply was offered; the director is a board of strings, each scene a card pinned to the records it needs (the CTO's reading of his Nov 16 picture).

## Traps

- A test that counts questions or wordings going red after a rule change is usually telling the truth. Find which change emptied the supply before touching the test.
- A script that prints every wording of a story makes a paper look repetitive; say so in the lines file.
- `data/english/parts/` edits can be refused by the permission check for a builder. Do not work around it: list the lines in bank shape for the lead.
- The bank's limits shape every line the lead writes: a fixed line of four or more words needs a record slot (the line-inserts baseline only goes down) and a part is ten words at most. Write to both from the start, and never fill the slot with the town's name just to pass.
- When a count test wants many different lines, do not write near-twins: split the line into two parts the bank combines (a lead and a stance), and delete the whole-line pieces they replace.
- Read a builder's fault before ruling on it. A card that looked wrong ("Went to school together" in July) was right: the player had graduated in June. Ask whose card it is.
- A picture of one screen can hide the next one: a drafting card passed while the bill it produced showed one step of three. Ask for the screen the player lands on after pressing the button.
- Before a stamp, check every line the head changes against the played file headed with that exact sha. Take the set of every "text" under `data/english` at the head and subtract the set at the Train head: the three-dot diff misleads when the branch sits on an older Train merge, and the set also catches an old line that was reworded, which is a changed line like any other (the Journal head showed 53 added lines and 23 reworded ones shown nowhere). A line that never played is shown or deleted; a stamp given on a file from another head is withdrawn.
- Played means said in a talk or printed in a story on a played run. A line the file lists as "drawn on other days", composed from the rows, or counted in a table at the top is not played: two promise lines were stamped on a composed sentence and the stamp was withdrawn the same hour. Count a line only in the played part of the file, and a variant that never staged in any played place does not ship; its text stays in the lead's lines file.
- A stamp reads the slots as well as the words: {name} in a colleague's opening was the player's own name.
- A press placed on "leans yes" after a "not yet" can never play, because a "not yet" comes only from someone leaning against. Ask the builder which states reach a line before writing it.
- The ten-word limit is `maxWords` in `data/english/parts/conversation.json`, a builder's setting, not an owner rule. Rule 128's long turn is several parts composed as one turn (an arc's `stateMoves` in `src/director/scene-talk.ts`), never one long part.
- One fault class gets one mechanism and one owner thread. Before assigning a class, ask which thread already holds the mechanism, and say so to both.
- A deleted line that exists nowhere else is a loss to rule on, not a cleanup: ask where each deleted line still plays.
- A line that plays is not yet a line that is placed right. At a stamp, read what places each new line (requiresFacts, excludesFacts, onlyWhen) against the lines file: two pleas written "only when the record holds a favor" were placed on asked-before alone and played for a player who had never backed the person.
- A said name that reads wrong in one line is usually the line's fault, not the name's. "Hold the line on discrimination" read as the other side; changing the short name would have broken 13 other lines. Ask for every line that reads the name before changing a form, and fix the line that put a side on a subject.
- Before sending lines to a builder, run them against the line rules (`npm run words:rules`), or at least the owner's spellings: in dialogue it is "Alright", never "All right" (owner, Nov 9; rule alright-in-dialogue). Two polling-place lines were pulled at the builder's gate for it. Also read every value a slot can hold inside the frame: "less than a month" broke three {remaining} frames that read fine with "three months"; when a span cannot read right in a frame, take the span out of the line.
- The stamp check turns a slot into "anything", so a line with a slot can be matched by a different line that has more words in that place: "Did you see what {outlet} had on {about}?" passed on the strength of "Did you see what the {outlet} had on {about}?" and went to the Train unplayed until the second reader caught it (#9455). Where one added line's pattern also fits another added line, find each in the played file by eye.
- When a scene offers the player a "why" (or "what happened", "when"), the line before it must not already have said it, and the answer must tell more than the line that prompted the question. Twice in one night the news line carried the reason and the "why" answer repeated it (clerk's petition, polling place): the news says the fact ("Some names came off.", "You're not in the book.") and the reason is the answer. The same check runs the other way: an ask that names its day leaves no "When do you need me?" to offer (#9436), and "Tell me what happened." is offered only where the record holds more than the subject (#9455).
- An issue slot holds the subject's short name, so a verb that acts on its object turns the name into the thing acted on: "{opponent}'s campaign is pushing {issue} hard" played as "is pushing the police hard" and, earlier, "is pushing evictions hard" for a rival who wants lawyers for tenants. At a stamp, read each slot line with every name the played file gives it; if the verb could mean for, against or at the thing, write it with "about" ("is talking about {issue} everywhere"). The Words lead passed this line twice and withdrew a stamp for it (#9416, Nov 15).
- A promise, a price or a pledge must say the proposal, not the subject. "You have my word. I'm with you on {issue}." let the player promise without being told which way, and "I'm with you on discrimination" read as the other side; the later vote keeps or breaks that word, so the player has to know it. Where a line commits anyone to a side, use the question's worded proposal (the `about` form in data/english/question-names.json) after "for", "against" or "back", and split the line by side if it must. At a stamp, ask of every commitment: could the player say what they just agreed to? (#9477, second reader, Nov 15; rule 116.)
- A line may say only who and what the record puts in the scene. "Your department heads are on their feet" played with one head in the room; "Good. I'll be here when you bring the sheets in." was said by a clerk standing on the street; "Someone wants a word" followed a lead that had just named the person. At a stamp read the cast count, the place of the staging and the sentence before each line.
- A scene that comes back must differ by a cause on record, and it comes back few times: the governor's briefing returned weekly with the same words until it was cut to a midway return and a last-day return, each with its own lines. When nothing on record has changed, the scene does not stage and nothing is told to the player; no line may say what happened "since" unless a record keeps it.
- The paper and a narration report the event's own record. A law picked from the year's news rows was written into an address story as what the mayor "pointed to", and an address with no body on record was written as "asks the council". Ask what the event row holds before writing, and write to the least it holds ("spoke about", not "asked for").
- An answer to "why" gives a reason from the record or it is a dodge and is cast as one, so the asker presses (rule 111). "I voted no. I'll stand on that." was written as a plain answer by the lead and came back. Ask what the record keeps of the reason (the roll keeps each member's position part by part) before writing reasons.
- An ending reads how the talk ended, not only the player's last choice: a donor whose last word was "Then I'll find a candidate who listens." was recorded as heard and the tie warmed.
- A teller may say only what they can know and what is true by the record: a clerk called a petition thin at 11 names when the office needed 10, because the trigger compared the campaign's own goal. Ask what a trigger compares.
- Wording must be true on every day the line can play. "I ran with you" could be said before the election; "I joined your ticket" is true from the day the person is seated. The same goes for "last week", "tomorrow" and "again".
- Replies with no stage follow every stage of the line before them: after a lost race the player was still offered "Somebody has to run for {office}." When a say line carries stages, its answers carry them too, and every stance and stage pair left without a reply is listed for the lead, never covered by widening a line.
- The bank's word count removes a slot but keeps the period after it, so "{about}." counts as one word. Count that way before sending a line near the ten-word limit.
- A label names what the number counts: a row of residents who work was labeled "Jobs". Ask what the figure counts before wording it.
