# The scene list

For the owner to read and riff on (owner, Nov 16: "just a list of scenes or something and potential scenes and stuff, I can riff off of that as well"). Kept by the Story lead on the Story thread (#9750) and refreshed every few hours. Part (a) is every scene kind the game has today, in plain words. Part (b) is what is planned or being weighed, by where you are in life, with the scenes each one unlocks. A riff on any line goes back to the thread verbatim and changes the engine, not only the line.

Updated Nov 17, 2026, after the cut.

## (a) Every scene kind that exists today

One line each: who comes to you or whom you meet, where, what is at stake, and who can see it. The key in brackets is for the crew to trace it.

### Home and family

- Someone at home or on the phone brings news of an eviction, a death, a job loss, an illness, or a family split or wedding. Anyone. [news-arrives]
- Someone you owe an apology asks for one, at home, at their home, or in public. Anyone. [apology]

### Work and money

- Your first day at a new school or job: a senior coworker or classmate sets you straight. Anyone. [first-day]
- Your last day at a school or job: classmates or coworkers say goodbye and ask what comes next. Anyone. [last-day]
- An employer calls with a job offer. At stake: money and work. Anyone looking. [job-offer]

### Meeting people and the town

- Someone who knows you accuses you of a crime at school, at work or in public, while people watch. Anyone. [accusation]
- A neighbor who shared a cause with you stops you with a clipboard and asks you to come to the town body's next posted meeting about it; a yes is a promise the meeting's record keeps or breaks, and you can only say yes to a cause you agree with. Anyone. Rewritten Nov 17 (#9947), ready. [cause-invite]
- A seated officeholder of your party who took you under their wing weighs in, at their home, on whether you should run. Anyone weighing a run. [advice]

### Running for office: doors, filing, the campaign, election night

- The clerk at the counter checks your papers when you file to run. At stake: the office and your petition. Candidates. [filing]
- Your family reacts when you file, only when the race itself costs the house something read from its books: your own money in the campaign, a job you left to run, or a young child at home while you work. The talk names the office, the race's length, the money put in, the job or the child; nobody says how long the savings last. A house the race costs nothing hears the filing as news at the evening talk. At home. Candidates. Rebuilt Nov 17 (#9905), ready. [family-filing]
- A voter opens the door to your canvass, the first time or again; their view of your platform decides how it goes, and what they say is placed by their record (a lost job, a rent notice, a sick relative, your rival's name, your stand on their question). At stake: their vote. Candidates. Rewritten Nov 17 (#9846), ready. [door-ask]
- A voter at the door is asked to sign your petition while it circulates; the petition record's answer decides, and the talk reads it. Candidates. Rewritten Nov 17 (#9846), ready. [door-signature]
- A reporter presses you for an on-the-record answer about your race. Candidates. [interview-sitting]
- You stand on stage at a debate with your opponent, before the press and the public. Candidates. [debate-sitting]
- Another candidate asks you to endorse them, and says why now from their own records: how long they worked for you, the question they carry, a job lost, an earlier run, the same party, or that they run against someone you backed. Anyone the candidate is close to. Rebuilt Nov 17 (#9909), ready. [endorsement-ask]
- A candidate whose speech moved you in the room comes over afterward to ask for your support. Anyone who heard a speech in person. [speech-afterward]
- Election day: you go to your precinct's polling place and cast your ballot while poll workers and neighbors watch. Anyone who votes. [polling-place]
- Election night, you win: supporters gather, you speak and make a promise on stage. Candidates. [race-won]

### Holding a local office

- A reporter catches you in the hallway after a vote, a bill, a veto or an act, and asks for comment; the questions name the bill and your vote, a lie is caught by the roll call, and the closes follow what you actually said. Officeholders. Rewritten Nov 17 (#9794), ready. [press-interview]
- A resident stops you in the street about a vote you cast or a law that just reached them. Fires far too often today (owner, Nov 17); each stop needs its cause on record and a cooldown, and the board picks a few. Officeholders and candidates. Graded Nov 17. [constituent-stop]
- Someone offers you a bribe at your office or a venue for a favor; you can take it, refuse it, or report it; the lines name the bill or the contract, the gift's weight in words and your own stand. Officeholders. Rewrite in build Nov 17. [bribe-offer]
- After you refuse, the same person presses harder. Officeholders. [bribe-pressed]
- A donor calls about a bill: their gift's weight is said in words against the other donors (the biggest check, a small one), your yes or no gives your own side on the question, the bill's stage places a stall, and a lie about a bill that never moved is caught by the sponsor or the chair. A gift that names no bill brings no call. Officeholders and candidates. Rewritten Nov 17 (#9931), ready. [donor-ask]

### Holding a seat in a legislature (town council, state house or senate, Congress)

- A member who sat on the committee that heard a bill asks you to vote their way in the hallway. Members. [vote-asked]
- The evening before the roll, a colleague asks for your vote and says whether you are the one name they still need or one on a list; your yes or no gives your own stand on the question, a thank comes only after a yes, and a lie is believed or caught. Members. Rewritten Nov 17 (#9935), ready. [vote-sought]
- A colleague asks you to take a seat, a committee or a role in the body. Members. [serve-sought]
- The sponsor of a bill whose veto override failed asks you for another try. Members. [vote-confirmed]
- A member who introduced a bill asks you to carry it with them. Members. [bill-carry]

### Executives: mayor, county executive, governor, president

- You give a public address (the yearly address, a State of the State, the State of the Union), and people answer it. Executives. [address]

## (b) Potential scenes, planned or being weighed

Each line says who, where, what is at stake, and what it unlocks. "Unlocks" means a later scene reads what this one wrote. Lines marked (building) have a builder on them now.

### Not in office: the first week

- Your first evening: the President's address, and someone at home says what they think of it, as short as "Can you believe that idiot?" or as long as the subject deserves. Unlocks: that person brings it up again when Congress acts on it. (building)
- A friend or relative who follows politics calls a few days after the address and says come for coffee; the talk is your way into what is going on in the town and the country. Unlocks: their run, a rally on the way, or a party committee (below).
- Your parent or an older relative who holds or held an office says what it was like, and whether you should think about it. Unlocks: the appointment and the party committee.
- The friend at coffee is deciding whether to run for something; your word goes into their decision. Unlocks: they ask you to help their campaign; they ask for your endorsement; if you talked them down and they run anyway, they say so.
- On the way, a protest or a rally is in the street downtown; someone in it asks you where you stand. Unlocks: the cause group's invitation.
- Someone from a party's local committee is at the coffee shop or the rally and asks what you think of the party. Unlocks: the party committee meeting; being asked to run when a seat goes unfilled.

### Not in office: a year with no race open (owner: work for someone else's campaign, a party committee or an appointed board until a race opens)

- A candidate in town, or their manager, asks you to help their campaign: doors, phones, a Saturday. Unlocks: knocking doors for someone else (the door scenes with their name on your card), their race night, their endorsement ask when you run. (building, with the Blockers lane)
- Your first door for someone else's campaign: the resident asks why you are working for that candidate. Unlocks: that resident remembers you when you run. (Owner, Nov 17: "This is good.")
- The party's local committee meets: a slate, an endorsement, a chair's election; you are asked to take a post (precinct captain, secretary, treasurer). Unlocks: the committee asks you to run; the chair's favor later. (planned, with Blockers)
- Someone with appointing power offers you a seat on a town board (planning, library, housing). Unlocks: the board's own meetings and votes; the first time someone asks you for a favor because of the seat. (planned, with Blockers)
- A council seat is going unopposed and someone who knows you says you should file. Unlocks: filing, the family's reaction.
- The party comes to your shop or your workplace to recruit you while your business or job is failing. Unlocks: party-welcome; the question of whether you can afford to run.

### Running for office

- The door that closes on you: the resident cuts you off or shuts the door, for a reason on their record (a grievance, a bad hour, your platform). Unlocks: the same resident at a town hall; a second knock weeks later that goes differently.
- The resident who will not let you go: one long turn after another, and your afternoon of doors gets shorter. Unlocks: nothing but time lost, which the campaign feels.
- The door that tells you something you did not know: a lost job, an eviction notice, a closed clinic, a road never finished; the resident asks what you will do, and your answer is a promise on the record. Unlocks: the same resident at office hours after you win; the recall table if the promise breaks.
- Your papers have a problem: the clerk checks every name on your petition against the voter roll on the due day and strikes the ones that are not registered voters of the race's own electorate (moved away, never registered, signed twice, dead); if fewer stand than the law requires, the filing is marked short and you are off the ballot unless you have days left to go back out. The check is a public record the paper prints. Unlocks: going back out, or a challenge. (Answered for the owner, Nov 17.)
- Someone challenges your petition, or you challenge theirs, and the board rules. Unlocks: a rival off the ballot and their grudge; a press story.
- The incumbent who said they would step aside files at the last hour, against their own word to you. Unlocks: the lie remembered in the race.
- Other candidates filing the same day at the clerk's counter; a party official watching who files; a reporter checking the list at closing. Unlocks: the first rival word.
- A long-time volunteer or staffer asks for your endorsement to run on their own (the owner's example). Unlocks: their race night; an ally on the body; or, refused, they leave your campaign and may run against someone you back. (Built Nov 17, #9909: the ask says why now from their records; a housemate's ask at home follows once #9809 lands.)
- A friend signs you up for a group that would help the campaign; at the first meeting the organizer names the price. Unlocks: the favor called in.
- The heckler who says you are not one of us, and the friend in the room who answers them. Unlocks: the friend's standing with you; the heckler at the next door.
- Answer for a vote you once cast: an opponent says you once voted for the other side; you own it, explain it, or deny it (a denial the record contradicts is a lie). Unlocks: the press if the lie is caught. Owner, Nov 17: "pretty thin. i have never seen consequences"; it must fire from the roll and change something he can see before it is listed as built.
- A death opens a seat; within days you decide whether to run in the special election, and someone with money asks to be the one who funds it. Unlocks: the donor's favor.
- The count is close: a recount, a missing box, the board that rules. Unlocks: the recount night; the concession call that waits.
- The convention keynote: as many clicks as the occasion and the speaker call for (rules 127 and 133; no fixed count, owner, Nov 17), short sentences, no two speeches in one format. Unlocks: the press the next morning; who heard it in the room.
- The precinct that votes for you and the county that does not: the morning after, a neighbor says "We all voted for you here." Unlocks: the next run's decision.

### Holding a local office

- Called in: the mayor, the chair or the party leader says "Sit down." What happens is why they called: a vote they need, a committee seat they offer, a complaint about your speech. Unlocks: the favor owed either way.
- The sole no: you are the only no (or the only yes) on a bill; colleagues and the press ask why, and it follows you. Unlocks: the next vote they ask for; the rival's line.
- The sponsor's case: whoever speaks for a bill says what it does without being asked, in a few sentences, on the floor. Unlocks: the question you ask next.
- Listening to the other side and changing the bill: the sponsor meets the people who oppose it (the chief of police, an employers' group, a union) and trades a section for their support. Unlocks: the amended bill's vote; the group remembers.
- The favor called in: someone you owe asks for it back: a vote, an endorsement, a job for a relative. Unlocks: the grievance if refused.
- The jobs: after you win, your allies want posts; the other faction gets none and turns against you next time. Unlocks: the appointment scene from the other chair; the rival's word.
- Wakes, weddings and favors: the officeholder who goes to every wake; the family remembers. Unlocks: their vote; their son's ask.
- Losing and then repairing: after a lost primary you go to the people who backed the winner and say no hard feelings; over months they start coming to you. Unlocks: the next race.
- The old comrade's letter: years later, someone who helped you once writes when you reach a higher office. Unlocks: the favor they ask.

### Holding a state or federal seat

- The leader's treatment: the leader leans in and works you for a vote: flattery, a favor, a threat to your bill.
- The bad committee seat: you are put on a committee that has nothing to do with your district and ask to be moved.
- Using the money to win something else: a member with a say over funding trades it for a project the executive wanted to deny your town.
- Your absences used against you: the rival reads out the votes you missed.
- The appointee who wants to run: your appointee announces for another office while holding the post; the law may force them out.
- The resigned colleague who wants their seat back: months later they ask to be reappointed, and the answer is no.

### Executives: the situation room and the desk

- The briefing when something goes wrong: a disaster or crisis opens; the people whose work it is brief you and you choose what to do first; for the mayor and the governor as it already is for the President. Unlocks: the press the next day; the budget line that pays for it.
- The other government's decision lands on you: the state or the federal government does something whose cost falls on your town (a facility, a cut, a mandate). Unlocks: your budget's amendment; the letter to the governor.
- The court makes you act: a ruling forces a special session, and you propose a package with a tax to pay for it.
- The department head who disagrees in the room, before the others (being built as a hard meeting).

### Industries, laws and the effect web (every level)

- A factory, a clinic or a store in town is losing money under a law or a tax, and its owner comes to you, the one who can change it, with what the books say. Unlocks: the bill you file; the closing if nothing changes; the layoffs people bring up at the door.
- A law you passed reaches someone: a paycheck, a price, a service; they bring it up in the street, at the door, at home. Unlocks: the next vote they ask for; the recall if it hurt. Owner, Nov 17: never seen; the law's reach record (law.effect-learned) must exist in a played year and the stop must fire from it before this counts.
- The case for a law nobody has written: someone the law would change stops you and asks for a law your own level can make, as a council member, a state legislator or a private person. Unlocks: the bill introduced, the order, or the case joined. (planned)
- A law from another level lands on your town: the state preempts your ordinance, or Congress sends money with strings. Unlocks: the letter, the lawsuit, the rally.

### Home, while in politics

- Your partner, after your filing: what it costs the house, in time and money, and whether you asked. Fires every filing today (owner, Nov 17: "this happens like every time"); it plays only when the books or the calendar give the partner something to say. Unlocks: the manager offer; the family's night on election night.
- The child who repeats what a classmate's parent said about you. Unlocks: the door at that parent's house.
- The relative who asks you for a job or a contract now that you hold the seat. Unlocks: the press if you say yes; the grievance if you say no.
- The divorce that comes in two scenes: a feeler, then the talk. (Owner, Nov 14: "if there's like a feeler scene, you know, or an exposition scene... that unlocks more scenes.")
