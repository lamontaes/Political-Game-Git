# How real local news stories and a written week move

Research for the Story lead, Nov 24, 2026, by Fixer A. It is for step 4 of the first-evening plan on #9750: "the Journal page and the first news through the same planner". It answers two questions the plan raises:

- How does a real local news story move from its lede through its body?
- How does a real person write up a week (or a day) so that several events become a paragraph?

It sets shape and rhythm only. No sentence below goes into a bank. Each quote is at most five words, taken from a story's opening words so a reader can find the place in the source.

It was written against the owner's Nov 23 rows:

- **P45:** "look at these news stories. These aren't stories."
- **P43:** "The journal is still a list of facts."
- **P51:** "I simulated a week. Nothing happened. No new news stories either."

**Each paragraph was coded for its role.** The roles are:

- lede
- stakes (what changes for residents)
- figure (a number is the point)
- quote
- paraphrase of a named person
- tally
- process
- background or history
- reaction (from someone affected or opposed)
- next step
- side item (another item from the same meeting)
- scene
- kicker

Each paragraph was also coded for whether it holds a number, a direct quote and a named person.

**Diaries.** Nine entries from two presidents' own diaries are coded in section C below: seven by Reagan and two by Truman. They are the closest real thing to a first-person Journal.

**Method limits:**

- One reader coded each text, so where a paragraph's role starts and ends is that reader's judgment.
- The sample is small and leans to budget stories, because September is budget season. Read the counts as shape, not as rates.

## A. How a local news story moves

**1. The lede is one or two sentences, and most open on the body that acted.**

| The lede opens on                                       | Stories                                                             |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| the body or business that acted                         | 7 (Douglas, Jacksboro, Freeburg, Flathead, Chanute, Wauseon, Ron's) |
| the people affected ("Ward County taxpayers could see") | 1 (Minot)                                                           |
| an absence ("Nobody came to speak Monday")              | 1 (Marion)                                                          |
| a scene in the place                                    | 1 (KTOO)                                                            |
| the place and time, then a judgment                     | 1 (Times-Republican)                                                |

- **Length:** ledes run 17 to 54 words. The middle value is 31.
- **Facts per lede:** a lede packs three to six facts.
- **Stakes:** the lede says what changes for residents in 5 of 11 stories. In 2 more it gives only the government's side (the savings to the city, the tax rate). In 4 it gives no stakes. Where the lede has none, the stakes arrive in paragraph 2 (Marion, KTOO), or never (Wauseon).

**2. The body runs in a few common orders, by kind.**

- **A vote or meeting story:**
  - It opens with the actor and the action.
  - Then come a figure or the process, and the named officials' views.
  - The tally sits in the middle (paragraph 13 of 22 in Chanute and in the Times-Republican story), not at the top.
  - It ends with the next step, then "In other business" side items.
- **A budget story:**
  - The lede is followed by stakes.
  - Then comes a household example ("For the owner of a...").
  - Then a run of figure paragraphs: five in a row in Marion, and five of six paragraphs in Minot.
  - Then officials' quotes, then side items.
  - 4 of 5 budget stories turn the levy into one household's bill.
- **A business closing:**
  - It runs 16 to 21 paragraphs.
  - About half of it is history in the owner's voice (KTOO paragraphs 3 to 14, Ron's 8 to 12), alternating paraphrase and quote.
  - Then come the owner's feelings ("very, very mixed feelings").
  - Then the customers' reaction, relayed through the owner (KTOO paragraphs 19 and 20).
  - It ends on the owner's next step or a kicker quote about their own plans.
- **A contested school board meeting (Times-Republican):**
  - The conflict comes first: public comment, then the superintendent's replies.
  - The vote and the pay figure come in the middle.
  - A parent's reaction after the meeting comes near the end.
  - It closes on the next meeting date.
- **A meeting told in agenda order (Wauseon):**
  - It has 19 paragraphs and no quote.
  - The two ordinances in the headline arrive at paragraphs 16 and 17.
  - This is the "list of facts" shape the owner rejects, and real papers print it too.

**3. A named person is paraphrased, then quoted in the next paragraph.**

- Pairs of paraphrase then quote appear in 7 stories: Chanute 7→8, Jacksboro 9→10 and 11→12, Freeburg 7→8, Ron's 4→5, KTOO 5→6, Douglas 4→5 and 12→13.
- **Where the first full quote falls:** 9 of 11 stories quote someone, first at paragraph 3 to 10, with a middle value of 4. The two that quote nobody (Wauseon and Minot) are the two most list-like.
- **Who is quoted first:**
  - an official in council and budget stories (a commissioner, an administrator, counsel);
  - the owner in closings;
  - a parent or a board member in school stories.

**4. Ordinary residents seldom speak.**

- One story of 11 quotes an ordinary resident directly: the Times-Republican's parent ("Yeah, thank you for ignoring").
- The closings relay customers through the owner.
- Douglas quotes a nonprofit director. Marion's lede makes the residents' silence the news.
- Where residents' feelings appear, they come late in the story and secondhand.

**5. Side items: a meeting story carries the meeting's other acts.**

- 5 of 7 meeting stories (council, budget, school) add the same meeting's other business, introduced as "In other business" or "In other matters", or as dash bullets.
- 3 stories end on such an item (Douglas, Marion, Chanute).
- A paper groups by meeting, not by subject.

**6. Endings vary, and none sums up a moral.**

| Last paragraph | Stories                      |
| -------------- | ---------------------------- |
| side item      | 3 (Douglas, Marion, Chanute) |
| next step      | 2 (KTOO, Times-Republican)   |
| kicker quote   | 2 (Ron's, Jacksboro)         |
| figure         | 2 (Flathead, Minot)          |
| paraphrase     | 1 (Freeburg)                 |
| procedure      | 1 (Wauseon)                  |

**7. Size.**

- Stories run 6 to 22 paragraphs and about 235 to 1,000 words.
- A paragraph is one or two sentences.
- Every story in this sample reports something decided or ended that week. None reports a law that stayed the same.

## B. How officials write up their own week

**1. There are three ways to order a week, and each writer keeps one.**

- **By day, Monday first:** District 7's letter. Each event sits under a bold day-and-time line, with two or three sentences under it. A "Looking Ahead" block follows in the same day order.
- **Biggest first, then the rest:**
  - Both District 13 letters lead with the budget in prose, then switch to lists by type ("Here are the rest").
  - Francis's column runs the other way. It opens on a light local hook (visitors to the Capitol) and ends on the weightiest subject, property tax, which takes about 75% of the column.
- **By topic or department:**
  - Commerce City runs about 30 to 40 items under department headers.
  - Sykes's column is three policy themes and holds almost no events. It is an argument, not a week.

**2. A paragraph mostly holds one event.** When it holds several, a shared meeting, place or set of visitors ties them:

- District 7's Monday paragraph holds two or three topics from one meeting.
- Francis's opening paragraph holds three requests from the same visitors.

Joining words are few where headers or days do the work ("I also learned more", "Here are the rest"). Where prose carries the joins, they are plain time and turn words: "This week", "Now,", "That's when", "Instead,".

**3. The writer's feeling is a minority of the sentences.**

| Writer                | Feeling or judgment / all sentences |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| District 7            | about 8 of 38 (1 in 5)              |
| District 13, Nov 2026 | about 4 or 5 of 30 (1 in 6 or 7)    |
| District 13, Nov 2025 | 2 of 5                              |
| Francis               | about 8 of 36 (1 in 4 or 5)         |
| Commerce City         | about 10 of 110 or more (1 in 10)   |
| Sykes (an argument)   | about 22 of 24                      |

The feelings are short and attached to one event ("grateful", "excited to share", "proud to introduce", "We hate to"). None of these writers states a feeling about the week as a whole.

**4. They open and close the same way every week.**

- The alders open with a greeting ("Dear Neighbors", "Hi Everyone") and close with a sign-off ("That's all for now").
- District 7 adds an invitation to get in touch.
- The legislators open on a reflection and close with an invitation or the next vote.

**5. A look ahead takes a large share.**

- The upcoming week takes about 35% of District 7's own prose, about half of the November 2026 District 13 letter, and about 90% of the November 2025 one.
- In the city manager's week it takes 8 to 10%.

**6. Residents appear as requests, not voices.**

- Francis lists what visitors asked for.
- Commerce City gives survey counts and the reports that led to clean-ups.
- No writer quotes a constituent.

## C. A week in an officeholder's own diary

Nine distinct entries were coded: seven by Ronald Reagan (White House Diaries, Reagan Foundation, 1981 to 1986) and two by Harry Truman (Potsdam, July 18 and 25, 1945, as transcribed on ww2db.com).

- The Foundation files one Reagan text, Thursday to Sunday of May 8 to 11, 1986, under two dates. It is counted once.
- One Reagan date (Sept 27 or 28, 1985) is inconsistent on the page.

**1. Most entries are short, and a long one groups by phase, not by event.**

- Six of the seven Reagan entries are one paragraph of 15 to 96 words, holding 1 to 5 events.
- The one long Reagan text covers four days in four paragraphs:
  - the Thursday errands;
  - the weekend's rides and swims, with the weather;
  - one phone call told with its cause and effect;
  - how he is sleeping.
- Truman's entries run 3 and 7 paragraphs, one paragraph per meeting or subject.

**2. The opening names what kind of day it was.**

- 5 of 7 Reagan entries open on the day's character or its weather ("A nice quiet day", "A free day", "A busy desk day", "Spring had come").
- Only the long Reagan text and Truman's July 18 open on the first event.
- None opens on the most important event.

**3. Most events get a bare mention, and a few get a reaction or detail.**

| Entry                      | Bare mentions | With reaction or detail |
| -------------------------- | ------------- | ----------------------- |
| Reagan, May 1986 weekend   | about 3       | about 5                 |
| Reagan, Oct 25, 1981       | about 1       | about 4                 |
| Reagan, four short Sundays | 1 to 3 each   | 0 or 1 each             |
| Truman, July 18            | about 6       | about 6                 |
| Truman, July 25            | about 2       | about 5                 |

The detail is a feeling, a physical fact (the temperature, a swim, how someone's health looked), or what someone else said. What makes an entry read as a day rather than a list is that one or two events carry the writer's reaction, not that every event is told in full.

**4. Feelings make up about a third of the sentences in the longer entries.**

- The longer entries: about 4 of 13, 3 of 8, 10 of 32 and 11 of 26 sentences.
- The very short entries: about half.
- Each judgment is tied to one thing ("He was great", "a terrible place", "for a change"). There is no summing-up of the day beyond the opening's one word ("quiet", "busy", "free").

**5. Home is woven in with work.**

- 6 of 7 Reagan entries mention family, home, health or the weather, at the opening or in the middle of the work: a daughter's visit between a briefing paper and a call from the Secretary of State, jet lag after a trip.
- Truman's July 18 opens with breakfast with his nephew and ends on wanting to go home. Truman's July 25 has none.

**6. Others' words are paraphrased, never quoted.**

- Each of the two writers reports what someone said one to four times per entry ("He says not serious", "She promised").
- Neither quotes anyone directly.

**7. Joins are minimal.** Reagan uses "&", dashes, "then", "but", "still" and "When". Truman uses sequence verbs ("Went to lunch", "Met at the gate") and, once, "Anyway".

**8. Closings.**

- Reagan closes on the last activity or a state (bed, the desk, homework, poor sleep, a judgment of a film).
- Truman closes on a feeling or an intent (wanting to go home) or the last point of a meeting.
- Only one entry looks ahead.

**9. Names.**

- Reagan uses first names for family and friends, and full names for officials the first time.
- Truman uses titles and last names.

**10. Shorthand.** Reagan writes with dropped subjects ("Had a talk", "Spent most of it"), fragments and abbreviations. That is a private habit, not a rule for a written page.

## How this compares with the game today

- **News** (`src/english/english-newscast.ts`, `composeNewscast`):
  - A printed story is a headline, a lede and a fixed list of context pieces for its kind. A closing is "closing-account" then "closing-workers", and a vote is "vote-margin".
  - No kind has these parts:
    - a named person's view (paraphrase, then quote);
    - a stakes paragraph saying what changes for the people of the place;
    - a reaction from someone the record reached;
    - a next step;
    - the same meeting's other business.
  - Each of those parts is something a record can hold (the act's sponsor and votes, the people a law reached, the next calendar date for the body, the other acts of that sitting).
- **The Journal** (`src/director/journal.ts`, `composeJournal`): it tells moments as lines and chapters, and has no step that groups one week's moments into a paragraph.

## What this suggests (for the Story lead to decide)

1. **Build a story from parts the records hold, in an order set by its kind.** The common parts are:
   - the lede (actor and action, plus the stakes when the record has them);
   - the stakes;
   - a named person's view as paraphrase, then quote;
   - the tally;
   - background;
   - a reaction;
   - the next step;
   - the meeting's other business.

   A part with no record behind it is left out, never filled. The Wauseon shape (agenda order, no quote) is the one to avoid. Real papers print it, and it is P45's "these aren't stories".

2. **Group by sitting.** One story tells a council or commission sitting. Its lesser acts become "In other business" lines at the end, instead of each printing as its own story. This also lowers the count of thin stories P45 objects to.
3. **Closings are about the person.** About half the story is the owner's history and feelings, and it ends with the customers or the owner's next step. The game's two context pieces cover the account and the workers, and miss the person.
4. **Who speaks:**
   - In this sample, officials and owners speak; residents seldom do, and late.
   - The owner's rulings already cover quotes. An official may say one figure, once, about their own act, and people say how it feels, never the figure.
   - The household example in 4 of 5 budget stories ("the owner of a $200,000 home pays...") is a figure. Whether the paper may print one is the Story lead's call under those rulings.
5. **A first-person page (from the diaries).** The page:
   - opens on what kind of day or week it was;
   - gives most events a bare mention and one or two a reaction, a physical detail or what someone said (paraphrased);
   - puts home and family in among the work;
   - closes on the last thing done or a state, not a summary.

   A longer stretch is grouped by phase (the errands, the weekend, the one call that mattered), not one line per event. The P43 "list of facts" is a page where every event is told the same way, with no opening word for the day and no reaction anywhere.

6. **A public week paragraph (from the officials' letters)** groups by one shared thing (the same meeting, place or people) and otherwise holds one event. The writer's feeling is about one sentence in five, tied to one event. A week is ordered one of three ways: by day, biggest first, or by subject. One writer keeps one order.
7. **What to leave out.**
   - Real papers print no story about a law that did not change.
   - Weekly letters give the coming week a large share.

   For P51 ("a week. Nothing happened"), a page can look ahead to the calendar when the past week holds little. It must not report as news what did not change.
