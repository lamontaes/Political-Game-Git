## What the owner can play now

- **Law where you live (Oct 12):** a new world in any of the 56 places opens with that place's real law at every level: federal, state, county and city minimum wage (the highest floor that legally applies wins; a city ordinance only counts where the state allows it), federal, state and local income tax (territories pay their own instead of federal), state plus local sales tax, the county's property tax, and SNAP, unemployment and Medicaid amounts. A raise scheduled for later in the year takes effect on its date, is announced as a public record, and lifts every town job under the new floor that day. No screen yet: the bench's "law here" view reads `lawHere` (needs OPUS-PLAY).
- **Who governs you (Oct 12):** every seat that covers your town has a holder from day one: president and vice president, your two U.S. senators and your House member (or delegate), the governor and every statewide elected office, your state senator and representative, your county board, row officers (sheriff, clerk, assessor and the rest) and your city council and mayor. Local seats are held by your own neighbors, chosen from residents whose personalities suit public life; state and federal holders are fictional people sitting in the party that really holds that seat. If a holder dies, the seat is filled the way that office's real rule says (a successor, an appointment, or a special election). No name matches a real officeholder. No screen yet: `townSeats(api)` lists them for the bench (needs OPUS-PLAY).
- **Running for office, the rules (Oct 12):** every seat covering your town has its next election with the real date, primary and filing deadline, and the office that takes filings (secretary of state, county clerk or elections office, city clerk or secretary, whichever the law names). You can file once if you meet that place's rules (age, residency, registration, and a fee or signatures). The fee comes out of your own money and goes to the government. Signatures come from real neighbors, each deciding for themselves. If you can't run, you get the rule that stops you. It needs OPUS-PLAY's filing screen and OPUS-PEOPLE's clerk conversation to play; the engine calls are `openContests`, `checkEligibility`, `fileCandidacy` and `requestSignature`.
- **Campaigning (Oct 12):** after filing, you knock doors for a few hours (the most outgoing adult in each house answers, if anyone will open to a stranger, and tells their family), recruit family and friends as volunteers, ask warm voters for money (never over the real legal limit for that office), give speeches whose statements become promises, run ads with the money raised, ask for endorsements (only someone who agrees with you more than your rival says yes) and commission polls of real people. Each person you reach changes what they know and how warm they are toward you for their own recorded reasons; click anyone for why (`causesFor`). Your rival knocks doors too. Views are a stand-in until OPUS-PEOPLE's views engine lands. Needs PLAY's campaign screen to play.
- **Election day (Oct 12):** on each race's real date, every adult in town decides whether to vote (their sense of duty and how strongly they feel about the race, against what their work week costs them) and whom to vote for (what reached them, how far each known platform is from their own views, the candidates' parties). Someone with nothing to separate the candidates leaves that race blank. The rest of the district or state votes as groups that split the way their named neighbors did, sized to the area's real 2024 vote. Incumbents stand again and state and federal races get a challenger from the other major party until OPUS-PEOPLE's "who runs" lands. Exit polls report what voters said. The winner takes the seat on the term's first day. Click any voter for their reasons (`ballotOf`). Named turnout runs about 35–42%, near the 2022 midterm's 46%. Needs PLAY's election-night screen to play.
- **Parties and primaries (Oct 12):** your state's real parties (everyone on its 2024 presidential ballot, or its legislature's parties; none in nonpartisan American Samoa) exist as organizations with members from town and a leader. About 56% of adults identify with the party on their side (Gallup 2024), about 7% lean nowhere (Pew 2024), and the rest call themselves independent. Primaries follow the state's method: party primaries among the party's voters (and independents where the state allows), top-two in California and Washington, top-four in Alaska. Losers come off the November ballot.
- **Your record follows you (Oct 12):** what you say in speeches is on the record as promises, and votes cast in office are on the record. A promise is kept or broken by your later votes. At the next election, a voter who knows you weighs each vote by their own view of the question, and holds a kept or broken promise against you if they agreed with it. Each part is a reason on their ballot. A rival's attack ad shows each viewer the worst of your record for them, and a clean record gives it nothing to show. Council votes start writing to the record once councils run on core2 (step 6).
- **Where your taxes go (Oct 12, first slice):** the federal government, your state, your county and your city each have a treasury and a budget (revenue by source and spending by function from the Census per-resident figures for your state; the federal one from the Treasury's fiscal 2025 statement), adopted on each fiscal year's first day. Every paycheck pays the taxes the law in force says: federal, state and city income tax, Social Security and Medicare. A Detroit worker's paycheck shows Detroit's 2.4% city tax going to the city. Not yet: sales tax, property tax, public payroll drawn from treasuries, and benefits reaching people.
- **Pass a law (Oct 12, first slice):** from a council seat you draft an ordinance with real amounts (for example, the minimum wage two dollars over today's floor). It gets a number and a cost estimate (how many town jobs it raises, and what that costs a year). If the state rules it out for its places, you're blocked in the rule's words. You learn a colleague's stake by asking them or reading their record, and you can trade: amend the bill, back their item, or owe them a favor. The council votes on each member's own reasons: their views, their constituents' lean, their tie to you, and any deal. Every vote goes on the record. A directly elected mayor signs or vetoes; two thirds can override. On its effective date the law takes effect, and the town's workers under the new floor get raises. Generated-world runs: an Illinois town's $15→$17 raise passed 4–1 and was signed (33 jobs raised); a Colorado town overrode the mayor's veto 5–0. Needs PLAY's bill paper and meeting screens, and PEOPLE's meeting scene. The same engine runs your state: a bill moves through both chambers (every seat votes, named legislators by their real seat's party until they're filled in), the governor signs or vetoes, and it takes effect by your state's own rule. In Illinois a $1 raise passed the Senate 40–19, was signed, and takes effect January 1, 2027 under Ill. Const. art. IV, § 10. Congress runs on it too: all 435 voting House seats and 100 Senate seats vote by their real party, your own representative and senators as named officials with records, and the president signs or vetoes (the presidency's party from data). A federal minimum-wage raise from your representative failed in the House 217–221. Larger bodies send a bill to its subject committee first: the chair (the senior member of the majority) holds a hearing only on a bill they don't oppose, witnesses with a stake (for a minimum wage, workers paid under the new floor) testify on the record, and the committee votes before the floor. A policy ordinance changes people's records on its effective date: an anti-discrimination ordinance in a North Carolina town gave 140 named adults that right on their record (enforced by a human rights commission), and a repeal takes it back off. Catalogued so far: anti-discrimination, a handgun purchase age of 21, paid family leave, right to counsel in eviction, bottle deposits. The mayor sets budget priorities (say, $100,000 more for parks) and on the fiscal year's first day the council votes the budget with them in it; each member weighs the changes by their own views and their duty to keep the city funded, and a rejected budget is carried forward as last year's. In two generated towns the council adopted the mayor's parks priority 5–0. The bill paper's catalog is data: the 18 accepted subject menus and their 133 topics, with 74 supported policies placed under them (wages and local taxes to raise or lower, 54 recorded policies to enact or repeal, 12 budget lines to expand or restrict); each shows its amount in force and whether the town may act, and a pick becomes a provision (`billCatalog`, `provisionFor`). An ordinance can also change a line of the city's budget: $500,000 more for parks passed 5–0 in one generated town and over a veto in another.
- **Your state and Congress legislate on their own (Oct 12, M3-10 first slice):** your state's legislature meets on its real 2026 session calendar (one that sits all year brings its agenda at the end of June, a tunable estimate) and passes laws whether or not you're in it. At the session's end the majority leader brings the few policies furthest from what they want, either enacting one the state lacks or repealing one it has. Each bill goes through committee (seats split by the chamber's party ratio), both chambers and the governor, and takes effect by the state's own rule. Every vote is a member's own. In New Mexico the legislature enacted paid family leave and two other acts, in force May 20, 2026, and 51 to 86 named workers per town got paid family leave on their records. In Kansas the Republican supermajority repealed three policies over the governor's vetoes. Congress brings its majority's agenda in mid-December. The Senate needs 60 votes to end debate (Rule XXII), so party-line bills pass the House and stall in the Senate, as they do now. Ballot measures come next.

- **Ballot measures and local bodies (Oct 12, M3-10):** every real 2026 statewide measure that settles a recorded policy question is on the town's ballot in its state (17 measures in 13 states, from Ballotpedia: voter ID in California, Nevada and North Carolina, abortion questions in Missouri, Nevada and Virginia, Washington's income-tax repeal, and others). On election day each adult in town decides for their own reasons and can be clicked for them. The rest of the state votes by its 2024 lean. A measure passes by the state's own rule (Florida 60%, Colorado amendments 55%, Nevada amendments only after a second approval) and becomes state law on its date. Each voter's view is their lean plus how popular the policy is nationally (polls from Pew, Gallup, KFF and others; 34 of 54 questions are marked estimates). In fresh runs California's voter ID passed with 72.6%, Idaho's reproductive-rights statute failed with 44.2%, and Washington's income-tax repeal failed with 45.5%. The town council and county board also bring their own ordinances four times a year, on the same engine. A Maryland county board passed rent stabilization at its first meeting, recorded as county law.

- **Recalls (Oct 12, M3-10):** where the state gives its towns a recall (the lifted municipal recall rules: no recall in New York, Ohio or Minnesota towns, for example), an ordinance that takes effect can start one. The resident most aggrieved by a member's vote, with the duty and fight in them, starts a petition. Neighbors sign if they oppose how that member voted, and with enough signatures in the state's window there's a recall vote. A Montana council member was recalled 146–93 and the seat filled by its vacancy rule. Louisiana, Mississippi and California members kept their seats. Open: a replacement race on the same ballot where the doctrine has one (it fills by the vacancy rule for now).

- **Interest groups (Oct 12, M3-07 first slice):** when a wage bill comes up, the town's employers it would cost decide for themselves whether to organize. One joins the employers' association when the bill would cost it more in a year than the dues and it has the cash. Dues move through the books into the group's own account. The group's lobbyist (a named resident) meets every council member, or the town's own legislators in a bigger body, and each member weighs the group's case among their recorded reasons. In Nebraska and Illinois towns, 14 of 16 and 16 of 17 affected employers joined, and the case moved each member by about −0.27. Next: groups that donate, advertise and back opponents, groups from causes, and starting groups grown from the past.

- **Constituent cases (Oct 12, M3-08 simple):** at each local office's monthly office hours, residents bring real problems from their own records: out of work with thin savings against their living costs, or paid under the minimum wage in force. They come when the problem presses hard enough and they're ready to reach out, and they go to the council member or mayor they know best. As an official you decide each case (`handleCase`: help or decline). Otherwise your office decides within a week by your own sense of duty. Helping a wage complaint gets the pay raised to the legal floor through the labor office. Helping a job seeker refers them to the workforce office. Either way the resident's view of you moves, and their next ballot reads it as a cause. In Texas and Illinois towns, being helped warmed a resident by 0.2–0.27, and being turned away cooled them by 0.2. Open: staff handling cases (M3-06), more problem kinds as other engines add records (benefits, housing).
