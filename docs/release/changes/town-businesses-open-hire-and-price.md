id: town-businesses-open-hire-and-price
impact: minor
section: Changed
title: Town businesses open where customers go unserved, hire by their books and set their own prices
---

A new business used to open on a roll of the dice, and a resident was picked
at random to run it. Now a resident opens one where the town spends more on
its kind than the businesses already there can serve. The one who opens it
is someone who has worked in that trade, then the oldest. Each business
opens at its kind's real profit margin from the IRS's business tax records,
and it is named for its founder's family, a family of the town, a street or
its county.

A business hires only while its sales cover one more worker, and it lets
the newest go when they no longer do. Whoever runs a business never quits
it. When the owner retires, dies or moves away and nobody is left, it
closes, and its record says who left. Hiring draws nothing: a resident
takes the work they have done before, then the kind of work the town is
shortest of. Each worker at the start has held their job for the typical
number of years for their age.

Each business sets its prices from its costs, how busy it is and what its
rivals charge, and customers choose among rivals by price. A city's sales
tax now follows what its town's shops, restaurants, inns and other
businesses sell, so a closing or a recession reaches the city's budget.

A town bank now holds the deposits real banks hold in its county for each
resident, from the FDIC's Summary of Deposits. Its losses come from its own
borrowers: businesses that close owing it, and households whose breadwinner
lost a job and stayed out of work past unemployment benefits and the day
banks write a loan off. Every business borrows from the town's largest bank.
