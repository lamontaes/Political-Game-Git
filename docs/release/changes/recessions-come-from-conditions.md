title: Recessions, interest rates and business closings now come from what is happening in the economy
---

A recession used to start on a roll of the dice: about one chance in 64 each
month, whatever the economy was doing. Now none is drawn. Output follows new
lending, the interest rate and lost jobs. When lending dries up, the rate is
set too high or layoffs spread, output falls. When output shrinks for three
months in a row, the game records a recession and names what pulled output
down. When it grows for three months, the recession ends. Across a century of
simulated years, recessions average about 13 months, and long ones are rare,
which is close to the historical record.

The interest rate is set by a committee of 12 people, as federal law sets it:
seven governors, the president of the New York reserve bank, and four other
reserve bank presidents who take turns by year. Each member has a recorded
view on inflation, and they vote at eight meetings a year.

A town's businesses keep books. Each takes in its share of what the town
spends on its kind of business. It pays its staff and its other costs, and
borrows from the town's bank when cash runs short. It closes when its cash
and credit are both gone, no longer at a flat rate a year. A new shop takes
its sales from the ones already there. Town banks take the shape of real
small banks in the same state. A bank whose losses use up its capital fails.
Each closing or bank failure is recorded with its cause, and it slows the
town's own economy.
