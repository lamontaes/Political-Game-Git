title: A council member whose bill you vetoed brings it up the next time you talk.
---

When you veto a bill as mayor or governor, the desk now asks why: the reasons on offer are the ones the record holds (the voters, the groups or the party against it, the views of people in town, the cost, your own stand from the campaign), or no reason at all. The veto cools things with the bill's sponsor. The next time the two of you talk, the sponsor brings it up once: whether the veto stood or was overridden, the reason you gave, and how they feel about it. You can stand by it, say you're sorry, or say you have to get back to work. Saying you're sorry wins back part of what the veto cost, more with someone who doesn't hold grudges; standing by it or walking off sours things further, more with someone who does.

A veto is no longer counted as time spent together: someone whose bill you vetoed, or who answered your whip, or whose promise was settled, no longer greets you as if you saw each other that day. A group that residents formed over a bill is now called by its own name ("Affordable Homes Coalition") when the sponsor or the desk says who pressed the members against it.
