title: Appointments go to people the appointer knows, and a job given is a favor owed
---

When a President fills the vice presidency or the Chief Justice's seat, a
governor fills a Senate seat, or a mayor fills an empty council seat, they now
choose from people they know instead of drawing a stranger. They weigh how much
they trust the person, whether the person is in their own party, whether the
person already serves in office, and what they owe or are owed. Their own
temperament decides how much each of those counts.

Every appointment that takes effect is remembered as a favor, and so is a job
at a town business given by the person who runs it. Someone who helped the
appointer and was passed over remembers that too.

Favors are paid back. A legislator who owes the person carrying a bill leans
toward voting for it, and in a town election the households that owe a
candidate their jobs lean toward that candidate.

Town public jobs are never handed out this way. Since the Supreme Court's 1990
ruling in Rutan v. Republican Party of Illinois, public employers may not hire
rank-and-file workers for their politics, and the game follows that rule. A
debt for a job or a post fades with time, so a following lasts only while the
jobs keep coming.
