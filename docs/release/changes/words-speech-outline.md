id: words-speech-outline
impact: minor
section: Changed
title: Presidents, governors, local heads and council members give addresses with one theme, a stated condition and plain asks.
---

Every address now goes through one outline planner. The President's inaugural and State of the Union, a governor's State of the State and a mayor's State of the City each follow the order real speeches of their kind use. Each picks one theme from the speaker's own issues and from what is happening in their world, and keeps to it. A point opens with a sentence of context from the world's records ("For 4 years now, prices have gone up about 5.4 percent a year"), then gives the speaker's stand, then the ask. Each ask is made once, to the level of government that can act on it, and says plainly whether the speaker is for it or against it. An inaugural no longer brags about a year the new President did not govern and no longer numbers its asks. The greeting is its own sentence, the President's home state is no longer named, and the Vice President is said with their title the first time and as "the Vice President" after.

A town's address is now given wherever the place's records seat a head: the mayor, an elected county executive, or whoever presides over the township or county board, speaking to "the council" or "the board" as that body is named. Its points come from what that body decides: the town's own budget lines and the ordinances it may pass, argued from the speaker's own views. A council or board member can give a floor speech on the measure before the body, addressed to whoever presides and ending in the vote, and a campaign speech built from what they run on. A governor's or local head's opening tells the budget's balance only when it is short or the speech is about money, with how it compares to the year before, and a law that took effect only when it is on the speech's subject and has already reached people there. A question named once is said after by a name that still says what it is, a paragraph never makes the same point twice, and a speaker argues every point of a speech from the same side of its principle.

A town council or board member's floor speech now plays in the floor-debate scene in their own words before the player answers them, and a local candidate's stump speech plays before the candidate comes over to ask for the player's support. A town runs only on questions its records hold: a town with no police spending does not campaign on police oversight. A governor's or town's address now plays its words in the scene at home, as the President's does.
