# Statehood research: D.C. and five inhabited territories

D.C. and the five inhabited U.S. territories have different constitutional and political positions. Congress has authority to admit new states, but the historical record supplies no single statehood route and does not establish a universal local referendum requirement. Residents' views and local votes can shape political support; they do not themselves admit a jurisdiction. Treat any requirement for a particular place as **UNKNOWN** until its controlling law and current congressional proposal are checked.

CRS's June 2024 overview describes all five territories as having elected governors, territorial legislative institutions, and House delegates; none has Electoral College votes. American Samoa's constitution specifies that county councils select its senators under Samoan custom, while voters elect House members. The overview describes territorial self-government as varying and potentially evolving. Use it for common institutional orientation, not for individual current political positions or plebiscite results.

## Federal route and limits of what is settled

- **States:** The Constitution's Admissions Clause says Congress may admit new states. It separately requires consent of affected state legislatures and Congress when a state is formed within another state's jurisdiction or by joining states or parts of states. The text does not prescribe a single application or referendum procedure. [U.S. Constitution, Article IV, Section 3](https://constitution.congress.gov/constitution/article-4/).
- **Inhabited territories:** CRS says a change in territorial political status, including statehood, requires congressional approval through a statutory change. It says would-be states have used different methods and there is no single path. That is a congressional research summary, not a judicial holding for every possible future proposal. [CRS, Statehood Process and Political Status (June 6, 2024)](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IF/PDF/IF11792/IF11792.5.pdf).
- **D.C.:** D.C. is a federal district, so its statehood question is distinct from territorial statehood. Article I gives Congress authority over the federal district, Article IV contains the state-admission clause, and the Twenty-Third Amendment grants presidential electors to the District. CRS identifies constitutional considerations raised by proposed D.C. legislation, including the District Clause and Twenty-Third Amendment. Whether a particular D.C. admission bill resolves all constitutional questions is **UNKNOWN** here. [CRS, D.C. Statehood: Constitutional Considerations for Proposed Legislation](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R47101/R47101.1.pdf); [Twenty-Third Amendment](https://constitution.congress.gov/constitution/amendment-23/).
- **Local consent:** Local elections, public opinion, a legislature's position, and any locally authorized vote can supply political input. These sources do not establish that one kind of vote is required, sufficient, or binding for every territory or for D.C. A jurisdiction-specific referendum requirement, threshold, ballot wording, and legal effect remain **UNKNOWN** unless directly established by the relevant statute or admission proposal.
- **Choices beyond statehood:** CRS describes statehood, independence (including free association), and continued territorial status as political options discussed for territories. This does not establish that each is legally or practically available on identical terms to each place. [CRS, Statehood Process and Political Status (June 6, 2024)](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IF/PDF/IF11792/IF11792.5.pdf).

## Use in the game

Keep individual statehood views separate from formal institutional acts. A resident's support or opposition can affect organizing and political arguments without mechanically deciding admission. Use each place's applicable institutions and sourced procedures; do not impose one referendum model on all six jurisdictions. Do not treat a local vote as Congress's admission decision. No local opinion percentages, live bill status, future ballot rules, or place-specific legal prerequisites are supplied by this intake.

## Research limits

This note relies on the cited official constitutional text, D.C. government sources, Department of the Interior, and a Congressional Research Service overview last updated June 6, 2024. The CRS overview is explicit that the territories' self-governance arrangements vary and may evolve. Current party positions, residents' present views, current bill status, individual territory-specific admission steps, and disputed constitutional questions require separate current research. This is source intake, not legal advice or a claim that statehood is underway.
