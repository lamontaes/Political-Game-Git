# Storm records need local evidence before becoming lived events

**Recommendation.** Do not turn each NOAA Storm Events row into a damaging, townwide disaster. Keep four layers separate: observed background weather, a storm that a character actually encounters, documented local damage, and a formal emergency or disaster declaration. NOAA/NWS records can provide candidate events and footprints. FEMA records federal declarations. None supplies a nationwide annual rate for an individual character feeling a storm.

## Nationwide calibration method

1. **Freeze the source window and geography.** For comparison with the existing Storm Events catalog, use complete years 2000–2024 unless a later refresh is deliberately chosen. NCEI says its records are organized by county for smaller-area events and by NWS forecast zone for larger-area events; the database distinguishes event records from the larger weather-system episode. Do not pool county and zone denominators as if they were the same geography. [NCEI Storm Events User Guide, issued September 12, 2025](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/storm-events-database/assets/pdf/Storm_Events_Database_User_Guide.pdf).
2. **Build an explicit place crosswalk.** Resolve each supported game place to a verified boundary. For a report with a point or warning polygon, record whether it intersects that boundary. If only county or forecast-zone attribution is available, label the match `county/zone context`; do not assign the incident to every town or every resident in that area. A place with no defensible boundary match remains **UNKNOWN** at town level.
3. **Deduplicate reports before drawing a storm story.** Use NCEI `episode_id` to retain the shared system and `event_id` to preserve distinct phenomena and impacts. A useful aggregate is unique `episode_id × place × calendar-year`; keep all related events underneath it. Never count each LSR line, event row, county designation, or damage row as a separate townwide disaster. NWS says LSRs can summarize multiple reports; NCEI's user guide shows a tornado-outbreak episode containing 44 event records.
4. **Keep the rate denominators distinct.** Background context uses observed station-days; candidate storm frequency uses complete county-years or forecast-zone-years; damaging events use verified place-years with an explicit local match; declarations use designated place-years and each authority type separately. Do not divide county reports by character count or use event counts as a person's chance of feeling a storm.
5. **Use time as published.** Preserve NCEI event start/end date and time, stated time zone, and episode dates. Preserve NWS alert onset/expiration. For FEMA, store incident begin/end and declaration date separately. If a source supplies only a date or period, keep that precision and flag the missing time; never move every incident to the first day of a month.
6. **Treat perception as a recorded character fact.** A storm can enter a character's experience only when a credible local event overlaps their known place and time, and the simulation has a supported exposure or encounter. A warning may be available to a person only if the game establishes a route by which they received it. Until then, the personal experience, conversation, fear, damage, and aid remain **UNKNOWN**. Research does not justify invented person-level annual chances.

NWS's general severe-thunderstorm definition identifies hail at least one inch in diameter or wind gusts of at least 58 mph. This may help distinguish a warning-level hazard from ordinary weather, but it is not a universal cutoff for felt experience, property loss, local emergency response, or FEMA assistance. FEMA states that a major-disaster request considers whether the event exceeds state and local response capabilities, and the President makes the federal declaration. Local emergency actions can happen without federal aid. [NWS severe-thunderstorm safety](https://www.weather.gov/safety/thunderstorm); [Stafford Act § 401](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title42-section5170&num=0&edition=prelim).

This note does not inspect current code or assert those counts are the current behavior.

NCEI is the public source of NWS Storm Data. Its data are significant-weather records, not a census of every storm or every local impact. Event types and report practices change over time, event rows may be grouped into episodes, and county/zone footprints are broader than many towns. The sources reviewed do not supply a validated nationwide conversion from county-level NCEI counts to town-local damage, personal exposure, newsroom coverage, or local emergency declarations. Exact tier thresholds, local response coverage, and each character's experienced exposure therefore remain **UNKNOWN** pending place-specific evidence.
