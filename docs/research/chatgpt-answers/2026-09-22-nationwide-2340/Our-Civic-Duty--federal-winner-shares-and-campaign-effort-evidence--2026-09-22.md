# A 93% vote share can happen; one campaign cannot be assumed to create it

The official federal results show that very lopsided candidate shares do occur in U.S. House contests, while they were absent from the contested U.S. Senate races in the four cycles examined. Those results do not establish a national baseline for local, county, state legislative or statewide executive races. Campaign experiments also do not support translating each week of one candidate's door-knocking into a fixed vote-share gain. The observed game path from 48% to 96% in 16 weeks has no evidentiary basis as a general rule.

## Owner direction

**OWNER DECISION — design direction, not a measured finding:** Rare 90%+ wins remain possible when the electorate and actual field support produce them. Campaign reach should be finite, and repeated one-person canvassing should face diminishing returns so it cannot manufacture a 48%-to-96% result by itself. The research here does not quantify the reach, diminishing-return rate or resulting ceiling; those values remain **UNKNOWN** pending calibration.

## What the vote totals show

**Measured from official certified results:** I calculated the top candidate's share and the top-two margin from the FEC's candidate-level general-election percentages for the 2016, 2018, 2020 and 2022 U.S. House and Senate elections. The series covers 50 states; nonvoting delegates are excluded. A contested race has at least two distinct FEC candidate identifiers with reported general-election vote percentages. The sample excludes races with an additional general-election runoff, ranked-choice rounds, or overlapping contests for the same state/seat that the FEC table does not distinguish. It contains 1,711 contested House races and 129 contested Senate races. For party-line fusion, I use the FEC combined-candidate percentage. Candidate share is a share of the reported candidate vote denominator; it is not a share of registered voters or all ballots cast. Percentiles are empirical order statistics, not modeled estimates.

| Office, 2016–2022 | Contested races | Winner share: 10th / median / 90th percentile | Victory margin: 10th / median / 90th percentile | Winners above 70% | Above 80% | Above 90% |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| U.S. House | 1,711 | 52.4% / 63.1% / 79.2% | 6.2 / 27.7 / 60.5 points | 446 (26.1%) | 161 (9.4%) | 71 (4.1%) |
| U.S. Senate | 129 | 50.1% / 56.9% / 67.0% | 2.8 / 16.0 / 40.3 points | 8 (6.2%) | 0 (0%) | 0 (0%) |

The 93% figure is therefore not intrinsically impossible. In the observed House sample, 71 of 1,711 contested races exceeded 90%. “Contested” here means at least two distinct FEC candidate identifiers had reported general-election votes; it does not mean the contest was close, well-funded on both sides, or seriously challenged. Scattered write-in totals do not count as a named opponent. In a single-winner plurality election, a token opponent can leave a winner with an overwhelming share. Races with fewer than two identified candidates are excluded from the contested threshold rates.

## What this does not establish

| Requested office type or split | Evidence status | Why it remains unknown here |
|---|---|---|
| Nonpartisan municipal mayor and council | **UNKNOWN** | No harmonized nationwide canvass series with consistent office, candidate-count, incumbent and denominator definitions was identified. City election systems and result formats vary. |
| Partisan county office | **UNKNOWN** | No nationwide candidate-level official results series with consistent county-office classifications was identified. |
| State House and state Senate | **UNKNOWN** | This memo did not assemble the 50 states' official state-legislative canvasses into a common verified corpus. Do not substitute federal House figures. |
| Statewide executive, including governor | **UNKNOWN** | No nationwide office-specific distribution was computed. Federal Senate is not a defensible proxy for governor. |
| House and Senate by safe / lean / competitive district | **UNKNOWN** | The FEC result files do not provide a common pre-election district-partisanship category. A crosswalk needs a named baseline and classification date. |
| Opponent campaign-response rate or activity | **UNKNOWN** | These election returns record results, not whether or how much an opponent canvassed, called, advertised or otherwise campaigned. Candidate filing and vote share cannot measure campaign activity. |
| Low-turnout local effects and undervote rate | **UNKNOWN** | The federal candidate-share calculations do not include a comparable nationwide local turnout/undervote denominator. Candidate vote share must not be described as a percentage of all eligible voters or all ballots. |

An undervote is a ballot with no valid vote recorded for that contest. It is excluded from the sum of candidate votes used in a candidate's vote share, even though it may remain part of a broader ballot or turnout denominator. A race can consequently have a candidate with a high share of votes cast for candidates while that candidate received a much smaller share of eligible voters. Keep those denominators distinct.

## What campaign studies say about movement

The available experiments estimate effects of a particular contact under a particular design. They do not estimate a universal weekly gain, a starting support range by office size, or an eventual support ceiling.

| Study and population | Direct finding | What it does not measure |
|---|---|---|
| Kalla and Broockman, *American Political Science Review* (2018): systematic meta-analysis of 40 field experiments, plus nine original field experiments, on campaign contact/advertising and general-election candidate choice | The meta-analysis estimates an average effect of zero on vote choice. Early contact can show an immediate persuasive effect that decays by election day; contact closer to election day generally has minimal effects. | A weekly slope, a ceiling, a statewide-office estimate, or a rule that every campaign and voter responds the same way. |
| Baum and Owens (2023), Tennessee State House District 37, 16 precincts, 2020 general election: candidate's own door-to-door campaigning in quasi-randomly selected precincts | Their difference-in-differences/first-difference estimates report a 3 percentage-point increase in the candidate's vote and a 6-point increase in two-candidate vote margin. In the district overall, the candidate received 58.2% and the opponent 41.8%. | A national average, a governor's race, sixteen weeks of activity, one volunteer's effect, or the effect of repeatedly contacting the same voters. This is one small, context-specific study. |
| Gerber and Green (2000), roughly 30,000 registered voters in New Haven, Connecticut, before the November 1998 election | Randomized nonpartisan canvassing increased turnout substantially; mail's effect was smaller and telephone calls showed no turnout effect in that study. | Candidate preference or vote-share conversion: this is a mobilization experiment, not a campaign-persuasion estimate. |

The evidence allows that personal candidate campaigning can matter in a particular race. It does not support treating each 90-minute volunteer shift as a fixed transfer of 1.35 percentage points, independent of electorate size, prior support, repeated exposure, persuadability, opponent activity or turnout. The studied outcomes do not justify compounding such a gain for 16 weeks. The model's 48% to 96% trajectory is therefore **unsupported**, rather than a measured or evidence-calibrated effect. This does not prove that no real campaign can ever move from 48% to 96%; it means these sources do not establish that one person's ordinary canvassing can cause that movement.

## What the evidence can support

The empirical fallback is to preserve the distinction between campaign effort, underlying support, turnout and counted vote share. Official federal returns establish that very high shares are possible, especially where the contest is not competitive. They do not tell us that effort produced the result. For local and state office types, safe/lean/competitive splits, starting support, weekly movement, opponent response, and a support ceiling, the honest research value is **UNKNOWN** until a defined data collection or experiment supplies it.
