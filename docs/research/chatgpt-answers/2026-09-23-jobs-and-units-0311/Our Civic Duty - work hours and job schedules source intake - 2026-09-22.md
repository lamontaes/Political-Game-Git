# Work-hour sources measure different things from a job's shift schedule

**Lead.** Official sources can support realistic weekly-hour ranges by occupation and industry, and diary data can show when people tend to work. They do not usually report the exact shift a particular employer offers. Treat weekly hours, full- or part-time status, days worked, and shift start/end times as separate facts. A 20-hour week could be two long shifts or several short ones; the sources do not justify making every job a four-hour shift.

## Reading the numbers for job offers

The CPS says 35 hours is a statistical definition of full time, not a legal rule. A person can be usually full time but work fewer hours during the reference week; a person usually part time can temporarily work 35 hours or more. CPS actual weekly hours also combine all jobs for multiple jobholders, while the occupation and industry category describes the main or sole job. [BLS CPS definitions](https://www.bls.gov/cps/definitions.htm).

Use survey hours to set plausible **weekly totals** and full-/part-time mix for a job category. Use ATUS separately to shape broad workday and time-of-day patterns. Do not divide a weekly total by an assumed number of shifts and call the result an observed schedule. None of these sources tells us an individual employer's offer, regular days off, break pattern, on-call status, seasonal schedule, or whether a position is a four-hour shift.

For territory coverage, do not substitute CPS national averages for Puerto Rico, American Samoa, CNMI, Guam, or the U.S. Virgin Islands. ACS/PRCS supplies the resident past-year measures for Puerto Rico; 2020 Island Areas census tables supply older usual-hour bins for the other four inhabited territories. Current occupation-by-hours evidence for those territories remains limited or **UNKNOWN** at the desired subnational granularity.
