# Qualified elector: what a life simulation must know

**Research date:** 2026-09-22  
**Scope:** Common design vocabulary plus selected official-law examples. This is not a 50-state legal survey or legal advice. It does not certify the qualification rules of every state or office.

## Answer to the filed question

“Qualified elector” is a cross-reference, not a uniform nationwide checklist. It generally points to the person’s legal eligibility to vote in the relevant place and election. Some jurisdictions treat registration as a separate administrative prerequisite to casting a ballot; others make current registration part of the definition of elector. Ohio is an explicit example of the latter. Therefore the game must not globally equate “qualified elector” with “registered voter,” nor assume registration is merely proof everywhere.

The reusable model should keep separate facts for (1) substantive eligibility, (2) registration and its status/date, (3) office-specific candidacy qualifications, and (4) which date each rule tests. A record may be eligible to register yet not registered; registered records can become stale, canceled, or challenged; a candidate may have an office-specific district residence requirement beyond ordinary voter residence. A registration record is strong evidence of what election administrators recognized, but should not silently erase a known disqualifying event or satisfy a distinct candidacy condition.

## Facts and how naturally a life simulation can represent them

| Fact or event | What law can ask | Existing life-simulation fit | Design handling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Birth date / age | At least 18 is the common constitutional baseline for voting, subject to election-specific preregistration and primary rules | Natural, exact and date-based | Derive age on the rule’s named assessment date; do not store an age boolean that goes stale |
| U.S. citizenship | Many state elector definitions require it | A life sim may know citizenship only if it records birth/naturalization | Store dated citizenship status and evidence/event source; unknown stays unknown |
| Residence in state / county / district / precinct | A jurisdiction may set a duration; candidacy may require residence in the particular district | Current and past addresses are plausible world facts, but “domicile” is not always reducible to the current address | Preserve dated residence intervals and the rule’s geographic unit; do not infer long-term residence from current town alone |
| Voter registration | In some states it is part of “elector”; elsewhere registration is a condition to cast a ballot but not the underlying qualification | Usually absent unless the simulation has a civic-registration action/state | Separate registration history, jurisdiction, status and effective date from eligibility |
| Felony conviction, sentence, restoration | State law varies; an offense, sentence status, and restored voting/civil rights can matter | Not safe to infer from generic “criminal history”; it would require explicit, sensitive world events | Only apply a rule when qualifying conviction/restoration facts are explicitly recorded; unknown must not become eligible or barred by default |
| Adjudication affecting voting capacity | Some states have a court finding specifically relevant to voting; it is not the same as disability, diagnosis or needing assistance | Not naturally known absent a precise judicial event | Use only a dated, jurisdictional voting-rights adjudication and restoration/removal event; never derive from health or personality |
| Office-specific eligibility | Minimum age, district residence, citizenship duration, incompatible office, term limits, etc. vary by seat | Some facts are known; others may not be | Evaluate the actual office rule pack independently of the general elector predicate |

## Official examples showing why a universal shortcut fails

- **Ohio:** The Constitution’s voter qualification expressly includes U.S. citizenship, age 18, residence for the time provided by law, and registration for 30 days. Ohio Revised Code §3501.01 defines “elector” / “qualified elector” as a person with the qualifications provided by law to be entitled to vote. Thus, for Ohio, registration is part of the elector status used by the state’s own text, not only a clerk’s optional evidence field. Ohio also has a rule to cancel a registered elector’s registration on felony conviction and on a voting-specific incompetency adjudication; the underlying qualification, registration record, and candidacy rule remain conceptually separable. [Ohio Const. art. V, §1](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-constitution/section-5.1), [Ohio Rev. Code §3501.01](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-3501.01), [§3503.21](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-3503.21).
- **Ohio candidacy timing:** Ohio Const. art. XV, §4 says no person shall be elected or appointed to state office unless possessing elector qualifications. For party-nomination petitions, Ohio Rev. Code §3513.261 requires the candidate to declare that they “are an elector qualified to vote for the office” sought. The Ohio Supreme Court held that “qualified to vote for the office” can require district-level elector status, confirming a distinction between merely being a state elector and being an elector for that seat. This supports at least a filing-stage check in that petition pathway; Article XV separately states an office-holding/election condition. The exact timing should be recorded per rule/office/pathway, not collapsed to one nationwide moment. [Ohio Const. art. XV, §4](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-constitution/section-15.4), [Ohio Rev. Code §3513.261](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-3513.261), [State ex rel. Markulin v. Ashtabula County Board of Elections (Ohio Supreme Court, 1992)](https://www.supremecourt.ohio.gov/rod/docs/pdf/0/1992/1992-Ohio-84.pdf).
- **Florida:** The Constitution defines an elector as a U.S. citizen, at least 18, a permanent state resident, and registered as provided by law. It also says a person convicted of a felony or adjudicated mentally incompetent is not qualified to vote or hold office until restoration/removal, with distinct restoration treatment for murder and felony sexual offenses. This is an explicit example where voting status and office eligibility both carry disqualification language, and restoration is a first-class dated event. [Florida Const. art. VI, §§2, 4](https://www.leg.state.fl.us/Statutes/index.cfm?Mode=Constitution&Submenu=3&Tab=statutes), [Fla. Stat. §98.0751](https://www.leg.state.fl.us/Statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0000-0099%2F0098%2F0098.html).
- **Maine:** The Legislature’s current Title 21, §247 entry is expressly repealed, illustrating that felony-voting restrictions cannot be inferred from a generic historic “felony” label or copied from another state. The current voting rule still needs its relevant statutory cross-check before turning that example into a Maine office profile. [Maine Rev. Stat. tit. 21, §247](https://legislature.maine.gov/statutes/21/title21sec247.html).

These examples establish variation, not a complete exception list. I have not sampled enough state law to name every state with a felony, registration, or competency exception, and the report intentionally makes no such claim.

## Assessment moment: answer with a rule field, not a single national date

There is no supported nationwide answer of “filing day,” “election day,” or “taking office.” A requirement can be tested at filing for ballot access, on election day for candidate/elector status, and again at qualification or commencement of office; a durational-residence clause anchors a lookback period to the date named in its text. Ohio demonstrates more than one check: the petition statute requires a present declaration at filing, while the constitutional rule speaks to being elected/appointed to office. Ohio’s constitution also makes each legislative chamber judge of its members’ election, returns and qualifications. [Ohio Rev. Code §3513.261](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-3513.261), [Ohio Const. art. II, §6](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-constitution/article-2).

For each qualification record, research should capture: the required fact; the seat/geographic scope; the source clause; the date expression (filing, election, commencement, or another named event); any lookback duration and anchor; whether later loss causes vacancy/removal; and the state/office/election pathway. When source text does not identify a timing rule, mark it unknown and keep a filing gate from claiming certainty.

## Ohio gameplay implication

At Athens, Ohio, where the reported blocker is the elector predicate by itself, the honest refusal can be replaced only once the game can evaluate the Ohio ingredients, including the registration fact/date and applicable residence. Ohio’s statewide age/citizenship/residence/registration values are documented above; exact state-legislative district residence is an office-specific condition and must continue to use the candidate’s actual address history plus the seat ID. Do not treat “the ordinary character is 40” as enough.

At Columbus, retain the separate multi-district refusal until a candidate-to-seat mapping yields one actual same seat ID. Solving elector status does not solve district ambiguity. Once facts and seat identity are known, the normal player path should explain any unmet condition in plain terms; it should allow the player to acquire a missing condition over life when law permits (for example, register, move, or reach age), then revisit filing without inventing success.

## Owner decision received after the research pass

The owner wants voter registration handled automatically when a person turns 18 and is otherwise eligible. The player should not have to perform a registration task. In the game, background registration should be a dated record derived when age, citizenship/nationality where applicable, residence, and other jurisdiction-specific conditions are satisfied; do not silently register an ineligible or unknown person merely because they reached 18. This is a game design decision, not a claim that every real jurisdiction automatically registers all 18-year-olds.

A separate player-facing question remains: for sensitive facts such as restoration of voting rights or a voting-specific court adjudication, how much should the candidacy screen reveal when the character record is incomplete? Until answered, unknown remains unknown rather than an automatic pass or bar.
