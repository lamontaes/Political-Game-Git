# Inhabited U.S. territories as political arenas — research brief

**Prepared:** September 22, 2026  
**Scope:** American Samoa, Guam, the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands (CNMI), and the U.S. Virgin Islands (USVI), alongside Puerto Rico and the District of Columbia as already covered elsewhere. Excludes the U.S. Minor Outlying Islands because the Census Bureau says they generally do not have year-round population. This is a broad source map, not a complete legal implementation specification or a certification of every local election rule.

## Owner intent to carry forward (game design, not a legal finding)

- These places should be real, playable political arenas; the player may eventually pursue statehood for a territory or D.C.
- There should be no special scripted “lead the fight” role. An eligible person can run for the relevant office. Support should grow through what the person says, who hears it, and how people respond. Statehood is pursued through ordinary politics and Congress.
- Territory residents must have their own varied opinions about their place’s future; do not give an entire electorate one fixed pro-statehood view. Model opinions as individual/local political facts that can change through campaigns and persuasion.
- Voter registration is background state: when a person turns 18 and otherwise meets that jurisdiction’s eligibility rules, registration should happen automatically. Registration should not be a player chore. This is an owner-approved game rule; it does not describe every real jurisdiction’s registration system.
- Do not assume a fixed approval route or a universally pro-statehood electorate. Preserve local self-determination and the fact that people may favor distinct futures. Make the struggle playable as an agenda pursued through normal political systems.
- Deep civic detail can exist while remaining optional for the player. The ordinary route should make the relevant offices and choices reachable without requiring a player to study every constitutional detail.

## Shared constitutional and federal frame

The Constitution gives Congress authority to admit new states (Article IV, Section 3) and broad authority over U.S. territories under the Territory Clause. The admission provision itself does not prescribe a universal referendum or one fixed territorial consent procedure. The historical statehood path has included local political action and federal legislation; for this game, local consent/organizing and a congressional fight are strong grounded parts of a proposed route, but the exact route and thresholds are game decisions, not a single constitutional checklist. [Constitution Annotated, Article IV](https://constitution.congress.gov/constitution/); [Constitution Annotated, Congress’s territorial authority](https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/essay/artIV-S3-C2-3/ALDE_00013511/%5B%27constitution%27%5D)

Residents of the four island territories do not elect presidential electors in the general presidential election. Their local elections and territorial congressional delegates are distinct from the states’ federal electorate. Federal law provides each of the four territories a nonvoting House Delegate; the House currently identifies delegates from American Samoa, CNMI, Guam and USVI, plus D.C., and a Resident Commissioner for Puerto Rico. House rules permit committee participation/votes but delegates do not cast ordinary votes on final House floor passage. Exact privileges can depend on House rules. [48 U.S.C. §1711 (Guam/USVI delegates)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&f=treesort&fq=true&granuleId=USC-prelim-title48-section1711&hl=true&num=20080&req=10+USC); [House directory/explanation](https://www.house.gov/representatives); [House History on delegates and committee votes](https://history.house.gov/HistoricalHighlight/Detail/25769807320?current_search_qs=%3FCommand%3DPrev%26CurrentPage%3D2%26PreviousSearch%3DNixon%252c%2BRichard%2BM.%252cSubject%252cFalse%252cFalse%252cFalse%252cFalse%252cFalse%252c%252cmm%252fdd%252fyyyy%252cmm%252fdd%252fyyyy%252cTitle%26SortOrder%3DTitle%26subject%3DNixon%252c%2BRichard%2BM.)

## Territory profiles

### American Samoa

- **Government / offices:** Locally adopted constitution and elected governor/lieutenant governor; bicameral Fono (House of Representatives and Senate); judiciary. The Senate’s selection is tied to Samoan custom and matai institutions, so it should not be modeled as an ordinary popular Senate race without checking the applicable constitution and law. The Election Office also publishes candidate qualifications for governor, local Fono seats and the U.S. Delegate. Interior describes American Samoa as having adopted its constitution in 1967 and holding its first constitutional elections in 1977. [DOI political status and government page](https://www.doi.gov/node/11562); [American Samoa Election Office — candidates](https://www.aselectionoffice.gov/candidates-information)
- **Local electorate:** The Election Office states local voters may be U.S. citizens or U.S. nationals; must be at least 18; have lived in American Samoa at least two years; and have been bona fide residents of the election district for at least one year. Its page describes felony/civil-rights and other disqualification rules. The constitutional text and live election rules should be checked together before exact implementation. [American Samoa Election Office — armed-forces and voter information](https://www.aselectionoffice.gov/armed-forces-info); [Election Office — registration](https://www.aselectionoffice.gov/registration)
- **Citizenship vs nationality:** People born in American Samoa generally acquire U.S. nationality, not U.S. citizenship at birth; U.S.-citizen parents and other routes can change the individual case. Nationality is enough for American Samoa’s local voting rule, but it is not equivalent to citizenship for federal elections/offices. DOI confirms the territory’s residents are U.S. nationals. This distinction is central to the player-facing electorate and should not be flattened into “all U.S. citizens.” [DOI political status](https://www.doi.gov/node/11562); [State Department nationality regulations, 8 FAM 302.1](https://fam.state.gov/fam/08fam/08fam030201.html)
- **Federal representation / presidency:** Elected nonvoting House Delegate; can take part in committee work under House rules, no ordinary final floor vote. Territory residents do not choose presidential electors in the general election. American Samoa’s Delegate eligibility statute uses “owe allegiance to the United States,” inhabitance, age 25, and no candidacy for another office; do not assume it has the same citizen-only qualifications as other delegates. [48 U.S.C. §1731 et seq.](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A48+section%3A1732+edition%3Aprelim%29); [House directory](https://www.house.gov/representatives)
- **Census geography:** State-equivalent for data, with districts/islands treated as county-equivalents; Census geography is not a substitute for cultural or political districts. [Census geography hierarchy](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/economic-census/guidance-geographies/levels.html)
- **Status preference evidence:** No current representative statewide election or referendum baseline on statehood was found in the reviewed official sources. Do not synthesize a preference from general political commentary. Unknown by electorate/time slice.

### Guam

- **Government / offices:** Organic Act organized territorial government; elected governor and lieutenant governor, a unicameral Legislature of Guam, judiciary, and village governments with elected mayors/vice mayors. Guam’s Election Commission maintains official eligibility and candidate qualifications. [DOI political status](https://www.doi.gov/node/11564); [Guam Election Commission candidate qualifications](https://gec.guam.gov/candidate-qualifications/)
- **Local electorate:** U.S. citizen, Guam resident, age 18 by election day (eligible 16+ preregistration; a 17-year-old turning 18 by the general election can vote in the paired primary/general cycle), and statutory mental/conviction qualifications. Residency is a personal domicile/residence inquiry, not automatically inherited from a parent or spouse. [Guam Election Commission voter rules](https://gec.guam.gov/voters/)
- **Citizenship vs nationality:** Guam-born persons subject to U.S. jurisdiction became U.S. citizens under the Organic Act. This differs from American Samoa’s general birth-status rule. [DOI political status](https://www.doi.gov/node/11564)
- **Federal representation / presidency:** One elected nonvoting House Delegate. Committee participation is available under House rules, but no ordinary final House floor vote. Guam has no presidential electors in the general election; local citizens may participate in party processes under the parties’ rules, which is not a territorial Electoral College vote. [48 U.S.C. §1711](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&f=treesort&fq=true&granuleId=USC-prelim-title48-section1711&hl=true&num=20080&req=10+USC); [House directory](https://www.house.gov/representatives)
- **Census geography:** State-equivalent. The whole of Guam is one county-equivalent in 2020 Census presentation; municipalities are county subdivisions, not counties. Preserve that distinction from actual village and election-district government. [Census 2020 technical documentation](https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/decennial/2020/technical-documentation/complete-tech-docs/demographic-and-housing-characteristics-file-and-demographic-profile/2020census-demographic-and-housing-characteristics-file-and-demographic-profile-techdoc.pdf)
- **Status preference evidence:** Guam has a dated official historical signal, not a current electorate baseline. A January 1982 multi-option status plebiscite gave Commonwealth 49%, statehood 26%, status quo 10%, incorporated territory 5%, free association 4%, independence 4%, other 2%. The September 1982 two-option runoff reportedly favored Commonwealth 73% to statehood 27%. These results are evidence of those ballots and dates only; they do not state current preferences. A later planned plebiscite was blocked after a federal court found ancestry-based voter exclusion unconstitutional; the Ninth Circuit affirmed in 2019. Guam’s Commission on Decolonization continues education/outreach for statehood, independence and free association. Do not build a plebiscite that excludes otherwise qualified voters by ancestry. [U.S. Government Publishing Office congressional hearing reproducing 1982 results](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-111shrg61779/pdf/CHRG-111shrg61779.pdf); [Guam Commission on Decolonization report to UN, 2019, containing case/status history](https://decol.guam.gov/sites/default/files/landworth-un-guam-working-paper-2019-1.pdf); [Ninth Circuit opinion, Davis v. Guam, 932 F.3d 822 (2019)](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/17-15719/17-15719-2019-07-29.html)

### Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands (CNMI)

- **Government / offices:** Self-governing commonwealth in political union with and under U.S. sovereignty, established through the Covenant. It has an elected governor and lieutenant governor, bicameral legislature (House and Senate), Commonwealth courts, municipalities and a federal district court. The Commonwealth Election Commission’s current pages show governor/lieutenant governor candidates, voter registration, local elections and voter statistics. Use its current roster/rules for seat counts and terms rather than relying on older DOI summaries. [Covenant / governing framework, DOI Manual](https://www.doi.gov/sites/doi.gov/files/elips/documents/575-dm-3.pdf); [CNMI Constitution, Law Revision Commission](https://cnmilaw.gov/constitution); [Commonwealth Election Commission](https://cnmi.gov.mp/)
- **Local electorate:** Constitution provides eligibility at age 18+, Commonwealth domicile and residence for the statutory period, not serving a felony sentence, not adjudged of unsound mind, and U.S. citizen or national; it authorizes the legislature to require citizenship. The current implementing statute and Election Commission form must resolve the operative citizenship/residency details. Do not assume noncitizen residents vote. The election commission’s 2026 voter form/statistics are the current operational source. [CNMI Constitution, Article VII](https://cnmilaw.gov/constitution); [Election Commission](https://cnmi.gov.mp/); [CNMI Election Law (Commission copy)](https://www.votecnmi.gov.mp/downloads/CNMI_Election_Law.pdf)
- **Citizenship vs nationality:** The Covenant made people born in the Northern Mariana Islands on or after the effective date U.S. citizens, subject to the terms of the Covenant. Check the person’s birthplace/date and statutory exceptions rather than infer citizenship solely from residence. [Covenant, Pub. L. 94-241, §301](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-90/pdf/STATUTE-90-Pg263.pdf)
- **Federal representation / presidency:** One elected nonvoting House Delegate (first elected in 2008); committee participation under House rules, no ordinary final floor vote. CNMI residents do not choose presidential electors in the general election. [House directory](https://www.house.gov/representatives); [House History](https://history.house.gov/Exhibitions-and-Publications/APA/Historical-Essays/Growing-Diversity/Pacific-Islanders/)
- **Census geography:** State-equivalent, municipalities as county-equivalents. [Census hierarchy](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/economic-census/guidance-geographies/levels.html)
- **Status preference evidence:** The 1975 plebiscite approved the Covenant establishing Commonwealth in political union: CNMI Law Revision Commission records 78.8% approval among ballots cast (with 95% of registered voters voting). This is strong historical consent for that arrangement at that moment, not a current preference among statehood, independence, free association or status quo. No current territorywide status-preference baseline found in the reviewed official sources. [Covenant process and recorded 1975 vote](https://cnmilaw.gov/covenant); [Covenant](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-90/pdf/STATUTE-90-Pg263.pdf); [House History of CNMI representation and Covenant](https://history.house.gov/Exhibitions-and-Publications/APA/Historical-Essays/Growing-Diversity/Pacific-Islanders/)

### U.S. Virgin Islands (USVI)

- **Government / offices:** Elected governor and lieutenant governor, unicameral Legislature of the Virgin Islands, territorial courts, and district/island-level election jurisdictions. Election System pages list elections and office qualifications. The 1954 Revised Organic Act remains a core federal/territorial framework; constitutional convention activity is ongoing, so don’t portray a proposed constitution as adopted. [DOI political status](https://www.doi.gov/oia/islands/virgin-islands); [Election System of the Virgin Islands](https://vivote.gov/elections/); [36th Legislature / Sixth Constitutional Convention action, 2025](https://www.vi.gov/governor-bryan-acts-on-bills-passed-by-the-36th-legislature-in-its-october-30-2025-session/)
- **Local electorate:** The Election System says a voter must be a U.S. citizen, 18 (or turn 18 before the next election), satisfy residence, not be disqualified under felony/civil-competency rules, and not be registered elsewhere. Individual requirements should use current Virgin Islands code and Election System administration pages. [Official USVI registration](https://vivote.gov/voters/register-to-vote/)
- **Citizenship vs nationality:** DOI states U.S. citizenship was conferred on U.S. Virgin Islanders in 1927. [DOI political status](https://www.doi.gov/oia/islands/virgin-islands)
- **Federal representation / presidency:** One elected nonvoting House Delegate, committee participation under House rules, no ordinary final House floor vote; no presidential electors in general presidential elections. [48 U.S.C. §1711](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&f=treesort&fq=true&granuleId=USC-prelim-title48-section1711&hl=true&num=20080&req=10+USC); [House directory](https://www.house.gov/representatives)
- **Census geography:** State-equivalent, islands as county-equivalents and towns as place-equivalents for specified Census products. [Census hierarchy](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/economic-census/guidance-geographies/levels.html)
- **Status preference evidence:** The reviewed official materials document repeated constitutional conventions and referenda on whether to adopt proposed territorial constitutions. Those ballots are not statehood-choice ballots. In 1981, a proposed constitution approved by Congress was rejected by Virgin Islands voters; this is evidence about that constitutional text, not a general status preference. No contemporary statehood-preference baseline was established in this source pass. Unknown; no inference from constitutional convention work. [DOI political status](https://www.doi.gov/oia/islands/virgin-islands); [2025 Sixth Convention extension](https://www.vi.gov/governor-bryan-acts-on-bills-passed-by-the-36th-legislature-in-its-october-30-2025-session/); [DOJ record of 1981 proposed-constitution referendum rejection](https://www.justice.gov/olc/opinion/department-justice-views-constitution-adopted-constitutional-convention-virgin-islands)

## District of Columbia note (for the same statehood agenda)

D.C. already has presidential electors under the Twenty-Third Amendment, despite lacking a voting House representative or senators. A D.C.-statehood bill would need to resolve the capital district/federal enclave and the amendment’s three-elector language; current CRS summaries discuss a reduced federal district and possible Twenty-Third Amendment repeal. D.C. should remain distinct from territorial political status: it is the federal capital district, not an insular territory. [Twenty-Third Amendment, Constitution Annotated](https://constitution.congress.gov/constitution/amendment-23/); [CRS, D.C. Statehood: Constitutional and Legal Issues](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IF/PDF/IF11443/IF11443.3.pdf)

## Research-to-game boundary

The player’s viable path can be expressed as automatic background voter registration on turning 18 when locally eligible → ordinary candidate eligibility and local or territorial political campaign → public agenda/support building through statements, audience reach and reactions → representatives and Congress consider an admission measure. Territory residents should already hold diverse opinions about statehood and other status futures; an individual character’s view can shift through persuasion and events. This is the owner’s intended player-facing model, not enacted law or a fixed guaranteed legal sequence. Local law controls who can hold each office, and territory-specific electorate/citizenship rules matter. A status campaign should present competing positions fairly and let actual local constituencies and recorded reactions shape outcomes. Do not set baseline approval percentages without a sourced, time-stamped polling/plebiscite packet.

## Known coverage gaps before turning this into game parameters

- Exact, current qualifications/terms and election calendars for every office in all four territories; especially American Samoa’s custom-selected Senate and local traditional offices.
- Current CNMI implementation of constitutionally worded voter qualifications; citizenship/national eligibility and detailed domicile period.
- Current, methodologically reliable territorywide status polling for American Samoa, Guam, CNMI and USVI. Guam’s 1982 results and CNMI’s 1975 Covenant vote are historical only; USVI constitutional-referendum results concern proposed constitutions rather than statehood.
- Precise statehood admission petition/ballot/congressional sequence and any locally required public-consent rule for each territory. Constitutional Article IV leaves important political process details to lawmaking; do not hardcode a one-size-fits-all route.
- Territory-specific presidential-party primary rules, which vary by party/year and are not Electoral College participation.
- D.C. 23rd Amendment resolution depends on final federal-district design and chosen legislative path; keep it a distinct special case.
