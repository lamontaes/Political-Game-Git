## Sourced baseline

### Governor (Commonwealth executive)

- The executive power is exercised by one Governor, elected by direct vote at each general election. Term: four years, commencing January 2 of the year after election and continuing until a successor is elected and takes office. (Puerto Rico Constitution, Art. IV §§1–2.)
- At the election date, candidate must be at least 35 and must be, and for the preceding five years have been, a U.S. citizen and bona fide citizen and resident of Puerto Rico. The Constitution does not state an age-at-filing condition here. (Art. IV §3.)
- The Governor executes laws; may call extraordinary legislative sessions; makes appointments where constitution/statute assigns them, with Senate or bicameral advice and consent as specified; commands the militia; may grant pardons/commutations within constitutional limits; and approves or vetoes legislation. The Governor appoints executive secretaries with Senate consent, except the Secretary of State also requires House consent and must meet the Governor qualifications. (Art. IV §§4–6.)
- No general term limit appears in Article IV's Governor provision. Do not infer one from a state profile.

Primary source (Spanish, official Puerto Rico Judiciary Constitution portal): [Article IV — Executive Power](https://poderjudicial.pr/constitucion/articulo-iv/).

### Legislative Assembly (Commonwealth legislature)

- Puerto Rico has a bicameral Legislative Assembly: Senate and House of Representatives; members are directly elected at each general election. The normal constitutional composition is 27 Senators and 51 Representatives. Article III §7 can add minority-party at-large members in its specified electoral circumstances, so 27/51 is the base, not an unconditional hard maximum.
- District/at-large mix: 8 senatorial districts, each electing 2 senators (16 district senators), plus 11 at-large senators; 40 representative districts, each electing 1 representative, plus 11 at-large representatives. A district senator/representative must have lived in the relevant district for at least one year before election or appointment; if a municipality contains more than one representative district, residence anywhere in that municipality satisfies the representative-district residency rule. At-large seats have no corresponding district-residency requirement in §6.
- Legislative eligibility: can read and write either Spanish or English; be both a U.S. and Puerto Rico citizen; have resided in Puerto Rico for at least two years immediately before election or appointment; be at least 30 to serve in the Senate or 25 in the House. The text makes the two-year and district residence conditions run to the election/appointment date, not filing or inauguration. (Art. III §§5–6.)
- Terms begin January 2 immediately after the general election. The Constitution's §8 excerpt specifies start date but not an express number of years; combining that provision with the Electoral Code's general election every four years yields a **derived four-year term**. Treat that duration as an inference from the election cycle, not a quoted §8 duration.
- The Assembly legislates for the Commonwealth, can create/reorganize executive departments, and laws passed by both chambers go to the Governor for signature or veto; overrides require two-thirds of the total membership of each chamber. (Art. III §§16–20.)

Primary source (Spanish, official Puerto Rico Judiciary Constitution portal): [Article III — Legislative Power](https://poderjudicial.pr/constitucion/articulo-iii/). Election-cycle source: [Puerto Rico Electoral Code (Ley 58-2020), official CEE PDF](https://ww2.ceepur.org/sites/ComisionEE/es-pr/Presidencia/DocumentosPresidencia/Ley%2058-2020%20-%20C%C3%B3digo%20Electoral.pdf), Article 9.1; see also the [CEE 2024 General Election Proclamation](https://www.ceepur.org/EleccionesGenerales2024/docs/2024-CEE%20Proclama%20Elecciones%20Generales.pdf).

### Federal representation: Resident Commissioner and distinctions

- Puerto Rico's qualified electors choose one Resident Commissioner at each general election. Federal law sets a four-year term beginning January 3 after that election—one year longer than the two-year U.S. House cycle. (48 U.S.C. §891.)
- Federal statutory eligibility language requires bona fide U.S. citizenship, age 25 or older, and English reading and writing. The codified provision's archaic negative phrasing is awkward; this statement follows the conventional reading of its stated qualifications. It does not specify a federal district-residency condition. (48 U.S.C. §892.)
- The Resident Commissioner represents the whole island in the House, not a voting House district. The office is distinct from state Representatives and from the territorial delegates elected by other territories. House Clerk says the Resident Commissioner and delegates may speak, introduce bills, offer amendments, and serve on committees, but may not vote on final passage or while the House sits as Committee of the Whole; current House FAQ also distinguishes the four-year Resident Commissioner term from two-year Delegate/Representative terms.
- Puerto Rico has no U.S. Senators: Article I of the U.S. Constitution allocates Senate seats to each State, and Puerto Rico is not a state. Puerto Rico residents also do not participate in the Electoral College presidential general election while domiciled on the island. A local presidential preference/party-primary process, if separately represented by game rules, must not be confused with casting electoral votes.

Official federal sources: [48 U.S.C. §891](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title48-section891), [48 U.S.C. §892](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A48+section%3A892+edition%3Aprelim%29), [House Clerk Member FAQs](https://clerk.house.gov/Help/ViewMemberFAQs), [House.gov — The House Explained](https://www.house.gov/the-house-explained), [U.S. Senate Constitution text](https://www.senate.gov/civics/constitution_item/constitution.htm), and [National Archives Electoral College FAQ](https://www.archives.gov/electoral-college/faq).

### Municipios (local general-purpose governments)

- Puerto Rico has **78 municipios**. They cover the Commonwealth's whole territory and are local governments composed of an executive branch and legislative branch. Each has an elected Mayor (Alcalde) and an elected Municipal Legislature. The municipal code defines the municipality as a legal government entity subordinate to Puerto Rico's Constitution and laws, with separate legal capacity and legislative, administrative, and fiscal capacity in municipal matters. Municipal autonomy operates subject to the Commonwealth Assembly's constitutional authority over the municipal regime and function. (Municipal Code, Arts. 1.006–1.008.)
- Mayor: direct vote by qualified electors of the municipio at each general election; four-year term beginning the second Monday of January after the general election, continuing until successor takes office. (Art. 1.012.)
- Mayor eligibility is explicitly assessed **at taking office**, with one look-back keyed to election: age 21+, literacy, U.S. citizenship, domicile in municipality for at least one year before election, qualified elector of that municipality, no felony or misdemeanor involving moral turpitude conviction, not removed from public employment for performance-related causes, and not declared mentally incapacitated by a competent court. (Art. 1.011.)
- Municipal Legislature: elected by direct vote of municipio electors at each general election for four years, beginning the second Monday of January in the following year; duties continue until the second Monday of January after the next general election. This is a municipio-wide contest, not a ward-based council seat structure in the cited general rule. The current consolidated Article 1.022 provides for direct-election seats plus minority-party seats: for ordinary 16/14/12-member bodies, parties may nominate 13/11/9 candidates; the top vote-getters fill those direct seats and three additional seats are allocated among the second- and third-place parties (or all three to the second-place party if only two parties are on the ballot). San Juan and Culebra have special counts and nomination numbers. This election mechanism is more specific than saying simply “elected.” (Art. 1.022.)
- Legislature size is a statutory population rule using the last decennial census: 16 members for population 40,000+; 14 for 20,000–39,999; 12 below 20,000; special counts are 17 for San Juan and 5 for Culebra. (Art. 1.020.)
- Municipal legislator qualification, generally due at taking office: literacy; domicile and qualified-elector status in the municipio; U.S. citizenship; municipality domicile for six months before filing candidacy; no felony or misdemeanor involving moral turpitude conviction; not removed from public office/employment for misconduct; not judicially declared mentally incapacitated; age 18+. That six-month residence clock is tied to **candidacy filing**, while the other qualifications are stated at taking office. (Art. 1.021.) The code's qualification rules include criminal/competency conditions that ordinary character data may not know; missing data should remain unknown in a game model.
- Municipal legislative power is exercised through the elected municipal legislature, while the executive is exercised by the elected mayor. The Code gives municipalities legal, legislative, administrative and fiscal authority over matters of municipal nature, and enumerates broad local powers (Art. 1.008 and following). This is a legislatively bounded autonomy within Puerto Rico, not sovereign/state-equivalent power. Do not treat the municipalities as U.S. counties in actual governmental jurisdiction merely because Census classifies them in a county category.

Current primary source, Spanish, official Puerto Rico Office of Management and Budget searchable compilation, revision labeled **March 11, 2026**: [Ley 107-2020, Código Municipal de Puerto Rico](https://bvirtualogp.pr.gov/ogp/Bvirtual/leyesreferencia/PDF/107-2020.pdf). The government-hosted copy is expressly “según enmendada.” Foundational text and excerpts are also hosted by [Puerto Rico Office of the Comptroller](https://casp.pr.gov/Leyes/Ley_Num_107-2020.pdf). Census count source: [2022 Community Resilience Estimates Puerto Rico file layout](https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/demo/technical-documentation/community-resilience/2022/pr/CRE_PR_2022_File_Layout.pdf), identifying 78 county-equivalent municipio entities.

### Municipal powers: what may safely be said at this level

The statute establishes municipal powers in municipal affairs and lists local governmental actions, but the general profile should not turn those powers into a fixed list of services delivered uniformly by all 78 places. Code Arts. 1.006–1.010 and the relevant later subject-matter chapters should be consulted before assigning a particular policy domain, taxing authority, or service to a game system. Census's older jurisdiction description is a useful allocation cross-check—not a current local-service guarantee—and says Commonwealth agencies provide functions including police, fire, water, sewerage, education, parks and much public health, while municipio functions notably included streets/plazas, refuse collection, cemeteries, markets and public welfare.

Sources: [Municipal Code (Arts. 1.006–1.010)](https://bvirtualogp.pr.gov/ogp/Bvirtual/leyesreferencia/PDF/107-2020.pdf) (Spanish; see article numbering); [Census Bureau, Geographic Areas Reference Manual, Puerto Rico chapter](https://www2.census.gov/geo/pdfs/reference/GARM/Ch7GARM.pdf) (English; this is a Census classification/geographic reference, not current municipal-law certification); [Census Bureau, 1982 Census of Governments Puerto Rico description](https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/cog/tables/1982/public-employment/1982-vol6-no2-fin-puerto-rico-vi-%26-guam.pdf) (historical service description only).

## Census treatment

**Yes.** The Census Government Units Survey / Census of Governments includes Puerto Rico municipio governments as general-purpose governments in the county-government class for its government-count framework; they are not subcounty “municipal governments” in the ordinary Census table classification, and Puerto Rico pueblos/ciudades are not separate governments. The 2022 Census of Governments data are the current five-yearly organization count; the Census Bureau's Census geography reference explicitly states the 78 municipios are functioning governments, the only general-purpose local governments in Puerto Rico, and statistical equivalents of counties. This is a classification answer, not a claim that municipios legally are counties or follow state-county law.

Sources: [2022 Census of Governments Organization Tables](https://www.census.gov/data/tables/2022/econ/gus/2022-governments.html), including Table 2 “Local Governments by Type and State”; [Census Bureau, Geographic Areas Reference Manual, Puerto Rico chapter](https://www2.census.gov/geo/pdfs/reference/GARM/Ch7GARM.pdf); [Census Bureau's 1982 Puerto Rico description](https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/cog/tables/1982/public-employment/1982-vol6-no2-fin-puerto-rico-vi-%26-guam.pdf) explicitly says municipio governments are counted as county governments in government statistics; current [Census Economic Census geography levels](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/economic-census/geographies/levels/2022-levels.html) labels municipios county equivalents. The COG narrative/profile evidence is more directly about governments; the latter is geographic tabulation classification.

## Evidence, inference, and possible game profile

**Official law / source facts:** institutional bodies; elections and direct/qualified-elector selection; stated seat counts and seat mix; Governor and municipal office terms and start dates; age, citizenship, residence, literacy, and disqualification requirements; the Resident Commissioner federal term and statutory requirements; municipal code's legal framing; Census classification.

**Derived inference:** Legislative seats have a four-year term because their term begins immediately after a general election held every four years, although Article III §8 states the start date rather than a numeric duration. The Resident Commissioner is at least 25 and must meet English literacy because that is the conventional construction of 48 U.S.C. §892's archaic negative phrasing. These are supported inferences and should be kept distinguishable from verbatim statutory field values.

**Possible game profile direction (not approved):** create an explicit US-PR government profile rather than reuse a U.S.-state range. Model the Commonwealth Governor, Senate, House, Resident Commissioner, and municipality mayor/legislature as separate offices/tiers. Store district versus at-large seats as distinct seat types; use the exact different assessment dates and residency clocks above. A common qualified-elector concept should be explicit: Electoral Code §5.3 requires U.S. citizenship, Puerto Rico domicile, age 18 by voting date, active qualified registration, and no court declaration of mental incapacity. Local offices add their own residency, age, literacy, criminal-history/removal requirements. Do not grant qualifications from facts the simulation does not record.

This is a design suggestion inferred from the request's need for a playable authored profile, not a decision about mechanics, accepted coefficients, institutional powers, or current game behavior.

## Remaining questions / limits

1. The Resident Commissioner eligibility statute uses awkward “who is not ... and who is not more than twenty-five ... and who does not ...” phrasing in the official codification. The expected interpretation is age 25 or older and English literacy, but a lawyer/official election guide should verify the grammar before encoding precise booleans.
2. Article III §7 can expand chamber membership for minority representation; implement only if the game has a supported rule/mechanism for that conditional allocation. The 27/51 counts are constitutional baseline composition, not always final chamber count.
3. Current Census Organization Table 2 links the Puerto Rico/local-government classification framework, but the rendered Census page does not expose the downloadable table cells in this handoff. The classification evidence from Census's Puerto Rico reference and government-count publication is direct; verify the exact row/cell and the current 2022/2025 municipio unit listing if the project needs a counted row ID or unit-level code.
4. Puerto Rico's municipal code has had amendments after enactment; use the government-hosted March 2026 consolidation, not the original 2020 Act, when extracting exact current municipal provisions. Municipal rules may be amended individually by later acts; this profile did not audit every 2026 session law or municipality charter/ordinance.
5. This did not inventory every Commonwealth constitutional/statutory office (e.g., Auditor, Ombudsman, judges, cabinet posts), each municipality's special local rules or election disputes, internal assembly rules, party primaries/independent candidacies, full authority boundaries for every policy area, or district boundary datasets. The scope is the assignment's government-form and core candidacy baseline.
6. Municipal autonomy's practical service/policy scope requires checking the Code chapter for each proposed authority and any applicable Commonwealth/federal preemption. The historical Census list of services is not a current guarantee.
7. This research says nothing about whether current game code can execute these offices, places, elections, or terms. There were no code edits or tests.
