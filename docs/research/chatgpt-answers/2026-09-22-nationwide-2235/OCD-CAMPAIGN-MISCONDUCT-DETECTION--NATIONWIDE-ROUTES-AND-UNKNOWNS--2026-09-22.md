# Campaign-fund personal-use detection routes — nationwide research note

Prepared 2026-09-22 for `campaign-misconduct-detection-routes`. Research only; no repository changes.

## Scope and conclusion

The available public record supports a **route map and procedural sequence**, but not defensible nationwide percentages for how personal-use matters are discovered, typical route-specific discovery delays, or the share that name a candidate directly versus conceal the benefit through an intermediary. No official FEC series located classifies closed matters by these discovery routes and reports those route shares. State public records are even less standardized. Do not turn the existing authored timing or probability values into “real” rates.

Federal law offers multiple entry paths: public disclosure and outside scrutiny; a sworn complaint from any person; Reports Analysis Division (RAD) review/referral; Audit Division work/referral; and other government referrals. The FEC’s public description says it reviews complaints case by case, and its statutory enforcement matters stay confidential until resolved. The public case record is therefore a selected and delayed view, not a census of detected or undetected misuse.

The game can model these routes as distinct opportunities driven by their own evidence and access. It should not use a single automatic discovery chance or imply that an allegation, a filed complaint, a regulator’s preliminary threshold, and a final violation finding are equivalent.

## What current federal sources establish

### 1. Reports can expose a payment, but do not prove personal use

Federal committees must report disbursements. Itemized reporting generally identifies the payee, address, date, amount and purpose when the itemization rules apply; ordinary disbursements are itemized after payments to the same payee exceed $200 in an election cycle. Filing cadence varies by committee and election context. House and Senate candidate committees generally file quarterly plus applicable pre-election reports; presidential committees may report monthly or quarterly. Thus a payment can become publicly visible only when the applicable report is filed and processed, and smaller or aggregated transactions may not appear as a separate itemized line.

A named candidate payee is possible and can be conspicuous, but the reporting rules do not require every personal benefit to appear under the candidate’s name. A vendor, payroll processor, family member, or other recipient may be the reported payee; records or investigation may be needed to establish ultimate recipient and purpose. The FEC specifically describes payroll reporting that identifies both the payroll company and ultimate recipients in a relevant setting. No located FEC source gives a national share of personal-use cases with direct candidate payee versus intermediary.

Sources: [FEC, Making disbursements](https://www.fec.gov/help-candidates-and-committees/making-disbursements/); [FEC, 2026 reporting dates](https://www.fec.gov/help-candidates-and-committees/dates-and-deadlines/2026-reporting-dates/); [FEC, Candidate salary payments and reporting](https://www.fec.gov/help-candidates-and-committees/filing-reports/candidate-salary-payments/).

### 2. Outside complainants and public-record scrutiny

Anyone may submit an FEC complaint. The FEC asks for specific facts and supporting records and asks filers to distinguish personal knowledge from information and belief; legal citations are not required. A rival, watchdog organization, journalist, donor, treasurer, staffer or member of the public can therefore be a complainant if they have a factual basis. The law does not create a special “opponent” route or require a complainant to be personally harmed.

A public filing can serve as a lead for a reporter, rival, watchdog, or researcher. It may also point to a mismatch that leads someone to seek invoices, photographs, corporate records, interviews, or insider material. Public disclosure alone is not a finding. A plausible-looking vendor payment may be legitimate; a vague purpose or a payment linked to personal circumstances may support a question but does not by itself establish prohibited personal use.

The enforcement matter is confidential while pending. The eventual public case file appears after the close vote takes effect, generally 30 days after certification. Consequently, a player/world outside the regulator cannot ordinarily know that a formal FEC matter exists merely because one has been opened.

Sources: [FEC, How to file a complaint](https://www.fec.gov/legal-resources/enforcement/complaints-process/how-to-file-complaint-with-fec/); [FEC, Complaints process](https://www.fec.gov/legal-resources/enforcement/complaints-process/).

### 3. FEC Reports Analysis Division (RAD)

RAD monitors filings and reviews report contents for compliance. It can ask for more information, clarification or correction. Depending on internal, cycle-specific referral thresholds, it may refer an apparent violation to the Office of General Counsel or Alternative Dispute Resolution Office. A RAD referral is a compliance-screening path that can begin without an outside complainant; it is not proof that funds were used personally.

The FEC publishes cycle-specific Review and Referral Procedures and thresholds. They concern selection/review and apparent reporting noncompliance, so they should not be generalized as a guaranteed transaction-level personal-use audit. The official sources do not provide a universal interval from transaction to RAD discovery or a share of personal-use matters originating there.

Sources: [FEC, Compliance offices (Audit Division and report review)](https://www.fec.gov/about/leadership-and-structure/fec-offices/compliance-offices/); [FEC, RAD review and referral procedures](https://www.fec.gov/updates/tip2019-rad-review-and-referral-procedures/); [FEC, Guidebook for Complainants and Respondents](https://www.fec.gov/em/respondent_guide.pdf).

### 4. Audit Division

The FEC says it may audit any political committee required to file reports. Before an audit under that authority, the Commission internally reviews reports to determine whether the committee meets substantial-compliance thresholds. Publicly funded presidential campaigns have required audits. Audit staff can identify a potential personal-use payment during a transaction review; the audit report may then recommend findings or further handling. The Raul Ruiz for Congress interim audit report, for example, documents audit staff identifying disbursements as potential personal use—an example of the route, not a frequency estimate.

This is an agency audit route, distinct from an outside independent accountant hired by a campaign. No national official statistic located reports how often personal-use matters arise from audits or the time from spending to discovery. The campaign-misconduct question’s “outside accountant or auditor” should therefore be treated as a possible witness/control failure route, not conflated with the FEC Audit Division.

Sources: [FEC, Compliance offices](https://www.fec.gov/about/leadership-and-structure/fec-offices/compliance-offices/); [FEC, Audit reports search](https://www.fec.gov/legal-resources/enforcement/audit-search/); [example FEC interim audit report, Raul Ruiz for Congress](https://www.fec.gov/resources/cms-content/documents/DrRaulRuiz_IAR_2018.pdf).

### 5. Referral to prosecutors

The FEC has exclusive civil jurisdiction over federal campaign-finance law. Criminal violations are a separate lane: DOJ’s election-offense guidance describes knowing-and-willful intent requirements for criminal FECA violations. The FEC can refer matters to DOJ; the prosecutor does not simply inherit every questionable transaction or civil complaint. A criminal investigation/prosecution threshold must not be used as the normal standard for an FEC civil case.

For state and local campaigns, prosecutorial jurisdiction and referral arrangements vary by state and offense. Do not presume that the federal route applies.

Sources: [FEC, Enforcement](https://www.fec.gov/legal-resources/enforcement/); [DOJ, Federal Prosecution of Election Offenses](https://www.justice.gov/criminal/criminal-pin/file/1029066/dl).

## Federal procedural sequence and evidentiary distinctions

A useful federal sequence, with branch points:

1. **Transaction occurs.** The campaign keeps underlying records; personal use is assessed under the federal “irrespective test” and other specific rules. A suspicious-looking expense is not automatically unlawful.
2. **Disclosure or private evidence becomes available.** Filed reports surface reportable payee/purpose/amount details according to reporting rules. Other evidence may come from invoices, bank or card records, vendor/subcontractor records, photographs, communications, a treasurer or staffer, or an accountant.
3. **A lead is noticed.** Public-record analysis, a rival’s review, press reporting, a whistleblower/insider, RAD report review, an FEC audit, or another agency can surface it. A lead may prompt more research and need not result in a complaint.
4. **Complaint or agency referral.** An outside person can file a sworn complaint with factual support. RAD/Audit or another public authority may refer apparent violations. These are different origins, but all may produce an FEC enforcement matter.
5. **Initial legal screen / “reason to believe.”** The FEC evaluates the complaint, response and available information. A reason-to-believe (RTB) vote permits investigation; it is a preliminary threshold, not a final finding. A no-reason-to-believe determination can close the matter.
6. **Investigation and possible conciliation.** The FEC may investigate; the public process page lists a 15-day respondent response period, then discretionary and variable investigation stages. Pre-probable-cause conciliation may run up to 60 days under the published process.
7. **“Probable cause to believe” and conciliation or closure.** This is a later, higher administrative threshold, still not a court judgment. If probable cause is found, the General Counsel attempts conciliation for at least 30 days; a civil penalty may be part of an approved agreement.
8. **Civil litigation / criminal referral where applicable.** Unresolved civil matters may proceed to federal court. Criminal conduct requires a separate DOJ decision and criminal proof standard. Public case materials are generally disclosed after resolution, subject to redactions and statutory rules.

FEC’s process page provides milestone durations where fixed (complaint notice: 5 days; response: 15 days; pre-probable-cause conciliation: 60 days; post-probable-cause conciliation: 30–90 days), but it leaves RTB and investigation duration blank. These are procedural stages after receipt, not typical time from the payment to discovery or resolution. The FEC says case-by-case review; no single case clock can stand in for a typical route-specific delay.

Sources: [FEC, How to file a complaint (process and timelines)](https://www.fec.gov/legal-resources/enforcement/complaints-process/how-to-file-complaint-with-fec/); [FEC, statutory and civil enforcement overview](https://www.fec.gov/legal-resources/enforcement/); [FEC, Personal use](https://www.fec.gov/help-candidates-and-committees/making-disbursements/personal-use/).

## State and local campaigns: nationwide scope, explicit unknowns

The legal rule, reporting cadence, itemization threshold, disclosure portal, audit authority, regulator, complaint form, confidentiality, referral partner, deadlines, sanctions and local-office coverage vary by jurisdiction. Some states place campaign-finance work in an elections office; others use an ethics or campaign-finance commission, and some divide responsibilities. Local candidates may follow state rules, local rules, or a split arrangement.

The repository contains `docs/research/92M-campaign-finance-ethics-lobbying.json` and [this 52-jurisdiction regulator source map](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1ZBWrR2STXFYIMNFRjJW0xaJ6OqfL04LM/view), a broad state/federal agency source map with regulator names and source material. It is useful for discovering the likely agency, but its presence does **not** answer this question’s narrower requirements: verified personal-use investigative authority; self-initiated report review; procedural clocks; complainant/evidence standards; personal-use penalty range; and route-specific incidence or delay. Do not treat the regulator-name list as a completed detection map. In this pass, exact detection procedures were not verified across all 50 states, D.C. and Puerto Rico. Those jurisdiction-specific details are **unknown here**, not assumed to match the FEC or neighboring states.

There is no federal “state campaign” case-origin dataset that can fill those gaps. Therefore:

- no nationwide state route percentages are supportable from sources reviewed;
- no universal state delay from spending to discovery is supportable;
- no uniform state direct-payee/intermediary pattern is supportable;
- no single 30-day report interval, review cycle, or penalty range should be applied to all states;
- the 52-jurisdiction source map is a starting index, not evidence that all 52 enforcement rows are verified.

## Design implications, clearly separated from law

These are possible game-model choices, not empirical rates or legal requirements:

- Keep route events separate: public report becomes available; a person with access notices; a journalist investigates; a rival chooses to complain; an insider shares documents or files; regulator screening flags an anomaly; an audit samples transactions; an agency/prosecutor referral occurs.
- Gate each route on information access and evidence. For example, public reporting can reveal payee, date, amount and stated purpose; an insider may provide receipts or context; an outside auditor may see books but has no inherent enforcement authority; the regulator can request records only through its legal process.
- Represent “lead,” “allegation,” “complaint filed,” “RTB,” investigation, probable cause, settlement/finding and criminal prosecution as distinct states. A complaint alone should not reveal guilt or create a public finding.
- Use jurisdiction-profile inputs for report cadence, itemization threshold, who has jurisdiction, own-initiative review, complaint procedure, confidentiality and sanctions. Where facts are not researched, mark the mechanic uncalibrated/unknown rather than silently importing FEC rules.
- For timing, start with the actual report schedule and procedural deadlines for the selected jurisdiction. Delays from spend to media notice, insider disclosure, RAD/audit detection, and final disposition remain unmeasured; any game ranges need to be labeled design assumptions.
- A player who is a candidate can have disclosed transactions observed through the relevant public portal; the discoverer should still need a reason to inspect or an alert. This produces different outcomes for high-profile, well-covered campaigns and obscure local races without pretending one detection probability fits all.

## What remains unanswered / owner decisions

1. Commission or accept a purpose-built 52-jurisdiction legal-source pass for the detection fields listed above. The existing 92M agency map is a lead list only.
2. Decide whether the simulation needs route incidence calibration at all. Public sources reviewed do not provide a denominator or usable route-share table. If desired, a bounded study of a coded sample of closed FEC personal-use matters could measure **publicly documented origin** only, with an explicit sample, date range, coding rules and missing-data category. It would not reveal undetected acts or all state cases.
3. Decide whether to model independent campaign accountants as routine controls or as uncommon access holders; official sources do not establish how often they detect/report personal-use spending.
4. If reproducing federal procedure, use jurisdictionally scoped legal thresholds. Do not display the factual labels “reason to believe” or “probable cause” for an ordinary state process unless that state actually uses those terms.
