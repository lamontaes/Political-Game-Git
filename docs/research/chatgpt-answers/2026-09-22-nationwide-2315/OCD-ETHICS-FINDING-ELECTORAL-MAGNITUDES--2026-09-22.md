# Public ethics findings do not have a supported universal vote penalty

The available election research supports an electoral association between some public corruption scandals and lower incumbent vote share, but it does not estimate the effect of an official ethics finding against a candidate. No reliable universal point loss or later-race half-life is established. Keep the current 300-basis-point, 150-basis-point, and six-year effects marked unverified; do not present them as researched magnitudes. The 24-jurisdiction scan confirms the ethics-routing bodies, but it does not establish personal-use campaign-finance penalties for those bodies. Federal law does provide a penalty framework.

## What the evidence measures

Peters and Welch’s study of U.S. House contests from 1968–1978 reports that candidates accused of corruption lost an estimated 6–11 percentage points relative to expected vote, with results varying by type of charge. Most accused candidates were still reelected. The paper studies charges and alleged or convicted corruption, not official ethics findings as a distinct treatment. The result is an adjusted association from an observational model, not a randomized or otherwise cleanly identified causal effect of a substantiated finding. ([Peters & Welch 1980, *American Political Science Review*](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055400168303); [abstract and reported estimate](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/apsrev/v74y1980i03p697-708_16.html))

Welch and Hibbing’s later study asks whether the 1982–1990 congressional campaign context changed the effect of corruption charges. Its abstract recaps the earlier 6–11-point range but does not establish a universal effect for a formal finding, settlement, or substantiated report. ([Welch & Hibbing 1997, *Journal of Politics*](https://doi.org/10.2307/2998224); [Penn State research record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/the-effects-of-charges-of-corruption-on-voting-behavior-in-congre-2))

The persistence literature conflicts in ways that matter for a game rule. Praino, Stockemer, and Moscardelli report that scandal involvement has an immediate negative effect on U.S. congressional incumbents’ reelection margins and can linger into later cycles. Pereira and Waterbury’s later analysis of congressional scandals finds that only election-year scandals systematically affect incumbent vote share; moral scandals are an exception, with effects over the term. Neither abstract provides a generalizable numeric half-life for an official ethics finding. They study scandal involvement, with differing samples, periods, and models. ([Praino et al. 2013, *Social Science Quarterly*](https://doi.org/10.1111/ssqu.12046); [Pereira & Waterbury 2019, *Political Research Quarterly*](https://doi.org/10.1177/1065912918795059))

These findings should not be collapsed into “a finding costs 3 points.” Allegation, investigation, evidence-supported report, formal violation finding, negotiated settlement, and criminal conviction are different information and legal states. Existing studies generally code scandals or charges, not those official dispositions separately. Candidate role, scandal type, timing, media attention, party, challenger quality, prior margin, and the electoral environment also vary. Evidence of an association does not identify the causal impact of an official finding on an otherwise identical candidate.

## Recommendation for the game

There is no supported universal numeric estimate for (1) an official adverse ethics finding during the current race or (2) persistence into a later race. A 6–11-point figure can be reported only as a historical observational estimate for broadly defined corruption charges in congressional elections, not imported as the loss from a substantiated finding. The literature supports recency and scandal-type sensitivity as design considerations, while not supporting a single six-year window or half-life.

Accordingly, the values currently described in the queue—300 basis points for a finding or conciliation, 150 for a supported report, and a smaller penalty for races filed within six years—remain unverified placeholders. The research does not validate their magnitude or their ordering. Do not replace them with a single literature number. A game rule should stay explicitly labeled as a design choice until the owner selects one or a more directly matched study is found. Repayment of the amount personally spent is a separate grounded remedy and must not be conflated with an electoral effect or civil fine.

## Federal Election Commission

Federal law prohibits conversion of campaign funds to personal use under [52 U.S.C. § 30114(b)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=(title:52%20section:30114%20edition:prelim)). Civil penalties are enforced under [52 U.S.C. § 30109(a)(5)–(6)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=(title:52%20section:30109%20edition:prelim)) and [11 C.F.R. § 111.24](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-11/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-111/subpart-A/section-111.24). Under the current 11 C.F.R. § 111.24(a), a civil penalty for a non-knowing-and-willful violation may not exceed the greater of $24,885 or the amount involved. For a knowing-and-willful violation, the cap is the greater of $53,088 or 200% of the amount involved. These are upper limits; the law does not set one fixed personal-use fine per payment, and the case facts and disposition matter. A negotiated conciliation may impose an individual amount, and a separate repayment or disgorgement of the converted funds is not itself that civil penalty. The FEC’s official [personal-use guidance](https://www.fec.gov/help-candidates-and-committees/making-disbursements/personal-use/) states the prohibition and the “irrespective” test.
