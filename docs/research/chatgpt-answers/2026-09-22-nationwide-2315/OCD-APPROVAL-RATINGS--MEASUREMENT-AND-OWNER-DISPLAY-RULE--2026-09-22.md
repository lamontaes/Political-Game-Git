# How approval of officeholders is measured

**Owner decision (September 22, 2026):** Show a numeric approval percentage to the player only when an in-world poll actually measured it. Where no poll exists, people may still hold opinions and act on them; convey those through recorded behavior, conversations, reporting and election results without claiming a precise approval rating. Keep the game’s latent public attitudes separate from an observed poll result.

Approval is a between-election judgment about how an officeholder is doing the job. The United States has long, repeatable public trend lines for the president and Congress as an institution, plus newer high-volume state-level tracking for governors and U.S. senators. Coverage becomes intermittent and local below that. For state legislators, mayors, county executives and councilmembers, a poll may exist for a particular place or contest, but this review found no comparable national approval series. Treat missing national baselines as **UNKNOWN**, not as neutral or zero.

Its reporting cadence and survey mode have changed: discrete multiday surveys through 2008, daily three-day averages in 2009–17, weekly averages in 2018, then discrete multiday surveys from 2019–25. Later samples and methods vary by release; use the individual release's survey note rather than carrying that margin forward. | Institutional approval does not identify which chamber, party, or member caused the response. Low body approval can coexist with high approval of an individual representative. |

It asks a direct governor job-approval item. ## Measurement rules supported by the sources

- Keep approval distinct from favorability. Gallup explicitly describes job approval as an evaluation of performance in office, while favorability asks about the person. The two can diverge.
- Preserve the question, response scale, respondent population, field dates, survey mode, sample size, weighting and uncertainty with every rating. The familiar approve/disapprove wording supports trend comparison only when its population and method are sufficiently comparable.
- Report “don't know/no opinion” separately when the instrument does. Do not convert it to disapproval or silently remove it.
- Treat election results as election results. Gallup described approval as an attempted between-election measure; its historical analysis found correlation with incumbent-party vote share in a limited set of presidential elections, not identity between the measures. Elections are infrequent, candidate-centered choices and should not be stored or presented as poll ratings.
- For small-area/member estimates, state uncertainty and suppress or mark UNKNOWN when the sample cannot support a useful estimate. A national large sample does not imply a large sample for each state, district or locality.

## Historical patterns and possible drivers

Only a few patterns are safe to carry forward as documented historical associations, not deterministic rules:

- Gallup's 1938-to-present presidential series provides the longest baseline. Gallup's 1998 retrospective put the yearly presidential average since Eisenhower at 56.1%; Clinton's 1998 average was 63.8%. The same report compared contemporaneous measures and concluded that positive views of the economy helped explain Clinton's high job approval during impeachment, while personal honesty/favorability moved differently. This is one case, not a universal coefficient.
- Gallup documents “rally-around-the-flag” spikes after some major national-security events, including the Gulf War and September 11, 2001. Its own retrospective warns that these rallies are hard to define and have become less common. A crisis should therefore permit, not guarantee, an approval movement.
- Gallup's 2009 comparison found no systematic relationship between the Dow and presidential approval across presidents and periods. Economic perceptions may matter, but a market index should not be used as a direct deterministic approval input.
- Gallup's Congress series spans since 1974 and can show wide movement. Its historical article notes that Congress's aggregate approval and individual member approval differ; body-level disapproval does not imply each incumbent faces equivalent local disapproval.

These examples justify a noisy, time-varying public signal. They do not establish universal effect sizes for policy, scandal, economic shocks, party, or crisis, nor provide an empirically grounded numerical formula for state and local officials.

## Implication for the game

A defensible first shape is a private-to-the-simulation public estimate attached to the office and jurisdiction: approve, disapprove and unsure shares, with an uncertainty/recency marker and a source class. For the president and Congress, long-run national trends can inform calibration; for governors and U.S. senators, state polling offers a model with explicit pooling; for less commonly polled offices, the game should show an uncertain or unavailable estimate until its fictional world has generated one. Let approval respond probabilistically to recorded events and perceived performance, with stronger or weaker effects conditional on context. Keep election outcomes, personal favorability, and institutional approval as separate records. No evidence here supports a single national approval starting value for every governor, legislator, mayor, county executive or councilmember.

**Limitations.** This is a measurement survey, not a complete census of every poll published by every local outlet. It establishes durable and recurring national/state measurement patterns and explicitly leaves national state/local officeholder estimates UNKNOWN. Polling organizations revise methods; any number used in implementation needs its own vintage and poll-specific documentation. The sources do not provide validated gameplay effect sizes.
