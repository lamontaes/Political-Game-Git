# Statehood: what happens between an admission bill and a functioning state

**Research date:** 2026-09-22. **Status:** factual precedent plus game design options. The owner has approved the *possibility* of statehood for inhabited territories and D.C., ordinary campaigning, and individual, changeable resident opinions that can include uncertainty. The owner wants key transition decisions playable while routine steps happen automatically. No universal admission procedure or implementation timing has been approved. This memo does not claim to settle constitutional controversy about D.C.

## Legal floor

[Article IV, §3 of the U.S. Constitution](https://constitution.congress.gov/constitution/) gives Congress power to admit new states. It does not itself require one universal referendum, election order, waiting period, or presidential proclamation. The eventual admission law must specify the relevant conditions and dates. Each state has two senators under Article I and the Seventeenth Amendment; House representation depends on apportionment and any transition provision. The [National Archives explains](https://www.archives.gov/electoral-college/allocation) that a state’s presidential electors equal its senators plus representatives. The federal district has a separate Twenty-Third Amendment rule, so D.C. needs distinct handling rather than the island-territory profile.

## Two enacted precedents; neither is a universal template

- [Alaska Admission Act, Pub. L. 85-508 (1958)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-72/pdf/STATUTE-72-Pg339.pdf): Congress accepted Alaska’s already voter-adopted state constitution; the act required elections for state officials including two senators and one representative, specified ballot propositions and certification, then made admission effective on the President’s proclamation after the required returns. Territorial officials and the delegate continued until admission; newly elected state officials took authority upon admission under the state constitution and law. [National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/education/lessons/alaska/statehood.html) records the January 1959 proclamation.
- [Hawaii Admission Act, Pub. L. 86-3 (1959)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-73/pdf/STATUTE-73-Pg4.pdf): Congress accepted Hawaii’s constitution, required election of state officials and a vote on specified propositions, and used a presidential proclamation after certified results as the admission trigger. Territorial officials and the delegate served until admission, then the elected state officials exercised their functions. The act temporarily granted one House representative until the next reapportionment; [House History](https://history.house.gov/Institution/Apportionment/Adding-Voting-Members/) notes that Alaska and Hawaii temporarily increased House membership to 437. This illustrates that initial House-seat allocation can be handled in the admission act rather than assumed from a generic formula on the day of admission.

These examples show a playable transition: campaigning for admission, enacted conditions, elections and certified returns, an effective date, and continuity of office. They do **not** prove every future admission must have those exact propositions, that a status plebiscite is always legally required, or that current territorial voter preferences match 1950s Alaska or Hawaii.

## Proposed game route for owner review

1. **Public argument:** locally situated people hold status preferences and reasons. Candidates and organizations argue for statehood or another future through ordinary speech, media, organizing and elections; reach affects who hears them, and support comes from individual responses, not an automatic fame bonus.
2. **Political consent and proposal:** a local legislature, popular ballot, or another lawful channel may express support where the jurisdiction’s own law and the proposed federal act require or permit it. This is political evidence, not a universal Article IV condition.
3. **Congressional action:** Congress debates and enacts a specific admission measure, with its conditions, date rule, transition offices, federal representation and fiscal/legal arrangements. A delegate or Resident Commissioner can advocate within their real powers but cannot cast an ordinary final House floor vote.
4. **Transition:** hold only the elections and votes that the enacted measure calls for, certify them under its procedure, continue existing officials until the legal change, and seat new state officials on the effective date. Preserve prior territory/D.C. history and identities; avoid instantly replacing everyone in the world.
5. **After admission:** state authority, two Senate seats, House representation under the act and later reapportionment, and presidential electors prospectively follow the new legal status. Existing territorial institutions may continue, convert, or end only as the admission measure and new state constitution direct. D.C. separately must address the reduced federal district and Twenty-Third Amendment electors in the chosen legal route.

Steps 1–5 are a **design recommendation** tied to real precedent, not an approved implementation or claim of one mandatory constitutional path. The owner approved the presentation depth: key political choices are playable; routine administration is automatic. The specific choices and admission-law mechanics still require design and jurisdiction evidence.

## Important open facts

- Current law and political preferences in each inhabited territory; dated Guam and CNMI votes cannot seed present-day support without a time/status model.
- An authored admission measure for each potential jurisdiction: referendum requirements, transition elections, initial House allocation, offices, lands/debt, effective-date trigger and continuity provisions. Those are consequential legislative choices, not fixed values for all territories.
- D.C.’s federal-district boundary and Twenty-Third Amendment resolution. The constitutional text requires a district for the seat of government and grants it electors; modern proposals differ on how to handle the residual district and repeal. Do not silently treat D.C. exactly like Puerto Rico or Guam.
