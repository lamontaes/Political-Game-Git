# National data can anchor an adult year, but not a person's event calendar

**Lead.** Official national sources describe employment status, annual residence changes, births and marriages, civic activity, and typical daily time use. They support broad background and recurring-setting choices. They do not reveal how many invitations, family requests, workplace disputes, appointments, or other events one particular adult experiences in a year. Keep those event frequencies **UNKNOWN** unless the game explicitly labels them as design calibration.

## Usable national anchors

| Domain | Official measure and reference | Denominator / coverage | Bounded use in play |
|---|---|---|---|
| Work status | BLS Current Population Survey annual averages for 2025: 62.4% labor-force participation, 59.7% employed, and 4.3% unemployment. Latest 2025 table is published. [BLS Table 57](https://www.bls.gov/cps/cpsaat57.htm) | Civilian noninstitutional population age 16+; labor-force participation and employment-population rates use that population, while unemployment rate uses the civilian labor force. Household survey annual average, not a count of job starts, quits, or dismissals. | Use age- and household-specific rates to describe population context or aggregate cohorts. Do not give an individual a 4.3% yearly job-loss chance; the unemployment rate is not job separation risk. Job changes and workplace conflict frequencies remain **UNKNOWN**. |
| Mobility | Census ACS 1-year mobility estimates: 11.8% lived at a different residence in 2024; 8.9% moved within the same state and 2.1% moved to a different state. | Marriage | NCHS currently provides national and state trend tables through 2023. Its 2026 schedule lists “Marriage Rates in the U.S. by State, 1990–2024” as planned for November 2026, so it was not yet an available release on September 22. State reporting and provisional status constrain comparisons. A crude per-population marriage rate is not the chance that a single character marries in a year; it counts events, not unique people or first marriages. | May anchor societal-level marriage volume or geography when vintage and reporting limits are stated. Do not turn it into a character marriage roll. Individual marriage timing, partner choice, and divorce risk are **UNKNOWN**. |
| Neighbors and civic contact | Census/AmeriCorps 2023 Civic Engagement and Volunteering supplement: 54.2% of Americans age 16+ helped or exchanged favors with neighbors between September 2022 and September 2023; 28.3% formally volunteered through an organization in the prior year. The report also says about 70% reported conversation or spending time with neighbors in the past year. [Census Bureau 2023 CEV findings](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2024/11/civic-engagement-and-volunteerism.html) · [AmeriCorps 2023 CEV headline report](https://americorps.gov/sites/default/files/document/CEV2023_HeadlineReport_11052024_final_508.pdf) · [Census supplement release and scope](https://www.census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2024/civic-engagement-volunteering-supplement.html) | Roughly 47,000 survey respondents age 16+ in the September 2023 CPS supplement, weighted to national estimates. Neighbor help/contact and formal volunteering are self-reported activities over September 2022–September 2023; not invitations or a census of individual interactions. | Supports the existence of informal neighbor help and organizational volunteering as possible settings. Does not establish who initiates, how often a specific pair meets, requests for family help, social invitations, or event conversion. Those remain **UNKNOWN**. |
| Daily time use | BLS 2025 American Time Use Survey: on an average day, 30.4% of people age 15+ reported socializing/communicating as their primary activity, and 5.3% reported volunteering as a primary activity. For socializing participants, the average was 1.92 hours that day. [BLS 2025 ATUS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/atus.htm) | Nationally representative continuous survey; one randomly selected person age 15+ per household reports a single prior-day diary. Percentages measure average-day participation, not unique people or events per year. 2025 fieldwork missed September 30–November 11 during the federal shutdown; BLS weighted remaining late-year diaries to represent the quarter and says the missing period's effect cannot be quantified. | Use age/employment/household-specific tables for how time can be distributed across work, care, chores, and social activity. Do not extrapolate one diary into annual invitations or conflict rates. The 2025 shutdown is a comparability caveat. |

## What this evidence does not settle

- No source here estimates annual friend invitations, family requests for help, errands, routine appointments, work conflicts, or repeat-event spacing for an individual adult.
- Aggregate rates cannot be assigned as independent per-character random probabilities without a separate, explicit game calibration and a coherent denominator.
- Timing and causality are missing: the sources generally do not say which person initiated an encounter, whether it was welcomed, whether it repeated with the same person, or how it changed a relationship.
- “Ordinary adult” is not a single demographic. Age, work status, household, parenthood, disability, local setting, and relationships matter. Use the survey's actual subgroup if applicable; otherwise retain **UNKNOWN** instead of applying a national average to every person.
- These datasets have distinct vintages and universes. Do not merge a 2025 annual-average labor statistic, a 2024 mobility survey, 2024 final birth records, marriage tables through 2023, and 2023 retrospective civic behaviors as though they were measurements of one same-year person.

## Recommendation

Use these sources to select plausible event *settings* and validate population-level background. Build individual life-event pacing from explicit authored game rules, label it as calibration, and keep unsupported frequencies unknown. In particular, the CEV is a strong national anchor for neighbors helping neighbors; it still does not answer how often friends invite a person somewhere or relatives ask for help.

**Research checked:** September 22, 2026. No individual event-rate inference is made.
