(1) CALIBRATION TOTALS

**T1. Share of local incumbents who seek re-election**

Count incumbents whose seat is up and who are eligible; leave out term-limited members. Do not count all sitting members: that gives the mechanical 36% seen in cities with staggered terms.

- **Band:** 58–78%, center about 68%.
- **Fail:** a multi-world aggregate below 50% or above 85%.
- **By office:**
  - School boards are lowest, at 59–70%.
  - Councils and mayors are 63–76%.
  - County commissions are highest, at 70–81%, from two single-state cases only.
  - Local rates must sit below state legislators (76%) and the US House (90%).

Evidence:

- **School boards, 16 states, more than 50,000 contests, 2002–2017:** 59%. This is the source that best covers small districts. [V]
  - URL: https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai25-1213.pdf
  - Quote: "That incumbents run for reelection only 59 percent of the time is possibly the most noteworthy result in Table 3."
- **City councils, cities over 2,500 people (ICMA surveys; 7,174 governments, 18,416 observations; councils without staggered terms):** 59% (1986), 76% (1992), 75% (1996), 73% (2001). [V]
  - URL: https://faculty.ucmerced.edu/jtrounstine/Trounstine_new_machines6.pdf
  - Quote (Table 4): "1986 59% (32%) 88% (25%)" ... "2001 73% (25%) 85% (24%)"
  - Footnote 7: "Table 4 only includes cities in which council terms are not staggered. The average run rate for cities with staggered terms is 36%."
- **Large-district school boards, 2020–2025:** 62–74%, and 69% in both 2024 and 2025. [R]
  - URL: https://news.ballotpedia.org/2026/02/04/eighty-four-percent-of-school-board-incumbents-won-re-election-in-2025-the-highest-percentage-since-2022/

**T4. Share of running incumbents with no opponent (the gap between T2 and T3)**

- **Band:** 35–67%, rising as place size falls.

Evidence:

- **Washington school boards:** 67%. [R] Quote: "But 67% of those incumbents were uncontested."
- **Georgia county commissions:** 47%. [R] Quote: "For the 374 races with incumbents, 177 (47.33 percent) did not have any opposition"
- **South Carolina school boards:** 40% (45 of 112). [R]
- **Florida school boards:** about 30–50% (DERIVED from "incumbents sought reelection and were challenged in between 37 and 48 percent of potential races"). [V]
- **School boards, by seat:** 45% of single-member races and 62% of multi-member races were contested, 55% overall. [V]
  - Quote: "Overall, we find that 55 percent of school board seats are filled through contested elections, dropping to just 45 percent for single-member races."

**Consistency rule the game must satisfy:** T2 = u + (1 − u) × T3, where u is the T4 share. For example (DERIVED), u = 0.45 and T3 = 0.72 give T2 = 0.45 + 0.55 × 0.72 = 0.85, which matches the T2 center.

  - Quote (Table 3): "Overall Turnover Rate 53% 27% 15%"

**T7. Variation: check the direction and rough size, not a level**

- **Ward versus at-large:**
  - District (ward) seats add 2.1 points to the share of incumbents who run and 2.3 points to the share who win (Trounstine Table 6, row "District Council 0.021 ** 0.007 0.023 ** 0.009"). [V]
  - In California, district incumbents win 89–98% against 72–87% at-large (Adams). [V]
  - In Canada, the close-race (regression discontinuity, RD) estimate is 17 points larger for ward races, but the difference is not statistically significant (Lucas). [V]
    - Quote: "(a difference of 17 percentage points in the case of ward vs. at-large contests and 10 percentage points in the case of mayoral vs council races), the differences were, in general, statistically indiscernible from chance variation."
    - URL: https://ucalgary.scholaris.ca/bitstreams/4adcb141-5f9a-4f71-a0a7-22daf2dd2c74/download
  - **Check:** ward incumbents are safer by 2–10 points; at-large incumbents are never safer.
- **Town size:**
  - Smaller places have many more unopposed races. In Illinois and Wisconsin in 2025, 87–88% of races were uncontested in counties under 53,000 people, against 67% in Cook County. [R]
  - Small-town mayors ran unopposed 56% or 79% of the time; two summaries of the same study disagree. [R]
  - In California, district seats under 25,000 registered voters were only 78% contested. [V]
  - Among runners, each log-unit of population adds about 1.3 points to the run rate and takes about 1.5 points off the win rate (Trounstine Table 6). [V]
  - The close-race advantage is roughly flat by size. Quote (Ferreira and Gyourko, footnote 25): "Regressions not reported here show this large incumbent effect almost does not vary much by type of election (partisan vs non-partisan), by size of the city and for the most recent elections." [V]
    - URL: https://realestate.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/562.pdf
  - **Check:** the unopposed share falls as size rises, and the contested win rate stays roughly flat.
- **Partisan versus nonpartisan:** no significant difference (Trounstine Table 6, −0.005 and +0.013; Ferreira and Gyourko as above). [V]
- **Spread across places:** the city-level standard deviation of the win rate is 21–25 points (ICMA Table 4). Played worlds must show spread, not every place at 84%.
- **Wave years:** Minneapolis lost 5 of 11 incumbents in 2021 and 3 of 11 in 2017. A city-year loss rate of 40–50% should be possible but rare, and it needs a cause. [R]
  - URL: https://news.ballotpedia.org/2021/11/08/five-incumbents-defeated-in-minneapolis-city-council-elections/

(2) LOCAL INCUMBENCY ADVANTAGE
======================================================================

**A1. Win probability (best identified).** The close-race (RD) design compares candidates who barely won with candidates who barely lost, on whether they run and win the next election.

- **Best estimate:** +37 points, 95% CI 32–41. Mayors in 1,016 US cities of all sizes, 1950–2014, n = 12,207. [V]
  - URL: https://joeornstein.github.io/pols-4641/readings/de%20Benedictis-Kessner%20-%202018%20-%20Off-Cycle%20and%20Out%20of%20Office.pdf
  - Quote: "incumbent mayoral candidates benefit from an approximately 37 percentage-point increase in the probability that they will run and win their next election relative to nonincumbent candidates."
- **Band:** +30 to +46 points. Other estimates:
  - Council members: +46 (Trounstine; 4 large cities, 1900–1985). [V] Quote: "For city councilors the effect is a 44 percentage point increase in the probability of running and a 46 percentage point increase in the probability of running and winning."
  - The mayor's party: about +35 (Ferreira and Gyourko, Wharton version: about 70% against 35%). The NBER version gives 0.323 (standard error 0.055). [V for Wharton; R for NBER]
  - Canada: +30 over the full span, +41 in the modern era (Lucas). [V]
  - Lucas's literature summary: "incumbent status increases the probability of re-election by about 32 to 37 percentage points." [V]
- **The parts of it:**
  - Running again: +42 to +46 points. The de Benedictis-Kessner (dBK) text says "approximately 42"; his Table 2 says ".46 (.41, .51)". Trounstine gives 44 for councilors and .42 for mayors. [V]
  - Winning, given they ran: .50 for councilors and .64 for mayors (Trounstine footnote 4). [V] This is not a clean close-race estimate, because who runs again is self-selected. Use it only loosely.
  - Raw gap, not causal: 81% of incumbents who run again win, against 22% of losers who run again (dBK). [V]
- **Election timing:** +62 points when elections are on-cycle (held with even-year November elections) and +35 when off-cycle. Among cities that switched timing, +52 against +25. [V]
  - Quote: "In places with on-cycle elections, this is an increase of 62 percentage points relative to nonincumbents. In places with off-cycle elections, however, this advantage is far lower: incumbents only have a 35 percentage-point increase in their probability of running and winning the next election."
- **Lower bound:** in Finnish municipal elections where tied votes were settled by lottery, the personal advantage was zero. [R] Finland uses an open-list proportional system, so this is the floor where holding office carries little personal standing.
  - URL: https://research.hanken.fi/en/publications/when-does-regression-discontinuity-design-work-evidence-from-rand/
  - Quote: "The experimental estimate suggests that there is no personal incumbency advantage."

**A2. Vote share**

- **Best estimate:** about +10 points of next-election vote share for council members (Trounstine close-race estimate; 10.3 on the "sophomore surge" measure, the vote gain in a first re-election). [V]
  - URL: https://faculty.ucmerced.edu/jtrounstine/Trounstine_new_machines6.pdf
  - Quotes: "incumbent councilors increase their vote share in the next election by about 10 percentage points." / "the increase in vote share for councilors is about 10.3 percentage points and about 10.8 percentage points for mayors."
- **Band:** +5 to +12 points of two-way vote share in contested single-seat races, center +8 to +10. Other estimates:
  - Mayors, close-race: only 2.4 points and not significant ("The analysis for mayoral candidates reveals a 2.4 percentage points for mayors but the result is not statistically significant"). The sophomore-surge measure gives 10.8. [V]
  - The mayor's party, winning margin: +24.8 points (NBER) or about 28 (Wharton: "The full gap is about 28 percentage points"). That is +12 to +14 points of two-way share (DERIVED: share gap = margin gap / 2). [V for Wharton]
  - Holding spending fixed: +5.0 to +6.3 points (Adams Table 4; coefficients .063, .054, .051, .050). [V] This understates the total, because incumbency also raises spending.
  - Multi-seat boards with about 4 candidates per race: the average incumbent leads the average challenger by 4.5 points. [V] Quote (Payson): "the average incumbent earning only 4.5 points more than the average challenger"
- **Descriptive levels:**
  - Mayoral incumbents average 65% of the vote, non-incumbents 44% (dBK). [V]
  - The median incumbent share in South Carolina's competitive school board races is 58%. [R]
  - A party that barely won wins the next race by about 10 points, about 55% of the two-way vote (Ferreira and Gyourko). [V]

(3) WHAT THE GAME'S APPEAL TERMS SHOULD BE CHECKED AGAINST
======================================================================

**What the evidence measures.** Every estimate in section 2 is the total effect of holding office. That total includes name recognition, the record, staff and resources, the money advantage, deterring strong challengers, and any voter preference for an incumbent as such. No local study I found separates a "public standing" channel from a "voters favor incumbents" channel.

**One advantage, not two.** The evidence supports a single advantage. The sum of every channel the game models must produce it: the public standing term, the voter-trait term toward incumbents, money, endorsements and challenger entry. No single term should carry the whole effect. The sum should come to:

- about +8 to +10 points of two-way vote share (band +5 to +12) against a comparable challenger in contested single-seat races;
- about 4–5 points per candidate in multi-seat at-large races;
- a close-race effect in played worlds of +30 to +46 points on running and winning.

**Hints on how the total splits.** These are weak and are my own inference, not a measured split:

- Money carries part of it. With spending held fixed, Adams finds +5–6 points, against about +10 without that control in Trounstine. That suggests roughly 4–5 points run through money, but the samples differ.
- Deterring challengers works through who enters the race, not through voter appeal. Candidates for open seats raise more than candidates who challenge incumbents (Krebs 2001), and 35–67% of incumbents face no opponent. This should come from would-be challengers' decisions in the game, not from voters' appeal terms.
- A voter preference for "the incumbent" as such looks small: zero in the Finnish lottery, and only 4.5 points on multi-seat boards (Payson).

**Suggested setting for the voter-trait term.** If the public standing term already carries recognition and record, the voter-trait term toward incumbents should average near zero across the population. It would be a spread between voters who favor incumbents and voters who distrust them, adding at most a couple of points net. The owner or the traits lead should confirm this before anyone tunes it.

**How a played-world check tells a double count:**

1. **Totals check.** Targets: contested-only win rate T3 66–78%, T6 18–30%, and the T2 = u + (1 − u) × T3 rule holds.
   - Signs of a double count: T3 above about 80% (contested races behaving like uncontested ones), T6 below about 15%, T2 above about 92%.
   - A hidden problem even when T2 is in band: T3 too high together with T4 too low. That means appeal is too strong and is being masked by too many challengers entering.
2. **Close-race check in played worlds.** Across every local race in many seeds, take candidates who won or lost election t by under about 5 points of two-way share.
   - Compare the share who run and win at t+1: target +30 to +46 points.
   - Among those who run again, compare next-election share: target +5 to +12 points.
   - Signs of a double count: above about 46 points, or above about 12 points of share.
   - Check the decision to run again on its own too (target +42 to +46). If the game's decision to run again does not depend on having won, this check will look small even when appeal is double-counted, and only the vote-share part will show it.
3. **Twin-world ablation (same place and seed).** Run four versions: standing term zeroed, trait term zeroed, both zeroed, neither zeroed. In the same contested races, measure the incumbent's two-way share gap against a matched non-incumbent (or the close-race gap).
   - If standing alone lands in the +5 to +12 band and both terms together go above it, the trait term is a double count.
   - If the trait term alone already reaches the band, the standing term is the duplicate.
   - If both together equal standing plus trait almost exactly, the two terms are stacked: one effect counted twice, not one mechanism working through two channels.
   - With money held fixed, the gap left over should be about 5–6 points (Adams).
4. **Flatness.** The advantage should be roughly flat across town size and between partisan and nonpartisan races (Ferreira and Gyourko; dBK footnote 15).
   - If the game's gap grows strongly with size or partisanship, a term is scaling with the wrong thing.
   - The advantage should be larger on-cycle (dBK +62 against +35), but the Texas school board data conflict (see section 4). Keep timing as a watch, not a fail.
5. **Spread.** The city-level standard deviation should be about 20–25 points (ICMA). A tight cluster means a constant bonus is driving results rather than records and traits.

======================================================================
(4) WHAT IS WEAK OR MISSING
======================================================================

**Missing evidence:**

- **No US close-race estimate exists for townships, small towns, villages or county commissions.** Small places rest on four weaker sources:
  - school board data (Kogan, Lavertu and Peskowitz; Washington; Ballotpedia's 10 states);
  - a Michigan survey of local officials' perceptions, not counted races (45% of township and 51% of village leaders strongly agree too few residents run);
  - the Finnish lottery result, from a non-US system;
  - county commission rates from two single-state cases only: Georgia 2016 (primary only) and Maryland 2010 (a wave year, with a broad "county office" scope).
- **No local study splits the advantage** into standing, voter predisposition, money and deterrence. The split in section 3 is my inference.
- **Ballotpedia figures seen only in search snippets are not used.** They never appeared on a fetched page:
  - 2015–2024: 68.6% of council incumbents ran, 13.2% lost;
  - 2024: 214 ran, 15.4% lost;
  - 2023: 442 ran, 9.3% lost;
  - 2014–2021: 68.7% ran, 13.6% lost.
- **Not found at all:**
  - a National League of Cities survey of incumbent re-election;
  - any Holbein study of local incumbency;
  - Warshaw's 2019 review across levels of government;
  - township or village board rates;
  - full texts of Krebs 1998 and Berry and Howell 2007.

**Weak sources:**

- **Council-member close-race estimates rest on Trounstine's unpublished 2008 working paper.** It covers 4 large Sun Belt cities, 1900–1985, mostly at-large seats. The published 2011 version was paywalled (403), so its final numbers may differ.
- **The ICMA run and win rates are old** (1986–2001). They come from city clerk surveys with a 64% response rate and are averages of city-level percentages.
- **Ward against at-large:** the only adjusted US estimate is about +2 points (Trounstine). The California gap comes from a convenience sample, and the Canadian 17-point gap is not significant.
- **Vote share:** beyond Trounstine's 4 cities, there is no well-identified US local close-race estimate of the personal vote-share advantage. The mayoral close-race estimate (2.4 points) is not significant. Multi-seat races need a different measure (share per candidate).
- **Ballotpedia's own counts lean toward large places.**

**Traps in the sources:**

- **A misread to avoid.** "About one incumbent councilor in every two cities declines to run and about one councilor in every nine cities loses" is not a base rate. In the source it follows "When states require the mailing of polling place information, fewer incumbents run," and it describes the effect of that mailing law (Table 5: 38.0% against 44.7% running, 84.0% against 85.6% winning). [V] Do not calibrate to it.
- **Trounstine's model-predicted running shares (38–45%) do not match the Table 4 averages (59–76%).** The denominators differ: the models likely include staggered-term cities, where every sitting member is counted. Use Table 4.
- **Timing is unresolved.** Texas school board incumbents lose more often in even years (19.76% against 14.44%), but dBK finds a bigger mayoral advantage on-cycle. Do not calibrate a timing term from this evidence.

**Conflicts within and between sources:**

- **dBK's effect on running again** is 42 in the text and .46 in Table 2.
- **Ferreira and Gyourko exist in two versions.** Both measure the party's advantage, not the person's, in cities of at least 25,000:
  - Wharton: 3,496 elections, 392 cities, 70% against 35%, a 28-point margin gap;
  - NBER: 4,543 elections, 413 cities, 66% against 33%, 0.323 and 0.248.
- **Ballotpedia's Datawrapper CSV** implies an 85.7% school board win rate for 2022, while the article gives 78.29%. The CSV labels its 2023 row Wisconsin, while the chart text says Colorado.
- **Berry and Howell's counts don't add up:** 157 − 42 = 115, not 112.
- **Small-town mayors running unopposed:** 79% or 56%, from two summaries of the same study.
- **Ballotpedia's average uncontested share:** 58% (2018–2023) or 64% (2018–2024).

B. CALIBRATION TOTALS, REWRITTEN FROM VERIFIED QUOTES ONLY
======================================================================

These are checks on totals across many played worlds, each a random place with its seed named. They never decide a race.

**T1. Share of incumbents who seek re-election.** The denominator is eligible incumbents whose seat is up. Ballotpedia's "seats up" figures include term-limited seats and are lower bounds.

- **School boards: 59–78%, center about 68.** Evidence:
  - 16 states: 59%.
  - Large districts, 2022–2025: 62–69%.
  - 10 states, 2023: 68% of seats.
  - Washington, 2023: 78% of seats (DERIVED).
  - California: 68–76%.
  - Florida: 67–77%.
  - South Carolina, 2000: 71–73% (DERIVED).
- **City councils: 59–76%.** Evidence:
  - ICMA, councils without staggered terms: 59–76%.
  - Ballotpedia, large cities, 2015–2024: 68.6% of seats (62.1–74.7% by year).
  - Staggered-term cities give a mechanical 36% when every sitting member is counted.
- **Mayors: 63–68% of seats up, about 77% of eligible mayors (DERIVED).**
- **County commissions and special districts: 80–86%.** Evidence:
  - Ballotpedia, 2017–2024: 82.1% of seats (even years 81.7–85.9%).
  - Georgia, 2016: 81% of eligible (DERIVED).
- **All offices together: band 58–80%, center about 69%.** Fail below 50% or above 88%.
- **Correction:** the synthesis rule "local below state legislators (76%)" is wrong for counties. Only school boards and councils sit below 76%. Counties sit at or above it. Every local office sits below the US House (90%).

**T2. Share of running incumbents who win, all races.**

- **Councils, school boards and mayors: band 80–90%, center about 84.** Fail below 75% or above 93%. Evidence:
  - School boards: 82% (16 states), 83–85% (Ballotpedia large districts and 10 states; 17% historical loss rate), 90% (Washington).
  - Councils: 82–88% (ICMA), 87.1% (top 100 cities, 2014–2020), 86.8% (2015–2024; 82.8–90.7% by year).
  - Ballotpedia local legislative, general elections: 83% (2022), 86% (2024).
  - Mayors: 81% (dBK), 83.1–83.4% (Ballotpedia).
- **Counties: 88–94%.** Ballotpedia shows 7.6% defeated (5.3–12.2% by year).
- **California is lower:**
  - all local offices, 1995–2025: 78% of ballot incumbents (council 78%, school 74%);
  - Payson's school boards: 73.5–78.4%;
  - Adams's at-large seats: 72–87%, with runoff entrants counted as winners.

  California-like places (on the ballot only, many seats per race) may sit at 74–80%.

**T3. Share of running incumbents who win, contested races only.**

- **Band: 66–78%, center about 72.** Warn above about 80%.
- Evidence: Washington 69% (74% for 2018–2022); 10 states 68% (74% historical); South Carolina 75% (50 of 67).
- Adams is removed: it is an all-races rate.
- California's Secretary of State and Payson figures are kept only as weak upper checks (74–78%), because their contested status is unverified.

**T4. Share of running incumbents with no opponent.** The band depends on place size.

- **Small and mid places (incumbent-level, verified): 40–67%.** Evidence:
  - Washington school boards: 67%.
  - Georgia counties: 47%, primary stage.
  - South Carolina: 40%.
  - Florida: about 28–52% (DERIVED).
- **Large-city councils and mayors: well below that.** Ballotpedia's race-level figures are 17.5% of council races and 5.7% of mayoral races uncontested.
- **Race-level size gradient** (open seats included):
  - Illinois and Wisconsin 2025: about 80% overall, 87–88% in rural or mid county classes, 62–67% in metro counties.
  - Rice mayoral study: 79% in small towns against 15% in the largest cities.
  - Ballotpedia counties: 33.5–58.8%.
  - Kogan, Lavertu and Peskowitz school boards: 45–55%.
- **Rule:** u must fall as size rises. Small-town u at 40–80% is plausible. Large-city u above about 30% is a fail.
- **Identity:** T2 = u + (1 − u) × T3. Washington checks it: 0.67 + 0.33 × 0.69 = 0.90.

**T5. Share of seats up where the incumbent keeps the seat.**

- **School boards and councils: 45–65%.** Evidence:
  - 16 states: 47% (DERIVED from 53% turnover).
  - Large-district school boards, 2025: 58%.
  - ICMA 2001: 62% (DERIVED).
  - Ballotpedia councils: 68.6% × 86.8% = 60% (DERIVED).
- **Counties: 70–80%.** Ballotpedia: 82.1% × 92.4% = 76% (DERIVED).

**T6. Among newly seated members, the share who beat an incumbent.**

- **Band: 18–30%, center about 22.** Under 15% means incumbents are too safe; above 35% means they are too weak.
- Direct evidence: school boards in 16 states, 21% loss and 79% not running.
- DERIVED from verified inputs:
  - councils, 2019: 22.5%;
  - Ballotpedia councils, 2015–2024: 22%;
  - large-district school boards, 2025: 26%;
  - 10 states, 2023: 24%;
  - Ballotpedia mayors: 26%;
  - Ballotpedia counties: 26%.
- For comparison: state legislatures 11/89 and the US House 31/69.

**T7. Direction checks.**

- **Ward against at-large: ward seats are safer, never less safe.**
  - Trounstine: +2.1 points on running and +2.3 on winning.
  - Adams, all races: 89–98% against 72–87%.
  - Lucas: 17 points, not significant.
- **Size:**
  - The unopposed share falls with size (item 50 and the Rice study).
  - Among runners, each log-unit of population adds 1.3 points to the run rate and takes 1.5 off the win rate. The win coefficient is significant only at p<.10.
  - The close-race advantage is roughly flat by size. Only Ferreira and Gyourko's footnote 25 supports flatness. dBK's footnote 15 supports only "still exists."
- **Partisan against nonpartisan:** no significant difference (Trounstine; Ferreira and Gyourko).
- **Spread:** the city-level standard deviation is 21–25 points for winning and 22–32 for running (ICMA). Payson's district-year standard deviations are 34–37. Played worlds must not cluster.
- **Wave years:** a city-year loss of 40–50% must be possible but rare, and must have a cause (Minneapolis 2021: 5 of 11).
- **Timing:** larger on-cycle (+62 against +35; switching cities +52 against +25). Keep it as a watch, not a fail. The Texas counter-evidence is unverified.

**A1. Close-race (regression discontinuity) effect on running and winning again.**

- **Band: +30 to +46 points, center +37.** Evidence:
  - dBK: +37, CI 32–41, n = 12,207, 1,016 cities.
  - Trounstine councils: +46.
  - Ferreira and Gyourko, party: +35 (Wharton), +32.3 (NBER).
  - Lucas: +30 and +41.
  - Literature summary: 32–37.
- **Running again alone:** +42 to +46 (dBK 42 / .46; Trounstine .42 for mayors and 44 for councilors).
- **Lower bound:** a lottery-based estimate puts the personal advantage at zero (Hanken). Its system detail is unverified.

**A2. Vote share against a comparable challenger, contested races with one seat.**

- **Band: +5 to +12 points, center +8 to +10.** Evidence:
  - Trounstine councils: about 10, or 10.3 on the sophomore-surge measure.
  - Mayors: 2.4, not significant; 10.8 on the sophomore-surge measure.
  - Ferreira and Gyourko, party: 24.8–28 points of margin, which is about 12–14 points of two-way share (DERIVED).
  - Adams, spending held fixed: 5.0–6.3.
- **Races with several seats:** about 4.5 points per candidate (Payson).
- **Descriptive levels:** 65% against 44% (dBK); median incumbent share in South Carolina's competitive races 58%. A party that barely won wins the next race by about 5 points (NBER) or about 10 (Wharton), so the next-race margin is 5–10.

**Double-count tests: unchanged in logic.**

- Fail signs:
  - T3 above 80%;
  - T6 below 15%;
  - T2 above 92% outside counties;
  - a close-race effect above 46 points;
  - a vote-share effect above 12 points;
  - in the four-version ablation run (standing term and trait term each zeroed in turn), the trait term pushes the standing-only gap out of the +5 to +12 band.
- The money-held-fixed residual should be about 5–6 points (Adams).
- The Krebs 2001 deterrence citation is unverified. Deterrence must still come from would-be challengers' decisions to enter, checked against T4.
