# Words and scenes research: the campaign season, election night, and big speeches

Date: 2026-10-26. Branch: `words/research-scenes-oct26`. Read-only research for the words-and-scenes lead.

Rules for this file: shapes and patterns only, no copied sentences. Every finding ends with the page it came from and a note of what that page showed. Only pages that were actually fetched are cited as sources. Where a fetch failed (rate limits, blocked pages), the gap is named. Practices differ by state and town, so spreads are given where pages differ.

## 1. A local candidate's season between filing and election day

### Findings

- **Campaign guides put the work in phases counted back from election day.** One local-race timeline runs: 6-9 months out, open a campaign account and start building a team; 5-6 months, recruit a volunteer network and plan phone and door programs; 3-4 months, ask influential people and groups for endorsements, attend forums and debates, start door-knocking; 2 months, plan poll monitoring; 30 days, press unions and community groups for endorsements, schedule forums, run a volunteer drive; 21 days, early-vote outreach and larger events such as rallies; 14 days, a final meet-and-greet to raise money for last ads, phone calls to push early voting, heavier door-knocking aimed at undecided voters; 7 days, get-out-the-vote calls and doors, a final town hall, a voter hotline and last reminders. (https://www.onlinecandidate.com/articles/political-campaign-timeline - a commercial campaign-site timeline; it is a planning guide, not observed practice.)
- **The same page says forum and debate dates are often set by outside groups**, and tells candidates to confirm filing, registration, early-voting, absentee and finance-report dates with the local elections board. (https://www.onlinecandidate.com/articles/political-campaign-timeline - closing "key dates" list.)
- **A first-time local candidate's own advice: filing deadlines sit surprisingly close to election day.** He recommends one "point person" to track tasks and deadlines, mentors who have served locally, optionally a campaign manager for follow-up calls and checklists, small local donors, a modest budget (his first goal was $1,000, filing finance paperwork once he passed it), door-knocking weekly, and showing up at farmers' markets, park events and board meetings. (https://www.bleedingheartland.com/2017/06/05/initial-advice-for-folks-looking-at-local-runs/ - a 2017 first-person blog post about an Iowa-style local race.)
- **Reporters call local candidates, and the advice is to ask an experienced official first.** The same author says to send a clear recent photo when papers ask, and that once you speak, the paper uses quotes as it sees fit. (https://www.bleedingheartland.com/2017/06/05/initial-advice-for-folks-looking-at-local-runs/ - media section.)
- **Candidates cannot collect absentee ballots; a point person tracks absentee request and early-voting dates.** (https://www.bleedingheartland.com/2017/06/05/initial-advice-for-folks-looking-at-local-runs/ - timing section.)
- **League of Women Voters forum lead times (Maine chapter checklist):** plan the format, ground rules, cosponsors and moderators 2-3 months ahead; confirm the venue and recruit moderator and timekeepers 2 months ahead; mail an individual invitation letter to each candidate **6 weeks ahead** with time, place, ground rules, format, a contact phone number and an RSVP date, then a confirming letter; media invitations, flyers and press release **4 weeks ahead**; agenda and timing rules out to candidates 2-3 weeks ahead; the event itself ends with a thank-you round. (https://www.lwvme.org/sites/default/files/pdfs/Candidates_Night_Checklist.pdf - the League of Women Voters of Maine "Candidates' Forum Checklist", text read via pdftotext.)
- **Another League's rules (Akron area):** every board-certified candidate for the chosen local races is invited, including unopposed ones; invitations go out in a traceable form (verified email, or certified mail); candidates RSVP by a set date; a candidate who declines may send a short statement the moderator reads; no-shows are planned around; a forum is not a debate (no planned candidate-to-candidate exchange); timed opening statements; audience questions screened by two people; no campaign clothing or recording inside. (https://my.lwv.org/sites/default/files/leagues/akron-area/lwvaacandidateforumpolicyguidelinesfinal8012019.pdf - Akron-area League guidelines dated 8/1/2019, read via pdftotext.)
- **Newspaper endorsement process, one example:** a campus paper's board interviewed 10 mayor and council candidates over about two weeks (Oct. 6-20), picked who to interview by fundraising reports and issue views, decided on Oct. 23, and published Oct. 30, eight days before the Nov. 7 election. Only competitive council wards got attention. With ranked-choice voting it also named second and third choices. (https://mndaily.com/220864/opinion/opendorsementprocess - Minnesota Daily, 2017.)
- **Spread on endorsement timing:** the endorsement example above is about 2.5 weeks before election day for the interviews and 1 week for publication. A search summary mentioned another large paper starting interviews as early as March for a primary; that page was not fetched, so treat it as unconfirmed.
- **Early and absentee voting:** a 2012 national tool noted roughly a third of voters were expected to vote before election day, that early and absentee voting is available in most states, and that "no-excuse" rules vary. (https://apps.npr.org/early-voting-2012 - NPR 2012 early-voting page; the state table itself did not load.) A search snippet (page not fetched, so unverified) gave an average early-voting start of about 19-22 days before election day, a maximum near 45 days, and absentee ballots available 21-45 days out in various states. This spread needs a proper source before it becomes game data; it belongs in the place's own records anyway.

### What the game can use

1. **Calendar anchored to the place's own records.** The filing date, the early-voting window and the absentee window come from the place's data; everything below is counted back from election day (E) and clipped to the days that remain after filing.
2. **A forum invitation arrives about 6 weeks before E from a civic group** (League of Women Voters, chamber, paper), with a place, a format, ground rules and an RSVP date a week or two later. A press note follows about 4 weeks out. A declined or missed forum is planned around and shows up in the scene as an empty chair.
3. **Forum versus debate is a scene choice.** A forum means timed opening statements and screened audience questions with no cross-talk; a debate adds a response to the opponent. Large fields become a "meet and greet" format.
4. **Newspaper contact comes in two steps:** a questionnaire or reporter call, then an editorial board interview in the 2-3 weeks before E, then the endorsement lands about a week before E. Boards pick interviewees by money raised and competitiveness, so a thin field gets fewer calls.
5. **Team building is front-loaded:** a point person (treasurer or manager) and mentors come first, volunteers recruited months out, a final volunteer drive about 30 days out. A family member joining is a natural early scene.
6. **Fundraising beats:** small local donors early; a final meet-and-greet at about 14 days out to pay for last mail or ads.
7. **Union and group endorsements cluster about 30 days out** (the planning guide's window); party and official endorsements can be earlier. Each endorsement has a source name that later speech text and journal lines can cite.
8. **Pace the final two weeks:** door-knocking intensifies for undecided voters, early-vote reminders go to supporters, and a last town hall and rally fall in the last 7 days.
9. **Reporters ask, a mentor advises:** a media call is a short scene where a trusted local official's advice changes what the candidate says.
10. **Other candidates and incumbents** appear mainly at forums and board meetings; no sourced pattern was found for private approaches, so these should be built from relationships and traits rather than a fixed date (gap: no page fetched on this).

## 2. Election day and night for a local candidate

### Findings

- **Fetch gap:** the two pages found for what a candidate does on election day (voting, press at polls, visiting polling places) returned errors on every fetch, so those details here rest only on the items below. Candidate voting with the press present is therefore not sourced in this file.
- **A final-days GOTV rhythm** from the planning guide: in the last 7 days, expanded get-out-the-vote doors and phone calls to registered voters who have not yet voted, a final town hall and a get-out-the-vote rally, a voter hotline, final email and text reminders; on election day, volunteers monitor polling places and report problems, and the campaign follows results. (https://www.onlinecandidate.com/articles/political-campaign-timeline - last-week and election-day rows.)
- **Election night in a small city (Bend, Oregon, 2012):** initial results were expected at the county services building around 8 p.m., where council, county and judge candidates were to gather; party gatherings ran from 5 to 11 p.m. at different venues; bars and a theater showed results on screens; candidates moved between venues after the first returns. (https://www.bendsource.com/news/best-places-to-watch-the-returns-tonight-2188123/ - Bend Source, Nov. 6, 2012.)
- **Returns come slowly, and networks sometimes call early.** In an Ohio example, early reports were mostly absentee totals while the state site was still posting; the winner was projected about an hour after polls closed using exit polls and stringers at key precincts; the loser conceded soon after, supporters offered condolences, then the winner gave a victory speech. The state's election chief called election-night figures projections because mail and provisional ballots may still be outstanding. (https://wysu.org/ohio-news/2024-04-17/theres-often-little-drama-at-ohio-election-night-watch-parties-but-that-might-be-changing - WYSU, April 2024; a statewide primary, not a local race.)
- **U.S. concession practice has two steps:** a private call to the winner, then a public speech to supporters, usually after the outcome is clear. Out of courtesy the winner usually waits for the loser's speech before giving theirs. Conceding too early can backfire (Carter 1980 spoke while western polls were open), and in close races the decision is hard because of media pressure and hope for a late turn. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concession_(politics) - Wikipedia article on concession.)
- **Four parts of a concession speech:** state the defeat and congratulate the winner; call for unity behind the winner's term; honor the democratic process and voter participation; vow to keep working on the issues. Losers also thank supporters and point to what the campaign achieved, and unless the race was bitter, wish the winner well. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concession_(politics) - same article.)
- **Close local races are conceded days later, in writing.** A Boston at-large council candidate waited for "every mail-in ballot" to be counted, then conceded the next evening in a written statement. Its order: not the result hoped for but proud of the campaign; thanks to family, team and volunteers; congratulations and good wishes to the winners by name. (https://www.boston.com/news/politics/2021/11/03/boston-city-council-fourth-at-large-seat/ - Boston.com, Nov. 3, 2021.)
- **A very slow mail count:** a Seattle council incumbent conceded in person, surrounded by supporters, **62 days** after the Nov. 5 election, once updated county results showed his opponent ahead by about 1,640 votes. He thanked voters for 16 years, praised his staff and campaign, thanked supporters, endorsers and donors, formally conceded, and hoped the winner would serve well. (https://www.fox13seattle.com/news/longtime-city-councilman-conlin-concedes-defeat-to-sawant.amp - Fox 13 Seattle, Jan. 6, 2014. This was a 62-day gap in a vote-by-mail county; it is the long end of the spread, not typical.)
- **Candidates sometimes stay away from the party until results are in.** (Reported in a search summary of two pages; the Bend Source page does show candidates moving among several venues, which is the part confirmed above.)

### What the game can use

1. **Election day itself is a short chain of scenes:** morning at the polls (the candidate votes; press presence depends on the race's profile and has no fetched source here), midday visits to volunteers at polling places, afternoon last calls, evening party.
2. **Polls close, first returns arrive within about an hour or two,** from the county elections site or a clerk's posted tapes, precinct by precinct. The first numbers are usually early/absentee or one precinct group, so the first scene should carry uncertainty.
3. **Venue pattern:** a small town has one place where candidates cluster for the first official numbers, plus separate party gatherings; candidates drift between venues. The game needs one watch-party scene per candidate with a screen, a table of volunteers and family, and a runner with precinct numbers.
4. **Call before speech.** The concession is a private call first, and a public statement second. The winner waits for the loser to speak before the victory speech.
5. **Concession order (written or spoken):** (a) concede and congratulate the winner by name; (b) thank family, team and volunteers; (c) say what the campaign did or stood for; (d) a short line on unity or what comes next. Boston and Seattle examples both open with pride or thanks and name the winner or wish them well; vary the order by how bitter the race was.
6. **Victory speech order:** the thank-yous come first (voters, volunteers, family, then the opponent's graciousness), then what the win means, then the work ahead. This order is inferred from the Ohio sequence and the concession elements, not from a fetched victory-speech page; mark it as an estimate.
7. **Close races stretch past election night.** If the margin is within what uncounted mail ballots could change, the scene is "waiting for the count" and the concession arrives in days (Boston, next day) or weeks (Seattle, 62 days). Timing follows the place's own ballot-counting rules, never a random draw.
8. **Written versus spoken:** local losers often send a written statement or email; in-person speeches follow where a party was held.
9. **Concession tone** scales with bitterness: a gracious concession wishes the winner well; in a bitter race the congratulations stay short.

## 3. Shape of inaugural, state-of-the-union and state-of-the-state addresses

### Findings

- **Short and long poles.** Lincoln's Second Inaugural (1865) is about 700 words in a salutation and four paragraphs: a modest opening, a look back at how the war began, a reflection on cause and providence, and a one-sentence closing of resolve. (https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/inaugural-address-35 - American Presidency Project text of Lincoln's Second Inaugural; section summary is the fetch tool's reading.)
- **FDR 1933** is about 1,400-1,500 words with this order: candid opening, diagnosis of the crisis, moral argument, action priorities, foreign policy in one passage, a call for shared discipline, a note on constitutional authority, and a confident closing with a request for blessing. It uses no statistics at all. (https://avalon.law.yale.edu/20th_century/froos1.asp - Yale Avalon text; length and "no statistics" are the fetch tool's reading of it.)
- **JFK 1961** is about 1,350 words: opening that frames the day as freedom's, stakes, generational handoff, pledges to groups in turn (allies, new states, the poor, neighbors, the UN), a parallel-clause appeal to adversaries, a call to action, and a closing with paired appeals to Americans then to the world. Numbers are used for time only ("first 100 days," "first 1,000 days," about 175 years since the oath). (https://avalon.law.yale.edu/20th_century/kennedy.asp - Yale Avalon text.)
- **Truman 1949** is about 2,300 words in eight movements, ending with a hopeful, faith-centered close; the middle is a "four courses of action" list. (https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/inaugural-address-4 - American Presidency Project text.)
- **Obama 2009** moves through: brief opening, crisis with measurable indicators plus dread, inspiration from past hardships, broad goals on issues without detail, and a close that looks back to Washington. (https://www.shmoop.com/study-guides/historical-texts/barack-obama-2009-inaugural-address/structure.html - study-guide summary, secondary source.)
- **Commentary on what lasts:** a commentator argues inaugurals work best as a statement of values rather than a policy agenda, that brevity helps, and that most are remembered for one phrase standing for the whole speech. (https://politicalwire.com/2021/01/20/the-secret-to-a-great-inaugural-address/ - Political Wire quoting John Avlon, opinion.)
- **State of the Union, Reagan 1985** is about 5,000 words (an estimate): opening on progress with economic statistics, a vision section, economy and technology, budget, space and environment, social issues, national security, then a close that returns to two guests in the gallery. One guest is a young woman from Vietnam graduating from West Point; the other an older Harlem woman caring for drug-exposed infants. Each person stands for one idea (opportunity; private compassion). (https://www.reaganlibrary.gov/archives/speech/address-joint-session-congress-state-union-0 - Reagan Library transcript page; section order is the fetch tool's reading.)
- **State of the Union, Trump 2019** is about 6,000 words (an estimate) with the opposite number style: dozens of figures (jobs, spending, arrests, dollars), alongside a dozen-plus named guests with short stories; opens with a call for unity and anniversaries, runs by topic (economy, justice, border, trade, health, family, security), and closes honoring survivors and veterans before a call to "choose greatness." (https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/address-before-joint-session-the-congress-the-state-the-union-26 - American Presidency Project transcript; the fetch tool's reading.)
- **State of the state length:** 44 governors in 2025 averaged 4,607 words; Democrats 5,265, Republicans 4,107; shortest 2,216 words, longest 10,934. Most frequent words were state, new, people, year and work. (https://news.ballotpedia.org/2025/03/07/44-governors-have-given-their-state-of-the-state-addresses-so-far-this-year/ - Ballotpedia, March 7, 2025.) Speech structure and guest use for governors were not found on a fetched page.

### What the game can use

1. **Length bands by role.** A small-town swearing-in remark: a few hundred words. A state governor's inaugural or state-of-the-state: about 2,000-5,000 words (average 4,600, wide spread). Make length a parameter tagged to the role.
2. **Inaugural skeleton:** salutation, short personal acceptance, state of things (diagnosis), a few values, two or three named commitments by group, a call to the audience, a short closing with hope. FDR, JFK and Truman all fit this skeleton at different weights.
3. **One number with a person.** The strongest pattern across the examples: a single figure or fact tied to one named person or family (Reagan's two guests, the Trump guests) rather than a table of numbers; reserve statistics-heavy passages for the economy section and keep them short.
4. **Two number styles as a trait choice.** Plain-spoken speakers (FDR) use no statistics; heavy-data speakers (the 2019 SOTU) stack figures. A speaker's style should come from traits; the pieces need both styles.
5. **Return to the guest at the close.** A State of the Union-style speech ends by circling back to a person introduced earlier; use the same trick in a governor's address and the mayor's state-of-the-city.
6. **Section order for an address-style scene:** opening and thanks, the year (what happened), the money, three or four topic sections each with one story or number, what we will do next year, closing. Topic sections come from the town's own record (a passed ordinance, a budget line).
7. **Closing options:** a call to the audience, a pledge, a blessing or hope line; JFK and Lincoln show a single sentence of resolve can carry the close.
8. **Opening options:** thanks to named officials; humility about the office (Truman, Lincoln); a frame of the moment (JFK, FDR calm); an anniversary or shared date (2019 SOTU).
9. **Mention the opponent graciously** in an inaugural after a contested race (a unity call is common to inaugurals and concessions alike).
10. **Do not state policy detail in an inaugural;** keep it to values and direction, and hold specifics for the later address.
