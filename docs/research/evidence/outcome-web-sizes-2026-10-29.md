# Outcome-web sizes, Oct. 29, 2026

Research sizes for ten links of the outcome web, each checked by a second reader against the page it came from. These sizes calibrate links that a later family wires. No link reads them yet, and `links.json` is unchanged. The quotes, the conversions, the ranges and the verifier's findings are in `data/research/outcome-web/sizes-2026-10-29.json`. SOURCED means the page prints the number for this measure. ESTIMATED means it is derived from the nearest study, with the spread stated. "Verified" means the verifier found the quote word for word and the conversion right.

**Eviction and hospital visits** (`eviction-to-hospital-visits`). Collinson and coauthors (2024) compared New York tenants whose eviction cases drew stricter or more lenient judges. In the year after filing, an eviction added 0.188 hospital visits (not counting pregnancy) per tenant, on a base of 0.739, so visits rise by about a quarter: size 0.254, range 0.005 to 0.504, SOURCED, verified. The paper's text says 29 percent, but its own table gives 25 percent; the table is used. Year two shows no significant change, so the effect lasts 12 months. A later family multiplies one evicted tenant's own care need in the health engine for 365 days. It never goes on a place-wide count, and it is not counted in both care and strain.

**Losing transit and unemployment** (`transit-loss-to-unemployment`). The link's own source, Tyndall (2017), studied the R train, cut off in Brooklyn after Hurricane Sandy. Nearby residents became 1.4 points more likely to be unemployed, on an 8.0% base, so the size is 0.175 per line lost (range 0.126 to 0.224), SOURCED, verified. The verifier notes that the outage lasted only part of the year and that the standard errors are not clustered, so the true effect and its spread may be larger. In the per-service unit the survey plans, a meta-analysis of commute times (Bastiaanssen and coauthors, 2020), combined with average transit waits of 10 to 20 minutes, gives 0.134 per unit cut in service (range 0.032 to 0.29), ESTIMATED, verified. The verifier lowered the floor for groups of all ages, because the studies are mostly of young people. An England study (Johnson and coauthors, 2017) gives 0.152 (range 0.039 to 0.297) once its table is read correctly: its numbers are changes in the employment rate, not percentage changes. As first converted it was not verified. A later family uses 0.175 while the unit stays "line lost", or 0.134 once the unit becomes service per resident. Either way the jobs engine reads it when it decides, never the shared unemployment count that eight links already read.

**Home broadband and employment** (`broadband-to-employment`). Zuo (2021) studied Comcast's Internet Essentials, cheap broadband for low-income families with school-age children. Enrollees were 8.1 points (14.3%) more likely to work. Counted per point of households with home broadband, that is 0.0014 (range 0.0004 to 0.0032), ESTIMATED, verified. This is a cautious center: only about 0.44 of enrollees were new home-internet users, and dividing the employment effect by the internet-use effect gives 0.0032, the top of the range. The effect sits in households at or under 185% of the poverty line, and in urban areas, the opposite of the row's "rural counties most". A later family sets the per-point size, adds a weight row by income fifth (full weight at or under 185% of poverty, none above), fixes the group text, and keeps it from double counting the married-women-work link. The paper itself would not load; its sentence comes from a reprint and its base from the journal's official appendix.

**Pollution in the birth year and adult earnings** (`birth-year-pollution-to-adult-earnings`). Isen, Rossin-Slater and Walker (2017) used the Clean Air Act of 1970: people born where the act cut particulates earned more at ages 29 to 31. A birth year 10% cleaner means about 1% more earnings (elasticity -0.1, range -0.29 to 0.04) and about 0.8% more quarters worked (elasticity -0.08, range -0.21 to 0.01). Both are ESTIMATED and verified. The paper measured total suspended particulates, at levels about ten times today's fine particulates, so carrying the elasticity over is an assumption. The survey reads this link where the jobs engine decides whether a person works, so a later family uses the work elasticity, -0.08, times the log of the birth year's particulates over the base, and never both.

**Heat and deaths** (`heat-to-deaths`). Barreca and coauthors (2016) found that each extra day with a high of 90 to 99 degrees Fahrenheit raised death rates in 1960-2004 by about 0.037 deaths per 100,000 people a year (range 0 to 0.111), ESTIMATED, verified. Deschenes and Greenstone (2011) give a larger figure, 0.936 per 100,000 for a day with a mean above 90 degrees, which scales to 0.138 for the game's milder hot day. The verifier widened that range to 0 to 0.30, because the scaling ratio is uncertain, so as first converted it was not verified. Air conditioning removed almost all of the excess. The verifier's corrected moderator is -0.0100 per percentage point of homes with air conditioning (range -0.0100 to -0.0052), capped so that full air conditioning means no excess deaths, never fewer than none. Places range from 0.1 to almost 100 hot days a year, so one proportional size cannot work. A later family puts heat deaths on a level scale with the no-air-conditioning slope, about 0.090 per 100,000 per hot day, applies the moderator, and counts only the change from each place's base, since today's death tables already include heat deaths.

**Electricity prices and air conditioning** (`energy-price-to-ac-use`). No study measures how air-conditioning use responds to price. Deryugina, MacKay and Reif (2020) found that household electricity use falls 0.09% per 1% price rise in the first six months and 0.27% after two years. At the national 12.94 cents per kWh, that is -0.0209 per cent per kWh in the long run (range -0.0209 to 0), ESTIMATED. As first converted it was not verified: the verifier moved the lag from six months to none, building up over 24 months. It is an upper bound, since a bill barely changes whether a home has air conditioning. Doremus, Jacqz and Johnston (2022) show that low-income households barely raise their electricity spending on very hot days (about 5% of others' response, with a spread of 0 to 0.8). That fits households not running air conditioning they own, and it is a possible income weight on the heat moderator, not a size. A later family either counts the link as covered by an electricity price response in the price engine or wires it with the build-up, but never both.

**Carbon monoxide and school absence** (`carbon-monoxide-to-absence`). Currie and coauthors (2009) found, in large Texas districts, that a day with carbon monoxide at 75 to 100% of the federal standard of 9 ppm raised absences by 5 points on a 3.58% base, and that lower levels did nothing. As a threshold at 6.75 ppm, that is 1.24 per ppm above it (range 0.19 to 1.81), ESTIMATED, verified. The verifier's floor comes from the paper's model with earlier and later periods, which also shows the missed days are mostly made up within the next six weeks. In 2025 EPA's 90th-percentile monitor read 1.72 ppm on its second-worst 8-hour average, a quarter of where the effect starts. At today's levels the effect is zero (range -0.063 to 0.019), ESTIMATED, verified. A later family grades the link about-zero, size 0, in data only. The threshold is the alternative if the lead wants a world to react to a rare bad day.

**Newspaper closures and borrowing costs** (`newspaper-closure-to-borrowing-cost`). Gao, Lee and Murphy (2020) found that after a county's daily newspaper closed, municipal bonds issued there paid 5.5 basis points more (range 3.1 to 7.9), SOURCED, verified. The paper's number applies from three years after the closure, so the verifier set the lag to 36 months; the effect is smaller in the first two years. A later family gives the row a basis-point unit and lets county and city treasuries read it on their own interest lines, not the state's. It counts at most one closure per county, and the link moves nothing until papers close in play.

**Attacks on the press and trust** (`press-attacks-to-media-trust`). Archer and Peterson (2025) showed people a Republican politician from their own state attacking their local paper. Among Republicans, the share calling the paper trustworthy fell from 74% to 43%, a drop of 0.419 in proportion per attack heard (range -0.485 to -0.353), ESTIMATED, verified. The verifier's rerun of the authors' data gives -0.428, and a stricter definition of trust gives -0.52. Trust in CNN barely moved: about 0, with a range of -0.171 to 0.024 for the attacker's supporters. That entry was not verified as first written, because it had no range. Trust was measured minutes after the attack, so how long the drop lasts is unknown. A later family points the link at trust in the attacked outlet in the news engine instead of general media trust, reads hostility as whether the person heard an attack, and registers how long it lasts as a stopgap.

**Reclassifying low-level offenses and larceny** (`release-lower-level-to-larceny`). Dominguez-Rivera, Lofstrom and Raphael (2019) studied Proposition 47, which made low-level theft and drug offenses misdemeanors. Larceny rose 128.94 per 100,000 a year, 8.44% of the 2014 rate. Scaled by larceny's share of household property crime (0.8125, from the Bureau of Justice Statistics for 2024), that is 0.0686 per law in force, ESTIMATED, verified. Bartos and Kubrin (2018) cannot rule out zero, so the recommended range is 0 to 0.1137. The authors' own "5 percent" summary describes property crime, not larceny, so 0.0406 is only a low alternative; the entry that first used it is marked not verified and now carries the corrected 0.0686. The row's current size, 0.0731 from the 2018 PPIC report, is close. FBI data give each place's larceny rate (1,147 per 100,000 nationally in 2025) if a separate larceny measure is built. A later family sets size 0.0686, range 0 to 0.1137, no lag (the rise came at once) and evidence "contested", and lists all five authors of the PPIC report.
