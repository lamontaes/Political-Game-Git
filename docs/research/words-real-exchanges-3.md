# Real exchanges, round 3: openings, endings, substance (18 situations)

## Read this first: evidence quality

Verified this session (read text): Romney at the Iowa State Fair with hecklers (2011 account); a former press secretary's guide to handling reporter requests; NPR/other guidance on comment requests; reporting on recall-petition fights (McKinney TX, Commerce CA snippets); public-comment minutes (Durham, Madera) showing how councils respond; congressional casework limits (CRS); LBJ pressure excerpts and the 2026 interview transcripts from rounds 1 and 2. Blocked or paywalled: Harper's Keller piece, a Latah County senator-at-the-fair article, NPR transcripts of the films, all film captions, Reddit/forum threads (search never surfaced first-person staffer posts). So for most situations below the openings, endings and moves are GENERAL PATTERN built from the verified mechanics plus broad familiarity, marked "(G)". A human with YouTube captions or Reddit access (r/Fedgovernment staffers, r/LocalGovernment, r/politics AMAs, "former staffer here" threads) should check them.

## The big finding for the owner's complaints (applies to all 18)

1. Replies that "kill" the talk are closed: complete, balanced, and final. Real replies are OPEN: they leave a hook (a question back, a missing fact, a name, a time, a condition) so the next turn has something to grab.
2. Real scenes reach different endings because one reply decides which door opens. Seven endings recur: PROMISE (small, dated), FAVOR OWED, TRUST LOST, STORY THAT RUNS, MEETING SET, STALEMATE WITH RESPECT, HANDOFF (to someone else). Write each branch so the ending is earned by a choice, not a stat.
3. Substantive reply = one of: a reason (because the money comes from X), a constraint stated plainly (I can ask, I can't order), a trade-off with a name on it, an admission with a cost, a specific next action with a date, a story from the speaker's own life, or an argument that answers the other person's actual words. Stock reply = general sympathy + general commitment + no new fact.
4. Length: real answers range from one word to a paragraph. Reasoned answers run 25 to 70 words, often in two or three sentences with a "because" and an "and so." Don't cap at one short sentence; do vary.
5. Natural openings are small, oblique, and specific (names, objects, times), not a statement of the topic.

## 1. Funeral: family asks the official to finish what the dead person cared about

Opens (G): Family member, low, after the service: "Dad kept talking about the crossing light on Alder." / Official: "He called my office about it every spring." (Specific object, shared history, no speeches.)
Endings: (a) PROMISE: official commits to one step and a date ("I'll put it on the agenda in March"). (b) HONEST LIMIT: it's the state's road; "I can carry it to them; I can't promise." (c) FAVOR OWED to the family: official takes the folder and a name to call. (d) AWKWARD: family wanted a yes and hears a process; polite, cold exit. (e) STORY THAT RUNS: someone overhears; "he promised at the funeral."
Moves: family states the object, not the policy -> official shares a memory about the dead person -> family names the ask -> official either narrows ("what exactly did he want?") or commits -> close with a small act (take the card).
Substantive: names what the dead person actually wanted and what the official can and can't do about it; mentions a cost. Stock: "He'll be missed; we'll honor his legacy."
Fragments: "He'd have wanted it done right." / "What did he want it to say?" / "I can't do it alone. I can start it." / "Call the office Monday. Ask for Priya." / "Not here. Come see me."

## 2. Laid-off worker the official knows asks for help

Opens (G): "You heard about the plant." / "I heard Friday. I'm sorry, Marcus. How many?"
Endings: PROMISE (call the retraining office and the employer); HANDOFF (workforce board name and number, not a favor); FAVOR (official offers a reference or a call to someone they know, quietly); TRUST LOST (worker asked for a job, official offered a pamphlet); STORY (worker tells the plant crowd the official only gave a number).
Moves: worker states facts (weeks, bills, the kid) -> official asks one factual question (severance? union?) -> worker names the ask or hesitates (pride) -> official offers the real power they have (a call, a hearing, a line on a list) and says the limit -> close with a date.
Substantive: knows the actual local programs and says which one fits; admits a favor has a limit. Stock: "We'll get through this together."
Fragments: "Eleven weeks of pay. After that, nothing." / "Did they say why?" / "I'm not asking for a job. Just a call." / "I'll call Dolores today. No promises on what she says."

## 3. Neighbor facing eviction asks the official to call the landlord

Opens (G): "Can I ask you something and you tell me no if it's wrong?" / "Ask."
Reality (verified, CRS casework): staff can intervene, ask an agency to look, advocate; they cannot force an outcome; evictions are mostly private landlord-tenant disputes, so the official has no authority. Councils often answer public comment with "staff will follow up."
Endings: (a) CALL MADE: official phones, the landlord hears "the council member is calling" and the pressure itself is the favor (and a risk: it looks like leaning). (b) REFERRAL: legal aid, rental assistance, a date for the hearing. (c) HONEST REFUSAL: "If I call, it could make it worse; here is what actually works." (d) TRUST LOST: promise then nothing. (e) FAVOR OWED in the other direction (neighbor later volunteers).
Moves: neighbor gives the date and number -> official asks "have you got the notice? Is there a hearing date?" -> neighbor explains why (hours cut) -> official lays out choices with a trade-off -> neighbor chooses -> official names the next action and when they'll report back.
Substantive: the official reads the notice, names the hearing date, and gives a real reason a call could help or hurt. Stock: "I'll look into it."
Fragments: "Thursday the 14th. That's the hearing." / "Has he said what he wants?" / "If I call, he knows you came to me." / "Bring the notice. I'll read it with you."

## 4. Reporter calls for comment before a scandal story runs

Verified: comment requests come with a deadline; offices pause, assess the story's origin, route to policy staff, get senior sign-off, set red lines; "no comment at this time" is a legitimate answer if facts will change; relationships built in quiet times make a correction easier; brush-offs and slow, process-heavy replies backfire; reporters push anyway; outlets state exactly who declined. Offices that ignore, then attack the messenger afterward, produce a second story.
Opens: reporter: "I'm working on a piece about X and I want to give you a chance to respond. I'm filing at five." / Official: "What's the specific claim?"
Endings: STORY RUNS WITH A STATEMENT; STORY HELD (a fact the reporter hadn't got is supplied); STORY RUNS ANGRIER (declined or attacked the source); CLARIFICATION (partial correction accepted); OFF-THE-RECORD explanation that shifts the angle.
Moves: reporter lists claims -> official asks for the exact wording and sources of the claims -> official corrects one fact with a document -> declines others -> reporter asks the follow-up they actually care about -> official picks: statement, call-back time, or lawyer.
Substantive: corrects a specific fact and offers a document; admits what is true. Stock: "I take these allegations seriously and look forward to cooperating."
Fragments: "Who's your source on the date?" / "I can send you the minutes." / "That part's wrong. The rest, I'll get back to you." / "Five is fine. I'll call at four-thirty." / "Are you recording this?"

## 5. Recall organizer confronts the official (door, event, table)

Verified: recall fights feature accusations of intimidation (being followed, filmed at tables), a councilwoman allegedly telling a signer "don't sign that petition," officials publicly saying residents may sign or decline free of pressure, opponents packing city hall.
Opens (G): organizer: "You got a minute? I'm not here to yell." / Official: "I've got a minute. Is that the petition?"
Endings: STALEMATE WITH RESPECT; TRUST LOST (official says "don't sign it" and a witness films it); STORY RUNS (incident clip); OFFICIAL CONVERTS one signer by answering a specific grievance; MEETING SET (public forum, or sit down at the clerk's office).
Moves: organizer names the one reason on the petition -> official corrects a fact or defends the vote with a reason -> organizer asks for a commitment (reverse? resign?) -> official refuses the commitment but offers a concrete thing -> both separate; one reminds the other of the rules.
Substantive: the official addresses the actual reason on the petition (the vote date, the fee) and admits what they'd do differently. Stock: "I respect your right to petition."
Fragments: "It's your right. Don't tell people it's a scam." / "You voted for the fee." / "I did. Here is why." / "Then say that at the forum." / "I'm filming this, by the way."

## 6. Resident testifies at a hearing and demands an answer

Verified: public-comment rules often bar the body from responding to off-agenda items; the usual result is "staff will follow up"; groups complain of silence. Human version: a council member can still respond with something concrete or a promise on the record.
Opens: Resident, at the mic, two minutes on the clock: "I'm here for the third time." / Chair: "Thank you. Your time is up, ma'am."
Endings: (a) ANSWER ON THE RECORD (a member says what they'll do and when). (b) STAFF FOLLOW-UP (resident gets a card and a name). (c) NOTHING (resident leaves; clip posts). (d) MEETING SET (member catches resident in the hallway). (e) COUNTERED (another member rebuts).
Moves: resident states facts, a number, a name -> chair cites rules -> a member breaks ranks ("can staff tell us...?") -> staff gives a date -> resident asks for it in writing.
Substantive: resident brings a number and a request with a deadline; official's answer includes who and when. Stock: "Thank you for your comments."
Fragments: "That's two minutes." / "I'd like an answer, not a thank you." / "Ms. Reyes, I'll walk out with you." / "Staff, by Friday?" / "In writing, please."

## 7. Constituent hurt by a law the official voted for

Opens (G): "You voted for it." / "I did."
Endings: (a) OWNED (official admits the cost, explains the reason, offers a fix or a hardship path). (b) DEFENDED (official argues the law's purpose; constituent leaves unconvinced; respect or not). (c) TRUST LOST. (d) FAVOR: official finds the exception or waiver. (e) STORY: constituent goes to the press.
Moves: constituent states the specific harm (dollars, date) -> official asks details and doesn't defend yet -> constituent: "you voted for it" -> official owns the vote and gives the actual reason (trade-offs, who else it helps) -> constituent counters -> official offers one concrete remedy or admits there is none.
Substantive: reason plus cost named plus remedy or honest "none." Stock: "I understand your frustration; the law was complex."
Fragments: "It cost me four hundred a month." / "I voted for it. Here's what it was trading against." / "That's not an answer for my kids." / "No. It isn't. Let me see if there's a waiver."

## 8. Colleague accuses the official of a conflict of interest

Opens (G): "Before we vote, I want to ask whether your brother-in-law's firm is on this contract." / "It's on the list. I disclosed it in March."
Endings: RECUSAL (official steps out); DEFIANT (stays, wins vote, loses allies); CLEARED (counsel opinion produced); STORY RUNS (press picks it up); APOLOGY (official concedes poor judgment).
Moves: accuser asks a yes/no with a date -> official answers the narrow question and cites the disclosure -> accuser widens ("then why vote?") -> official argues the line of the rule, or concedes appearance -> chair rules or asks counsel -> close with an action.
Substantive: the official produces the specific disclosure and addresses appearance vs. rule. Stock: "There is no conflict; I act in the public interest."
Fragments: "Is the firm on the bid list?" / "It is. I disclosed it." / "That's not what I asked." / "Counsel, can I vote?" / "I'll step out. Not because I'm guilty."

## 9. Friend feels betrayed by the official's vote

Opens (G): friend doesn't start with the vote: "You didn't call me back." / "I know. I was going to."
Endings: REPAIR (official explains and apologizes for how, not what); RIFT (friend says "I don't know you anymore"); COOLED TRUST (friendship continues, politics off the table); FAVOR OWED by the official (a promise to make it right on the next vote); STORY (friend tells others).
Moves: friend names the personal cost, not the policy -> official starts to explain, friend cuts off -> official stops explaining and asks what it cost -> friend says -> official gives one honest reason (not the press line) -> friend decides.
Substantive: the official owns the "how" ("you should have heard it from me") and gives the real reason. Stock: "I hope you can understand it was a difficult decision."
Fragments: "I heard it on the radio." / "That's on me." / "Don't explain it to me like I'm the council." / "You really wanted to know why? Okay." / "I need some time, Ben."

## 10. Former ally who broke with the official wants back in

Opens (G): "I saw your numbers on the Hunter bill." / "I saw yours."
Endings: WELCOME BACK (conditional); COLD (door closed, with a reason); TERMS (specific act first, such as a public statement or a vote); STORY (the reunion becomes news); STALL ("let's talk after the election").
Moves: ex-ally makes a small non-political remark -> official lets silence sit -> ex-ally says what changed (new facts) -> official names the cost of the break and what repair would look like -> ex-ally agrees or bargains -> official sets a test.
Substantive: mentions the specific break event and the specific repair. Stock: "Water under the bridge."
Fragments: "I was wrong about the budget." / "You were loud about it." / "What changed?" / "Do it at the Tuesday meeting. Then we'll talk."

## 11. Staffer apologizes for a mistake (wrong number in a speech, missed deadline)

Opens (G): "I need to tell you something before the event." / "How bad?"
Endings: FORGIVEN with a fix (correct on the record); DRESSING-DOWN (cold, short); STAFFER OWNS it publicly; COVER-UP temptation resisted; TRUST RAISED (staffer who tells early gets more responsibility).
Moves: staffer states fact first (what, how big, who knows) -> official asks "can we fix it before it's out?" -> staffer proposes the fix -> official picks and assigns -> official says one line about the cause (not a lecture).
Substantive: staffer arrives with the fix, not an excuse; official asks a practical question. Stock: "I am so sorry; I take full responsibility."
Fragments: "I used the 2019 number." / "Who's seen it?" / "I can send a correction by noon." / "Do that. And tell me next time at nine, not at five."

## 12. Staffer asks the official's blessing to run for office

Opens (G): "I'm thinking about something and I want you to hear it from me." / "Is this the school board?"
Endings: BLESSING with conditions (timing, not against a friend); NEUTRAL ("I can't endorse; I won't stand in your way"); WARNING (honest cost, spouse, money); LOSS OF A STAFFER (a transition plan); RIVALRY (official realizes it hurts them).
Moves: staffer states the office, the reason, timing -> official asks who else, money, spouse -> official gives their own war story or cost -> staffer asks directly for the blessing -> official answers with a condition or says no.
Substantive: the official asks about money and spouse and tells a true story from their first race. Stock: "I'm so proud of you; you'll do great."
Fragments: "Seat's open in November." / "Have you talked to Jen?" / "My first race cost me a house." / "I can't endorse you. I can lend you the van."

## 13. Police chief warns of a protest and asks the official to cancel an event

Opens (G): "I need ten minutes. Not on the phone." / "Sit."
Endings: EVENT CANCELED; MOVED (new place or time); GOES AHEAD with a plan (officers, route); CHIEF OVERRIDDEN and later blamed; STORY RUNS (leaked: "chief asked mayor to cancel").
Moves: chief gives facts (numbers, source, route) and a recommendation -> official asks what the chief needs to hold the event safely -> chief gives a number (officers/cost) -> official weighs politics aloud -> official decides and asks the chief to put the risk in writing.
Substantive: the chief names specifics and a cost; the official asks what changes the risk. Stock: "Public safety is our top priority."
Fragments: "Two hundred, maybe more, from out of town." / "What do you need to keep it safe?" / "Eight more officers and the south lot." / "Put the risk in writing for me."

## 14. Executive's office offers the official an appointment to a commission

Opens (G): "The governor's office called." / "About what?"
Endings: ACCEPT (with strings: staff, schedule); DECLINE (conflict with own seat); STALL (asks for terms); OWED (a favor to the executive); STORY RUNS ("offered a seat to quiet a critic").
Moves: executive aide states the offer lightly -> official asks the real question (what does it come with? what do they expect?) -> aide names expectations obliquely -> official sets terms or asks for time -> both leave with a call back.
Substantive: the official asks who benefits, what they would be asked to vote on, and what independence they keep. Stock: "I'd be honored."
Fragments: "It's unpaid, meets monthly." / "Who else is on it?" / "They'd like you in by Friday." / "I'll tell you Tuesday."

## 15. On the floor mid-vote, a leader asks the official to switch

Verified (LBJ excerpts, oral histories): the ask arrives as a complaint-question ("can't you go with us"); reciprocity or past-vote rebuke; blunt order; the other side answers with a short line or a laugh; counters speak to the member, not staff; votes flip overnight.
Opens: leader, at the rail, low: "I need you on this one." / Member: "You've got me down no."
Endings: SWITCH (with a trade named); HOLD (member stays, accepts penalty); ABSTAIN/PASS (a middle exit); TRUST LOST (leader threatens, member remembers); FAVOR OWED (member switches for a committee seat or a district item).
Moves: leader asks -> member says why they're no (a reason tied to district) -> leader names what's at stake with a count ("I'm two short") -> member asks for something concrete -> leader gives or refuses -> clock forces a choice.
Substantive: both sides use numbers and a price; refusals cite the district. Stock: "I appreciate your leadership but I must vote my conscience."
Fragments: "I'm two short." / "Then you need someone who's not me." / "What do you need?" / "The bridge money back in." / "Done. Don't look at me when it posts."

## 16. Public argument with a neighbor over a local law (grocery store, ballfield)

Verified-ish: the Iowa fair shows the pattern: an interrupter, a short slogan, the official sets a rule ("he asks, I answer"), the crowd turns, the encounter ends with no resolution and the official leaves for something ordinary (food).
Opens (G): "So you're the one who voted for the leash law." / "That's me. Hi."
Endings: STALEMATE WITH A JOKE; ESCALATION (someone films; a story runs); QUIET CONVERSION (the neighbor hears a fact: "the dog bite numbers"); NEIGHBOR LEAVES IN ANGER; OFFER (come to office hours).
Moves: neighbor attacks with a personal example -> official asks a question about it -> neighbor tells a fuller story -> official gives a reason in plain words -> neighbor pushes ("so I'm wrong?") -> official refuses to win ("we see it differently") and offers a meeting, or the bystander cuts in.
Substantive: official uses the neighbor's actual example and concedes the part that's true. Stock: "I hear you and I respect your opinion."
Fragments: "Eleven years I've walked that field." / "Which dog was it?" / "You think I wanted this?" / "Come to the Thursday meeting. I'll sit by you."

## 17. The opponent who walks up at a rally / county fair / forum

Verified: Romney at Iowa fair: an opponent's side in the crowd, a short challenge, a statement of principle, repeated shouts, a rule-setting turn ("one question, one answer"), no resolution, official leaves for photo-friendly activity and declines to discuss the clash afterward. At candidate forums the pattern is a polite jab ("we've never met") followed by a policy dig.
Opens (G): "Thanks for coming out. We haven't met since the filing." / "No. Good luck, truly."
Endings: HANDSHAKE FOR THE CAMERAS (nothing changes); A JAB THAT RUNS (a line clipped for ads); A QUIET TRUCE (agree on a joint event); A DEAL (e.g., agree to a debate); AN INSULT (storms off).
Moves: opponent opens with courtesy -> inserts a small barb -> candidate answers the barb with a fact -> opponent names their issue -> candidate ends by turning to the nearest voter.
Substantive: the barb cites something checkable (a vote, a number). Stock: "May the best candidate win."
Fragments: "Nice crowd. Mine's smaller." / "You skipped the forum." / "I was at the plant that morning." / "Let's do the debate then." / (turns to a voter) "Ma'am, I'm sorry, you were saying?"

## 18. Volunteer right after a rally

Evidence: thin (local accounts: candidate greets volunteers one by one, then sets the day's plan; first-timers nervous; staff, not candidate, make the actual ask; person-by-person phone asks about what the volunteer wants to do; campaign work talk is tools, food, snacks, signs). No captions reachable.
Opens (G): "Is it always this loud?" / "Only when it's going well."
Endings: SIGNED UP (slot and place); HANDED OFF (named staffer); DECLINES POLITELY (can only give money or a sign); A BIGGER ASK (hosts a house party); A STORY (volunteer later tells the candidate the line that landed).
Moves: volunteer opens with a small observation -> candidate asks a name/where from -> volunteer tells one fact (a sister, a union card, a truck) -> candidate hands off to a named staffer -> staffer proposes one slot -> volunteer sets a limit -> candidate adds a short thanks.
Substantive: volunteer offers a specific skill/time and sets limits; candidate responds to that specific skill. Stock: "Thank you so much for your support!"
Fragments: "I counted forty chairs, we ran out." / "Where'd you drive from?" / "Dana has the clipboard." / "I can do Saturday morning, two hours." / "Bring water, and don't argue with dogs."

## Cross-cutting rules (updated)

1. Give every reply a hook: a fact, name, date, condition, or question that the next speaker can pick up. Closed, complete replies kill scenes.
2. Allow reasoned replies of 25 to 70 words: a reason, a constraint, a trade-off, a cost; mix with one-word and fragment turns.
3. Openings are small and specific, not the topic sentence. Start with an object, a time, a name, a minor grievance.
4. A scene should reach at least three different endings (promise, limit, favor owed, trust lost, story runs, meeting set, handoff); the reply chosen decides which one.
5. Constraints are stated plainly ("I can ask; I can't order"), with who has the power named.
6. Admissions cost something and are followed by an action (fix, correction, recusal), not an apology speech.
7. Pressure shows as bookkeeping, a count, a deadline, a film camera, or silence, not a speech about stakes.
8. Official says what they will do and when, or says they won't, or says what changes their mind. Never "I'll look into it" alone.
9. Don't explain feelings; show them by what the person asks next or won't say.
10. Replies answer the other person's last specific words, not the topic in general.
11. Endings are practical and abrupt (a time, a name, a door, a turn to someone else), occasionally awkward; some end unresolved with respect.
12. Never have the other side agree because the speech was good; agreement follows a price, a fact, or an earned trust.
