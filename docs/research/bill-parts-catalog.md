# Real bills are built from about sixty kinds of parts, and the game uses about fifteen

Lead. I read 222 enacted laws and ordinances and broke each one into parts. They use 61 distinct kinds of part. Eight parts carry most bills: an amount, a prohibition, a duty, an effective date, a covered group, a rate, an exemption and an administering agency. The game's 42 registered bill variants handle spending, eligibility widening, reporting, fees and sunsets well. They cannot yet change a tax rate, ban or punish anything, narrow who qualifies, override a local government, or cap a private price. Those five patterns appear in 128 of the 222 measures. No owner decision is needed to use this catalog; the five gaps in section 5 are candidates for the next bill-building work.

## What the sample is

- 222 measures: 32 federal, 158 state (all 50 states, three or four each), 2 District of Columbia, 9 territory, 16 city and 5 county.
- Every one of the 18 subject menus appears. Every operation appears at least 13 times: create 64, restrict 49, expand 43, lower 21, replace 19, raise 13, repeal 13.
- Each measure's record lists its parts, the numbers in each part with units, who it touches, and one line on daily life.
- A part marked "inferred" came from memory or a secondary summary, not from the official text. 181 of the 753 parts carry that mark.
- Each record states how it was sourced. 43 were read in the official text. 106 had their bill page, act number or signing found on an official site. 53 rest on news or official press releases. 20 have details inferred from memory and should be checked before anyone relies on the numbers.

Most state bills come from the 2025 regular sessions. That is the most recent session I could confirm for all 50 states, and four states (Montana, Nevada, North Dakota, Texas) held no regular session in 2026. A few 2026 laws are included where they were easy to confirm. Federal laws run from June 2023 to September 18, 2026.

## 2. The 18 menus

Counts are measures filed under the menu first. "Also" counts measures that touch it second.

**01 Infrastructure** (8, also 4). Instruments: appropriation, program authorization, tax, fee. Operations: create and expand; one raise. Recipes:

- A fuel tax or fee increase with a revenue split between state, counties and cities (Oregon HB 3991).
- Bonds put to voters with named projects (Denver).
- State borrowing handed to towns by formula (Massachusetts Chapter 17).
- New tolling authority that still needs federal approval (Indiana HEA 1461).

Gap: no transit fare, rail or port law at state or local level.

**02 Healthcare** (19, also 13). Instruments: eligibility, mandate, prohibition. Operations: mostly restrict (12 of 19). Recipes:

- A cost cap on insured patients ($35 insulin in California and Nevada).
- A ban on a business practice by pharmacy benefit managers with regulator enforcement (Arkansas, Maine, Alabama).
- Medicaid eligibility narrowed by work hours, premiums or removal of a group (federal, New Hampshire, Minnesota, Idaho).
- A sunset removed so a program becomes permanent (Montana).

Gap: no hospital budget or health workforce funding bill with numbers.

**03 Education** (16, all state). Instruments: prohibition, program creation, mandate. Recipes:

- School phone bans with local policy deadlines (Alabama, Tennessee, North Dakota).
- School choice money per student with a cap, an income priority and a named agency (Texas, Tennessee, Wyoming, Idaho).
- Bans on DEI offices at public colleges (Kentucky, Ohio, Mississippi).
- Formula changes (Alaska's $700 per student).

Gap: no federal education law and no student aid or teacher pay law.

**04 Taxes & Public Finance** (55, also 20). The largest menu. Instruments: tax (31) and appropriation (21). Operations: create 20 (budgets), lower 18. Recipes:

- A flat rate cut with future cuts tied to a revenue trigger (Georgia, Kentucky, Oklahoma, Mississippi).
- A property tax credit paid from a state fund plus a cap on local levy growth (North Dakota, Indiana).
- A new excise tax with an earmark (Hawaii, Michigan, Illinois).
- A tax holiday or one-time rebate from surplus (Georgia).
- A budget act with line-item vetoes and a fiscal-year availability window (Ohio, South Carolina, Maryland).

Gap: no debt-limit or state borrowing cap measure outside the federal Fiscal Responsibility Act.

**05 Assistance & Social Insurance** (8, also 13). Instruments: eligibility and appropriation. Operations: restrict 4 of 8. Recipes:

- A state asks for a federal waiver to change what benefits buy (Idaho SNAP soda ban).
- Emergency cash to bridge a federal lapse (Virgin Islands Act 9047, Guam PL 38-75).
- Funding rules that shift cost to states by error rate (SNAP cost share).

Gap: no unemployment benefit amount change and no disability or elder care program in the sample.

**06 Housing & Land Use** (14, also 3). Local governments lead here: 7 of 14 are city or county. Recipes:

- The state overrides local zoning near transit or on commercial land (California SB 79, Nevada AB 241, New Mexico SB 96).
- A city replaces its whole development code (Bozeman, Missoula).
- A rent cap with a notice period and a new-construction exemption (Washington).
- A tax break in exchange for affordable units (Montgomery County).

Gap: no homebuyer assistance program.

**07 Labor & Employment** (20, also 3). Instruments: mandate (10) and benefit (6). Recipes:

- A wage floor phased in over years with inflation indexing (Michigan, Los Angeles, New York City).
- Leave in days or weeks keyed to employer size (Illinois NICU leave, Colorado, Iowa, Mississippi).
- Unemployment pay for strikers with a week cap (Oregon 10 weeks, Washington 6 weeks).
- A voter-passed law narrowed before it starts (Nebraska LB 415).

Gap: no workers' compensation law.

**08 Business & Economic Development** (8, also 11). Instruments: program creation, fee, license. Recipes:

- A license with reserve rules and a size split between federal and state oversight (GENIUS Act).
- An insurance backstop and loan program (Hawaii).
- Special tax and zoning zones for data centers with a revenue split (West Virginia HB 2014).

Gap: no general business license or corporate tax change at state level.

**09 Energy & Environment** (12, also 6). Instruments: mandate, prohibition, agency. Recipes:

- Permit deadlines for power plants (South Carolina's 6-month rule).
- A new energy department that absorbs an old office (Maine).
- Bonds to take charges off utility bills (Connecticut).
- The federal government voids a state emission waiver (Pub. L. 119-15).

Gap: no water quality or climate emissions target law.

**10 Justice & Public Safety** (15, also 12). Instruments: penalty, prohibition, procedure. Recipes:

- A new crime with a definition and a felony class (Alabama conversion devices, Wisconsin sextortion).
- Mandatory jail minimums (Oklahoma DUI).
- Pretrial release rules tied to prior convictions (North Carolina).
- A firearm sales ban with a grandfather clause and a one-year delay (Rhode Island).
- A local sales tax for jails put to voters (Kansas City).

Gap: no juvenile justice or fire and EMS law.

**11 Government & Democracy** (9, also 11). Instruments: procedure and election rules. Operations: replace 4 of 9. Recipes:

- A ballot design change (New Jersey office-block ballots).
- Harder citizen petitions (Florida HB 1205).
- A legislature taking approval power over agency rules (Kentucky).
- A public records release with a deadline (Epstein Files Act).

Gap: no redistricting or ethics commission law recorded.

**12 Rights, Family & Consumer Protection** (4, also 13). Mostly appears as a second menu. Recipes:

- An "unfair or abusive practice" power for the attorney general (New York FAIR Act).
- All-in price disclosure (Colorado junk fees).
- A protected class removed (Iowa SF 418).

Gap: no marriage, custody, adoption or child welfare law.

**13 Science, Technology & Communications** (8, also 6). Instruments: mandate and prohibition. Operations: create 5 of 8. Recipes:

- Disclose a safety plan and report incidents within a set time (California SB 53, New York RAISE Act).
- Age checks with parental consent (Utah app stores, Nebraska social media).
- A start date pushed back with no other change (Colorado SB25B-004).

Gap: no state cybersecurity or government digital services law.

**14 Agriculture & Food** (8, also 4). Operations: restrict 4 of 8. Recipes:

- Bans on food dyes or additives with a phase-in, schools first (Virginia, West Virginia, Arizona).
- Per-acre aid to farmers after disasters (American Relief Act).

Gap: no crop insurance, forestry or fisheries law.

**15 Parks, Culture & Community Life** (4, also 2). Recipes:

- A ban on development inside state parks (Florida HB 209).
- Bonds for libraries, museums and a zoo put to voters (Denver).
- An arts agency merged into tourism (West Virginia).

Gap: thin. I found no state library, historic preservation or arts funding law I could confirm.

**16 Immigration & Citizenship** (6, also 3). Recipes:

- Mandatory detention on arrest (Laken Riley Act).
- Required state and county cooperation with federal agents plus grants per county (Texas SB 8, Florida SB 2-C).
- A ban on local "sanctuary" policies (South Dakota).

Gap: no settlement or integration services law; visas and naturalization are federal only.

**17 Defense & Veterans** (5, also 4). Recipes:

- Annual defense authorization tables (NDAA).
- Veterans' property tax exemption at a lower disability rating (Washington) or as a local option (New York).
- Higher caps on home care spending (Dole Act).

Gap: states pass few defense laws; base-adjacent land rules are the main state lever (Kansas SB 9).

**18 Foreign Affairs & Trade** (3, also 3). Recipes:

- Sanctions with tariff rates up to 500%, a presidential waiver and a 5-year sunset (Pub. L. 119-111).
- Emergency foreign aid (Pub. L. 118-50). State proxies exist only as foreign land-purchase bans (Texas SB 17).

Gap: expected, since states cannot conduct foreign policy.

## 3. How levels differ

**Federal.** Federal laws are the largest and the most bundled. One act can hold a tax cut, a Medicaid rule and a SNAP formula (Pub. L. 119-21). Congress also has instruments states do not: continuing resolutions, rescissions, reconciliation, and one-sentence resolutions that void an agency rule. Federal laws usually authorize first and appropriate later; the NDAA sets ceilings that a separate bill funds.

**State.** States pass most of the sample's taxes, wage rules, bans and eligibility changes. Common state-only parts: a revenue trigger for future tax cuts, a veto override (5 state overrides in the sample), line-item vetoes on budgets, and a default effective date such as July 1. States also reach down: 13 measures override or limit local government.

**D.C. and territories.** They look like small states with a larger overseer. D.C. put a tipped wage change inside its budget support act. Guam enacted its budget over the governor's veto. The Northern Mariana Islands repealed and re-enacted a budget three months into the year. Two territories paid emergency food aid when federal SNAP lapsed.

**Cities and counties.** What they did in this sample: set property tax rates within a state cap, hold bond and tax elections, set wage floors for some industries, license and zone, and ban specific products. What they could not do: pass their own felonies, borrow without voters in Colorado (TABOR), raise Texas property taxes past the state voter-approval rate, or keep local choice once a state preempted it. Utah and Florida removed local choice on fluoride. Missouri took the St. Louis police away from the city. Texas required every county sheriff to work with federal immigration agents. Cities also face citizen referendums: Los Angeles's hotel wage was suspended by a petition. The limits above are inferred from these cases, not from a survey of state law.

## 4. Timing

- 42 measures have both an exact signing date and an exact start date. The median gap is about 78 days. 9 took effect within a day. The longest are Virginia's school dye ban at 832 days and Alexandria's leaf blower ban at 549 days.
- State laws cluster in spring. 96 of 158 state measures were signed between March and June.
- Common default dates: July 1 (start of most state fiscal years), January 1 (tax years and wage steps), and 90 days after adjournment in some states.
- Phase-ins run 2 to 14 years. Wage floors step up once a year. Taxes step down once a year, often only if revenue grows (Mississippi's income tax falls to 3% by 2030, then toward zero).
- Sunsets run 3 to 20 years. Tax cuts and sanctions end on a date unless renewed. Removing a sunset is itself a common bill (Montana HB 245).
- Some laws are enacted but not in force. Mississippi's DEI ban and Wyoming's scholarships were halted by courts. Oregon's gas tax and Los Angeles's wage were paused by referendum petitions. The game should keep "enacted" and "in force" as separate states.
- Some laws exist only to move a date. Colorado spent a special session delaying its AI law by five months.

## 5. The game's current bank against real patterns

I count 42 registered variants in 20 families, using 8 instruments, plus one transit appropriation template. The brief said 46; the difference may be on a branch I did not read.

**Variants that match real patterns (measured against the sample):**

- `appropriations` single, supplemental and local-match variants (`src/simulation/legislation-fiscal-families.ts:107`). These match budget acts, supplemental bills (Delaware HB 226, Guam PL 38-59) and cost-share rules (WRDA 2024).
- `program-sunset` terminate, extend and repeal (`legislation-fiscal-families.ts:671`). These match federal extenders, Montana's sunset removal and the Social Security Fairness Act.
- `service-charges` flat fee and dedicated surcharge (`legislation-fiscal-families.ts:1125`). These match Missoula's rental fee, Chicago's bag fee and Hawaii's earmarked green fee.
- `assistance-eligibility` raise the limit, add a category, index the limit (`legislation-administration-families.ts:116`). These match Arkansas's presumptive eligibility and Washington's veterans threshold.
- `public-workforce` posts and classification standards (`legislation-administration-families.ts:1184`). North Carolina SB 124, which cut degree requirements for state jobs, is a direct match for the classification variant.
- `broadband-access` buildout to unserved areas (`legislation-infrastructure-families.ts:750`). This matches the Rural Broadband Protection Act's vetting and service standards.
- `disaster-recovery` grants and waivers (`legislation-resilience-families.ts:71`). These match the American Relief Act and the Virgin Islands tax amnesty.
- `agency-reporting` report, audit and measures (`legislation-administration-families.ts:680`). Real bills use reporting as a part inside larger bills. It is rarely a bill by itself (inferred from the sample).

**Common real patterns with no variant yet.** The five most important, ranked by how often they appear. Together they appear in 128 of the 222 measures.

1. **Tax rate and base changes.** 42 measures change a tax. The game's revenue instrument imposes charges and surcharges but has no income, sales or property tax rate, bracket, credit, deduction or exemption. Recipe to build: rate, phase-in, trigger, exemption, effective date.
2. **Bans with penalties.** 30 measures prohibit something and 17 set a penalty. The game has no prohibition or penalty instrument. Recipe: definition, prohibition, exemption, penalty, enforcement, grandfather.
3. **Narrowing eligibility.** 9 measures restrict who qualifies or add a condition (work hours, premiums, removing a group). All three game eligibility variants only widen. Recipe: covered population, work requirement or premium, exemption, effective date, administering agency.
4. **Preemption and local options.** 14 measures override a lower government and 5 give them a choice. The game has no way for a state bill to limit or empower cities and counties. Recipe: preemption, local option, levy cap, deadline for local ordinances.
5. **Caps and floors on private prices and wages.** Rent caps, insulin caps, wage floors, overdraft fee limits and leave mandates appear in about 20 measures. The game's regulatory variants reach water systems, utilities and agencies, not private landlords or employers. Recipe: covered population with a size threshold, cap or floor, indexation, phase-in, notice, enforcement.

Other real patterns without a variant: borrowing and bond questions; agency creation, merger or takeover; license and permit systems; election and procedure rules; one-time rebates; continuing resolutions and rescissions; laws that only change a date; veto overrides; referendum suspension.

## What happens next

The catalog does not change the game. It is input for the legislation owners when they scope the next bill families. The 20 records marked "inferred-details" need a check against official text before their numbers feed any game value. Real data calibrates the game only; the game generates its own laws.

Method. Research ran September 26, 2026. Sources were official legislature, Congress and city sites, GovInfo for federal text, and news or press releases where official pages blocked automated reading (LegiScan, Congress.gov, and New York, Hawaii and Colorado legislature pages refused it). No accounts or API keys were used. American Samoa is missing: I found no 2025 or 2026 law I could confirm. Records and sources are in `data/research/bill-samples/`, one file per jurisdiction.
