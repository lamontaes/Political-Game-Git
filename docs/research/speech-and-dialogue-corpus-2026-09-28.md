## The register cards

Each card lists moves, not finished lines. "Measured" means counted from the sources below; "use in the game" is an inference.

- **May ship word for word:** formulas from the Congressional Record, federal hearings and White House briefings, which are public domain.
- **Patterns only:** formulas from the Pennsylvania Journal and MeetingBank.

1. **House floor, one-minute period.**
   - Measured: 295 of 298 open by addressing the chair. 58% are tributes, 11% mix tribute with a party jab, 31% are arguments. "I rise" opens 71% of tributes and 34% of arguments. 73% include a figure, date or year; 21% make an explicit ask. Median 180 words.
   - Formulas: "Mr. Speaker," "Madam Speaker," "I rise today to honor," "the gentleman from Ohio," "the gentlewoman from Texas."
   - Use: the member picks tribute or argument from what happened at home and their own record; only an argument names the other party.
2. **House floor, debate.**
   - Formulas: "I yield myself such time as I may consume," "Will the gentleman yield?," "Reclaiming my time," "I ask unanimous consent to revise and extend my remarks," "I yield back the balance of my time."
   - Attacks go through the bill or "the majority," rarely the person by name.
3. **Senate floor.**
   - Measured over 548 long turns: 86% open "Mr. President" or "Madam President." 67% name a party, the administration or a leader. 28% answer another senator. 34% end "I yield the floor." Median 695 words; 13% run 1,500 words or more.
   - Five senior senators in leadership roles give 31% of speeches. Of 639 turns under 150 words, 41% ask unanimous consent.
   - Formulas: "the Senator from Maine," "my colleague," "I suggest the absence of a quorum," "I ask unanimous consent that."
   - Use: floor time by role and seniority; a speech can open by answering the last speaker.
4. **Committee questioning.**
   - Measured: 73% open with thanks. Only 6% ask "yes or no," and 5% cut a witness off. 20% mention home. 47% end "I yield back." Median turn 482 words over 956 turns.
   - Witnesses hedge with "I think" or "I believe" (64%), thank the member (23%), don't know or recall (10%), offer to follow up (8%), say it's too early (2%).
   - Use: a hostile turn needs a reason on record (an opposing official, a scandal, the member's stake); otherwise questioning is courteous.
5. **State legislature floor (Pennsylvania House).**
   - Measured: 82% of member turns of 40+ words open "Thank you, Mr. Speaker." 61% end "Thank you." 20% name a county or "my district." 12% ask for a yes or no vote. 18% name a party or the Governor. Median 189 words.
   - The chair recognizes members by county ("the gentleman from Lackawanna").
   - Use: a state floor is more courteous and more local than Congress, and members are named by county.
6. **City council public comment.**
   - Measured: every speaker gives a name; about 4 in 5 greet the body; about 3 in 4 give a district, neighborhood, years lived, or renter or owner; about 1 in 3 speak for a group; about half tell a personal story and half cite a number; about 3 in 5 end on an ask and 9 in 10 on "Thank you." Anger in about 1 in 12. Electoral threats in 4 of 287. Median about 410 words.
   - Chair: "Next speaker, please," "Your time begins now."
   - Council business: "through the chair," "move to approve," "second," "all in favor," "motion carries."
   - Use: greet, name, place, optional group, story or number, ask, "Thank you." Temper and stake decide whether the rare shaming line appears.
7. **Press briefing.**
   - Measured: median question 14 words, median answer turn 17. A third of answer turns are four words or fewer; a fifth run 150+. About 1 in 8 questions stack; about 1 in 7 are short follow-ups. About 1 in 4 substantive answers use a set evasion: "I'd refer you to," "as I said," "I'm not going to get ahead of," "I don't have anything to share," "I'm not going to speak to," "that's a hypothetical."
   - Use: the secretary's knowledge and the day's news decide which evasion fits.
8. **Town hall** (small sample: 7 televised town halls, 33 questions).
   - A question runs a median of 46 words and the answer 151. Few questioners greet or introduce themselves. Answers speak to "you" (23 of 33), commit (9), draw on the official's own family (5), name the other party (6).
   - Draft: a constituent town hall without TV is looser, with applause, anger, interruptions, and "talk to my staff after."
9. **Rally** (draft): local references first, a callback line the crowd knows, contrasts and three-part lists, the opponent's name as a cue for booing, a story about one person, the ask to vote or volunteer. U.S. crowds cheer and shout more than they clap together.
10. **Diner or workplace small talk** (Santa Barbara corpus, 2,659 turns):
    - Median turn 4 words; 45% three words or fewer; 69% overlap; 7% laughter; 9% questions; swearing about 1.3 per 1,000 words.
    - Topics: weather, prices, sports, road work, people everyone knows. Politics comes in sideways through a price or a neighbor's problem. People leave without a formal goodbye.
11. **Family kitchen** (Santa Barbara corpus, 6,795 turns):
    - Median turn 4 words; 48% three words or fewer; 73% overlap; 15% laughter; 10% questions; swearing about 0.8 per 1,000.
    - Patterns: chores as the frame ("hand me that"), questions as directives ("Did you call your grandmother?"), stories told by two or three people with corrections, old grievances in passing, nicknames.
12. **Eulogy** (draft): how the speaker knew the person, one or two stories with a small detail, a laugh early, the person's own words if recorded, what they leave behind, a direct word to the family. No policy.

## How a speech becomes memorable (the simulation's version)

1. **The moment:** a recorded, high-salience world event the audience knows about.
2. **Standing and risk:** the speaker's record, relationships with the audience's groups, and any warning they overrode, recorded as fact. (RFK in Indianapolis, April 4, 1968.)
3. **Words from the speaker's own life:** a personal disclosure only from the speaker's own history events.
4. **An outcome people credit:** listeners may later form a belief crediting the speech; it is a belief, and it can be wrong.
5. **Carriers:** witnesses, recordings, outlets and retellers, through the knowledge records (direct, told by, media, rumor).
6. **Retelling, quotation, commemoration:** later speakers quote it; councils name things after it through ordinary measures; anniversaries prompt retelling.
7. **Age at the time:** people aged about 10 to 30 keep a stronger memory.
8. **Family passes it down:** an heir gets weaker "told by" knowledge.
9. **Infamy works the same way:** a gaffe replayed on TV travels through the same carriers.

**Test cases the system should be able to produce:**

- RFK in Indianapolis, 1968; counter-test: the same speech on a quiet evening is forgotten within months.
- Gettysburg, 1863: modest reception, then growth through later quotation.
- The fireside chats: scarcity raises the impact of each address.
- Checkers, 1952: a televised address changes a party's decision about a person.
- Cross of Gold, 1896: a convention speech wins a nomination.
- Welch to McCarthy, 1954: one line in a televised hearing becomes the remembered unit.
- Carter, 1979: later events change how a speech is remembered.
- Dean, 2004: infamy through replay.
- A 2004 convention keynote: a speech raises name recognition among people who never met the speaker.

## Build steps (approved as D-3)

1. Add a register to line parts; forms of address resolve from the register, the role and the jurisdiction.
2. Refusals sound real: declining and deferring get a reason and an optional slow opener ("Well," "I mean"); agreeing gets shorter.
3. Compose addresses as move sequences. Devices (contrast, three-part list, disclosure, quotation, callback) appear only in public registers, and disclosure only from the speaker's own history.
4. Record speeches as events with their structure, audience and witnesses.
5. Reception: each listener reacts through the existing decision machinery, summed into a recorded crowd response.
6. Salience and retelling: salient events are retold over time as new "told by" knowledge. Memory fades unless retold, and age at the time sets strength.
7. Crossing generations: family retelling writes knowledge for children.
8. Attribution and commemoration: beliefs crediting the speech, council measures honoring it, later quotation.
