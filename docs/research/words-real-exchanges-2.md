## 1. Reporter vs politician (stance, dodge, contradiction)

### Turn-by-turn shape (observed)

1. Open: a specific, checkable fact (a law he voted for, a book claim, a poll number). Not "what's your position."
2. Subject: claims ownership of the fact, then pivots ("I voted for that law" -> but presidents need flexibility).
3. Asker, second turn: does NOT repeat. Changes the handle: names the contradiction with specifics (the statute, an analyst, a colleague), or asks a one-word probe, or gives a consequence ("so does that mean X?").
4. Subject, second answer: reframes to another bill/opponent/issue; or answers the easy half only. Hedge words appear: "I would assume," "I'm optimistic."
5. Asker, third turn: narrows hard ("Why is he wrong?" / "Are you doing that right now?") or states the unresolved point herself and leaves it hanging.
6. Subject, third answer: either a principle ("we have to be realistic"), or a partial concession on a small piece, or a quip.
7. End: asker thanks and moves on, or closes with a one-line accountability ("we will watch what you get done"). Subject usually gets the last word only when the asker gives up the follow-up. Rarely does anyone say "you've convinced me."

### How a caught contradiction is answered (observed)

- Straight regret, rare, only when the cost is already paid: a senator calling a past confirmation vote his biggest regret and saying he made a mistake (Welker/Tillis). The admission came after several yes/no questions that he answered cleanly. Honest answers make the interview feel unusual.
- Partial concession plus retraction: willing to look at changes but no "blank check"; said a thing then took it back mid-sentence with "strike that" (Kennedy).
- Disputing the premise: "you're mistaken" then restating his own side (Ryan, round 1).
- Attacking the question's frame: "easy to say as a Monday morning quarterback" (Sanders on an endorsement he regrets); not admitting error, tying the outcome to events.
- Escalation (hostile guest): deny, attack the asker for reading the claim, counter-accuse, "let's finish the interview" (Trump/O'Donnell). The asker tried a third follow-up and got talked over.
- Make a joke of it: a quip to close an answer ("but he might").
- Question-for-question: turning it around on the asker's source or knowledge.

### Hallway/gaggle endings (from reporting, not transcripts)

Senators who dislike hallway talk say so up front ("I don't do hallway interviews"; one prefers a proper conversation to a five-second answer). Endings: elevator doors close; "I'll have a statement later"; walking away mid-question; a one-line deflection ("you'll have to ask leadership"). Reporters shout the next one at the back. GENERAL PATTERN.

### Move patterns (6 to 10)

1. Own the fact, then pivot ("I voted for that, but...").
2. Answer the easy half; skip the hard half; asker names the skipped half.
3. Reframe to a different bill, opponent or time period ("that's appropriations, not authorization").
4. Quote-back probe: asker repeats one word of the subject's own phrase as a question.
5. Third-party handle: "your colleague says X" / "analysts say" instead of "I think."
6. Narrowing question: "Why is he wrong?" / "Is that impeachment or investigation?"
7. Partial concession on a detail, none on the point.
8. Retraction mid-sentence ("strike that") that the asker leaves on the record.
9. Principle exit ("their job is not to seek approval"; "we have to be realistic").
10. Closing quip or a deferral to a date ("after the elections").

### Example fragments (rhythm)

- Asker: "Candid?" (one word, from the subject's own phrase)
- Asker: "Why is he wrong?"
- Asker: "I don't mean to cut you off."
- Subject: "Norah, you're mistaken."
- Subject: "I voted for that law."
- Subject: "Strike that."
- Subject: "I'm not saying it's not Iran."
- Subject: "We're going to be back after the elections."
- Asker: "But that's the other side, that's not the press."
- Host to guest: "What's in it for you guys?"

### What AI-sounding gets wrong

Answers the question in sentence one; politely restates it; concedes after one push; asker says "That's a fair point."

## 2. Whip / colleague asks; council recess bargains

### Verified

- LBJ pressing senators (National Archives Prologue excerpts): the ask comes as a question that already contains the complaint ("can't you go with us"), then reciprocity or a rebuke about past votes, then an order; the other side answers in one line, often a jab or a laugh. Ellipses and stress on single words. Dirksen answers a bargain ("one or two Republicans") with a joke about LBJ's own past conduct, not a yes.
- Oral histories (round 1): counters ask the member, not staff; undecided is a real category; a vote can flip overnight without explanation.
- Senate floor deals by "unanimous consent": the leader's aide asks the other side, "do you have a problem?" and says "come to the floor if you'll object."

### General pattern (not verified from a transcript)

Council recess in the hallway: the member holds a folded agenda, speaks low, gives a reason that is about the district ("my side of town can't take it"), asks for a swap on a different item, and sets a time ("before we go back in"). The refusal names someone else ("the chair won't have it"). Whip-to-member: "tell me where you are" before "I need you."

### Turn-by-turn

1. Ask framed as a favor or a reminder of a past favor, not as a speech.
2. Target hedges with a condition or a missing fact ("I haven't seen the final language").
3. Whip asks what would change it (a number, a name, a date), not why.
4. Target names a price (or says "nothing; it's the district"). Whip answers with what they can actually give, or admits they can't.
5. Close: a precise deadline or a "put me down as undecided," not a speech.

### Patterns

1. Favor-for-favor reminder, one line.
2. Ask framed as a complaint ("can't you go with us for once?").
3. Condition hedge ("if the language on X changes").
4. Soft no with a middle ("I won't work against you").
5. Swap offered on an unrelated item with a deadline.
6. Joke-jab at the asker's own past conduct.
7. Warning phrased as bookkeeping ("I'll have to write you down as no").
8. Overnight flip reported flatly, reason withheld.
9. "Who else is in?" used as pressure or as a reason to stay out.

### Fragments

- "Can't you go with us once?"
- "Show me the language."
- "I won't be against you."
- "Put me down undecided."
- "What do you need to get there?"
- "Before we go back in."
- "That's not mine to give."
- Laughs. "You did the same thing."

## 3. Volunteer after a rally

Verified is thin (a candidate greeting volunteers one by one, thanking them, then setting the day's plan and mood; nervous first-timers; cheerful wet returners; volunteer manager phones people one by one to ask what they want to do; a field organizer who treats a new volunteer as a full member). No transcript of the first 3 turns after a speech. GENERAL PATTERN below; check against film captions.

### Turn-by-turn (general)

1. Volunteer opens with something small they noticed (a line that got a laugh, the line at the door, a heckler, that the room ran out of chairs). Not "great speech."
2. Candidate answers with the person: name, where from, one question back ("you came from where?"). Short. Tired voice allowed.
3. Volunteer reveals a stake or an oddity (a sister, a truck, a union card, speaks Vietnamese). Candidate hands off by name to a staffer ("Dana has the clipboard").
4. The ask for the weekend is made by the staffer, in the form of a place and time with an easy out, or by the volunteer volunteering. Candidate adds one line of thanks.

### Patterns

1. Detail-first opener (the crowd, the room, a moment).
2. Candidate asks one factual question about the volunteer.
3. Hand-off by first name.
4. Ask is a slot ("Saturday ten, Maple and Fourth"), plus a fallback ("or phones Monday").
5. Volunteer states a limit ("I can do two hours").
6. First-timer nerves said aloud ("I've never knocked doors").
7. Candidate says what NOT to worry about ("they're mostly nice; dogs are the problem").
8. Shared tiredness joke.

### Fragments

- "Standing room only, huh?"
- "Where'd you drive in from?"
- "Dana's got the sheet."
- "Saturday, ten, Maple and Fourth."
- "I've never done doors before."
- "Two hours, then I've got my kid."
- "Bring water."

## 4. Party chair recruiting; donor leaning

### Verified (round 1 oral histories, restated)

Recruit: soft plant ("maybe you should run"), "think about it," next day follows; the real objection comes at the deadline and is about home; the decision turns on a small insult or an unexpected call; friends promise to "take care of" the rural side. After deciding: phone county chairmen one by one, short answers ("we're with you").

### Donor (no transcript read; GENERAL PATTERN plus facts from the Romney Boca Raton recording coverage)

Verified from coverage: paying guests at a private home, a Q-and-A where they instructed the candidate how to campaign "in a very forthright manner"; the candidate spoke candidly in a way he did not in public; he later said it was "not elegantly stated." Donors ask big-picture "how do you convince people" questions, and the candidate answers in the donor's frame.
General: the lean comes late and sideways, often after family and golf; it names a problem rather than a vote ("the permitting thing is killing us"); the politician answers with a process step ("I'll have staff look at it") rather than a promise; the donor says "I'm just telling you what I'm hearing"; a threat is a silence or a mention of "next cycle."

### Patterns

1. Small talk first: family, boat, a mutual friend.
2. Problem stated as a story about the donor's own business.
3. Politician offers a process, not an outcome ("let me get you with my chief of staff").
4. Donor's "I'm just telling you what I'm hearing."
5. Politician's honest limit ("I can't promise how I'll vote; I can promise a hearing").
6. Pressure via comparison ("the other guy returns my calls").
7. Exit with a plan to meet ("lunch after the session").
8. Recruit: real objection arrives last and is about spouse or job.

### Fragments

- "How's Linda and the kids?"
- "I'm just telling you what I'm hearing."
- "Let me get you with Priya."
- "I can't promise how I'll vote."
- "You should have told me weeks ago."
- "My wife doesn't want me to."
- "I've got a job."

## 5. Family at home

Thin. Verified (round 1): a candidate announces rather than asks; spouse answers briefly or with a question ("What decision?"); a wife's dry complaint about not seeing his name in the paper; absence told as a number of weekends; a NPR panel on spouses: spouses act as surrogates, some choose not to campaign, some stay beside a scandal, some do not appear; the public speech of spouses is careful soundbites. No private-home transcripts.

### Turn-by-turn (general)

1. Announcement at dinner or by phone: one sentence, fact first.
2. Spouse's first reply is a practical question (money, schedule, who drives the kids), not a feeling.
3. Child interrupts with a flat factual question.
4. Win night: noise, someone cries in the kitchen, the spouse worries about tomorrow's schedule. Loss night: logistics first (who calls whom, signs to pick up), then silence, then one oblique line.
5. Long absence: told as a count and a repair ("I'll be home Thursday, I promise the recital").

### Patterns

1. Fact-first announcement.
2. Spouse answers with a question.
3. Dry joke in the shape of a complaint.
4. Absence as a count.
5. Child's blunt question.
6. Loss handled by chores.
7. Spouse says what they will NOT do ("I'm not doing the speech").
8. Late-night quiet line the next morning.

### Fragments

- "I'm filing Monday."
- "Filing for what?"
- "Who's picking up Ben?"
- "That's four weekends."
- "Did you win, Dad?"
- "Eat something."
- "I'll be home Thursday."

## Cross-cutting rules (updated)

1. The first answer should miss; the second narrows; resolution (or a partial one) comes at turn three or never.
2. The asker changes the handle each time: a number, a statute, a colleague's view, a one-word echo. Never repeat the question.
3. Admissions are rare and are paid for; a clean "that was a mistake" should feel like an event, not a default.
4. Caught-contradiction answers come from a menu of distinct moves (own then pivot, dispute premise, attack frame, partial concession, retract, quip, counter-ask). A choice list differs by move, not by tone.
5. Concede wording or a detail, not the point; if conceding the point, name what it cost.
6. Each line has one concrete handle (a bill, street, count, name, date, object). No lines that could be said in any town.
7. Lines vary hard in length: one-word probes next to a long half-answer; fragments; mid-sentence retractions; interruptions.
8. People don't narrate feelings or summarize each other; they ask logistics or state a fact.
9. Hedges are specific ("if the language changes," "I haven't seen the final"), not "I'll think about it."
10. Endings are abrupt and practical (a time, a name, a thank-you, "we'll see," an elevator door). No moral, no recap.
11. Pressure arrives as bookkeeping, a joke, or a silence, not a threat statement.
12. Family and volunteer talk is sideways: errands, schedules, a small detail from the event, not praise or declarations.
13. Never write the other person's agreement into a line unless the world state earned it.
