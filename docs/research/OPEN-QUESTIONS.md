### What should decide which figure paints in front of which

**P1** · asked by hardcoded-content audit (hardcoded-content audit thread) · filed 2026-09-22 · `scene-depth-ordering`

**The question.** In a layered 2D scene where figures stand on a floor drawn in perspective, what rule should decide which figure or object paints in front of which — and what must a scene and a figure each record for that rule to be computable rather than asserted by whoever authored the room?

**Why it matters.** The owner wants a graphics skeleton in this release, and the part he has named as an assumption is depth. The pieces are built — contact lines, perspective scale, occluders, hitboxes — and the ordering on top of them is a number an author writes per anchor. That holds while one person places every figure in a room and stops holding the moment a figure moves within a scene or a room is reused, which is exactly what an interactive environment requires. Getting this wrong is not subtle on screen: a person stands inside the furniture.

**A usable answer.** A rule stated so it could be implemented, plus what it needs recorded. For example, and only as an example of the shape: order by the y of the contact point with the floor, tie-broken by something named, with objects that a figure can stand behind carrying their own contact line so they enter the same order. Where the rule breaks — a figure partly behind and partly in front of a long object, something hung on a wall, a figure lying down — say what the scene has to declare instead. Saying that some cases cannot be ordered by a rule and must stay authored is useful, if it names which.

**Already checked.**

- src/presentation/scene-registry.ts, the runtime scene contract, read on main 273fd2b8 — The depth model exists and is more complete than the question implies, which is why the question is narrow. Each anchor carries a zOrder, described in the file's own comment as paint order, with the note that perspective depth is derived from contacts rather than from it. Each anchor also carries contactFloorYPercent, the floor line it stands or sits on, and that is what perspective scale is interpolated from. Occluders are first-class, with their own zOrder, a region and a plate clip. What it does not establish is where a figure belongs in the order when it is not standing at an authored anchor: zOrder is a number the author of the room wrote down.
- src/presentation/scene-placement.ts, which places a body at an anchor — Computes scale from the contact floor line, checks the sprite against the anchor's footprint and foot spread, and returns the placement carrying the anchor's zOrder through unchanged. So scale is computed from geometry and order is copied from authoring. That asymmetry is the whole of this question. It cannot say what the order should be, because nothing asks it to.
- src/presentation/scene-occlusion.ts and the seat-contact work in the character rendering triage lane — Occluders can be released and plate clips applied, and seat contact is recorded per anchor, so a seated figure meets its chair at a known line. That is the nearest measured ground the project has and it is cited as context, not as an answer: it establishes that the game knows where a figure touches the world, and says nothing about ordering two figures against each other.
- The shipped rooms themselves — They look right, which is evidence that the authored orders are correct for the figures currently placed in them. It is not evidence that the rule generalises, and it is why this reads as an assumption rather than a bug.

> Filed from the owner's own words on 2026-09-22: the game cannot figure out the z space, the depth, and that is an assumption. The second half is the precise part. There is a depth model; what is authored rather than computed is the ordering.

> It is worth saying plainly that the question is narrower than 'how does 2.5D depth work', because the repository already implements the usual answer and an answer that re-derives it would cost a round trip. What is wanted is the rule that turns a figure's own position into its place in the order, and what has to be recorded for that rule to be applicable.

> The honest failure today is not visible breakage in the shipped rooms — one author places each anchor and gets it right. It appears the moment a figure moves within a room, or a room is authored by somebody who did not also place every figure in it.

> An answer that says the authored order is correct and should stay, with the condition under which it stops being correct, is a real answer and would close this.

**The question.** Across the few hundred people a player meets in one life, how common should each family of temperament traits be — which ones should turn up in most people, which in a handful, and which should be rare enough that meeting one is worth noticing?

**Why it matters.** Every generated person currently draws every trait from one seeded curve, so each pole is equally likely on everybody and the population is uniform by construction. ### Whether the national range is the right instrument for Puerto Rico and DC

**P1** · asked by nationwide coverage (research audit thread (technical team lead)) · filed 2026-09-22 · `national-range-instrument-for-non-states`

**The question.** The game generates rules for an unresearched jurisdiction by drawing from the range the read states span, which varies state to state and stays stable per state across saves. That instrument was derived from states. Two of the jurisdictions the game actually seats are not states: the District of Columbia, governed by a Mayor and a Council and sending a Delegate rather than members, and Puerto Rico, whose legislature does not resemble the state legislatures the range was read from and which sends a Resident Commissioner. So: should either of them be governed by the national range at all? If not, what instrument is right for each — a separately read set of real rules, a range drawn from a different comparison group, or an explicit refusal to generate until somebody reads their actual law? And if the range is kept for one of them, what should it be ranged over, given that the thing being interpolated is not the thing the endpoints were measured from?

**Why it matters.** This decides whether two of the fifty-two jurisdictions a player can start in are simulated with rules that were inferred from a comparison group they are not members of. The owner's standing rule is that an unresearched jurisdiction gets a realistic range rather than a refusal or an average, and that real law always overrides — but that rule was framed around states. Applying it unexamined to a territory and a federal district produces confident, stable, wrong rules, which is worse than a declared gap because nothing on the surface says the number was interpolated from the wrong population. It is also the difference between a jurisdiction the game can honestly claim to cover and one it merely does not crash in.

**A usable answer.** A position per jurisdiction, for DC and for Puerto Rico separately. For each: whether the national range applies, and if not, whether the answer is to read its real law, to range it against a different comparison group (naming which), or to decline to generate and let the jurisdiction be explicitly unsupported for now. A sentence on legislature structure for each is enough to act on, since the generator mirrors structure.

**Candidate answers.** None is recommended; the choice is the reader's.

- **Treat both as genuinely unknown rather than generated**. DC and Puerto Rico stay outside the range. Neither gets interpolated rules. Both are declared explicitly unsupported until somebody reads their actual law, and the surface says so in the game's own fail-soft voice rather than refusing silently.
  - For: The owner's three states for a rule are sourced law, generated-and-playable, and genuinely unknown. A territory and a federal district are the clearest case in the whole project of the third state: the endpoints the range would be measured between are state legislatures, and neither of these is one. This keeps the range honest by keeping it to the population it was measured from.
  - Against: It leaves two jurisdictions a player can start in unplayable for the political half of the game, which is close to the refusal the range policy was written to abolish. It is also the status quo by accident rather than by decision, and the status quo currently reports DC as "refused" with no reason a player can read.
- **Each gets its own instrument**. The two are decided separately, not as one class. DC is ranged or read against a comparison group it actually belongs to; Puerto Rico gets its own, drawn from a different group again or read from its real law. Neither inherits the state range.
  - For: They are not alike. DC has a Mayor and a Council and sends a Delegate; Puerto Rico has a bicameral legislature that does not resemble the state ones and sends a Resident Commissioner. Deciding them together is the same error as ranging them against states, one level up. This is also the only option that ends with both playable.
  - Against: It is two instruments to build, maintain and explain, for two jurisdictions, and each needs a named comparison group that somebody has to justify. It risks a bespoke rule path per jurisdiction, which is the thing the range exists to avoid.

### The durations and bands under personality change

**P1** · asked by people and life (people and life thread) · filed 2026-09-22 · `personality-change-bands`

**The question.** The shape of personality change is settled: how hard somebody is to change depends on how strongly the trait is theirs, and what accumulates is separate experiences spread over time rather than repeated attempts. The numbers underneath it are not. For an ordinary-life temperament: how much harder should a defining hold be than a faint one — twice, three times, more? How long should a value have to stand before it counts as settled, and does that differ by trait? How much resistance should a value still carry the week after it changed, as a share of its settled resistance? How far apart do two experiences have to be before the second is genuinely a second one — a month, a season, a year? And how much should accumulated experience ever be able to add, given that a single formative event is worth three times a passing one?

**Why it matters.** These numbers are what a player actually feels, and the shape above cannot save them. Set too loose, characters reinvent themselves every few months and stop being people. Set too tight, nothing anyone does to anyone ever matters and the system is decoration. Both failures look identical in tests, which pass either way, so nothing in this repository will ever catch a wrong answer here.

**A usable answer.** Rough ranges rather than exact numbers, worked against a named example: how many separate experiences, over how long, before somebody who is strongly a certain way plausibly stops being that way; and the same for somebody who barely leans. A position on whether the settling period should differ between a way of thinking and a way of getting on with people would settle five numbers at once. Any answer replaces numbers in a pack file and touches no code.

**Already checked.**

- src/simulation/people-trait-pack.ts and src/simulation/legislature-trait-pack.ts, the movability numbers as shipped — They are the whole of the question. Every one was chosen to be plausible rather than researched, and nothing in the repository checks them against anything.
- The owner's answer of 2026-09-22 on what the pacing depends on — Settles the shape and nothing about scale. It says a strongly held trait is harder than a faint one; it does not say whether a strongly held trait should shift once in a decade or three times in a life.
- src/simulation/people-trait-occasions.test.ts, walked at the new numbers — Establishes that the live route still moves somebody and that it now takes years rather than a fortnight. It proves the mechanism behaves at these numbers; it has nothing to say about whether years is the right answer.

> The current set is authored and labelled as such. Ordinary-life traits declare a settled resistance per strength running roughly 1.5 to 6, settling over eight to fifteen years, carrying half to three fifths of that resistance the week after a change, counting experiences sixty to one hundred and eighty days apart, and capping accumulated experience at three. Bargaining manner is deliberately easier and settles over four years, which is roughly a term.

> One relationship between the numbers is load-bearing rather than taste, and should survive any answer: a value held as defining must resist the weakest force plus the maximum accumulated experience, so persistence alone never moves anybody, and must still be below the strongest force plus that maximum, so every character can change. Both are asserted in trait-resistance.test.ts.

> The live occasion in play argues with the weakest force, so the spacing and the cap together decide whether being turned down repeatedly can ever move somebody at all, and how many years of it that takes.

**Why it matters.** Every seated placement in the project is currently settled by looking at it. We can measure a figure's turn and the point where it meets a seat, and we do, but nothing tells us whether a figure turned one way belongs in a seat drawn another way, so the last step before a person appears in a chair is a judgement nobody can check or repeat. That makes seated coverage un-plannable: we cannot say which seats our existing people can fill, or what to ask for when they cannot.

**A usable answer.** A stated rule with a number in it — for example, that a figure reads as seated when its turn is within some stated angle of the seat's drawn angle, or that turn agreement matters less than a named cue such as where the near knee falls — together with which properties a seat and a figure each have to carry for a room to apply that rule without a person looking.

**Candidate answers.** None is recommended; the choice is the reader's.

- **Angle agreement within a tolerance**. A seat records the angle it is drawn at, a figure records the angle it is drawn at, and a figure reads as seated when the two are within some stated number of degrees.
  - For: It is computable, it extends to seats nobody has drawn yet, and it turns the hand-written permission lists into something derived.
  - Against: It assumes apparent turn is a single angle, which may be false: a figure can read as turned because of its face and shoulders while its body is nearly square.
- **Contact and occlusion carry it, not angle**. What sells a seated figure is that its hips meet the seat surface and its near leg reads in front of the chair's near edge. Turn agreement is secondary and a wide mismatch passes unnoticed.
  - For: It would explain why square figures have looked acceptable in angled chairs so far, and we already measure the contact point.
  - Against: If it is wrong we would be filling angled seats with square figures and only find out when somebody looks at a finished room.

### Which cues make a drawn figure read as turned, when its outline says otherwise

**P1** · asked by character art and scenes (character rendering triage thread) · filed 2026-09-22 · `apparent-turn-versus-silhouette-turn`

**The question.** For a flat drawn human figure, which cues actually tell a viewer which way the body is facing — the asymmetry of the outline, the direction of the face, the foreshortening of the shoulders, the crossing of the limbs — and in what order of strength, such that a figure whose outline is measurably lopsided can still read as facing straight ahead and the reverse?

**Why it matters.** Our admission gate decides which way a seated figure is turned by measuring its silhouette, comparing the horizontal centre of the lower body against the upper. That number is stable and repeatable, and it is the only evidence the pipeline has. But three plates it calls clearly turned read close to straight-on when a person looks at them, and the prose written by whoever first catalogued those same plates disagrees with the measurement on two of four. If the outline is a weak cue, then the gate is confidently filing figures under a facing they do not appear to have, which puts them in the wrong seats and is exactly the kind of wrong-but-plausible number that nothing downstream can catch.

**A usable answer.** A ranking of the cues by how strongly each one determines perceived facing in a flat illustrated figure, with enough detail to tell us whether a silhouette-only measurement is a sound proxy or a weak one — and, if it is weak, which cue we should be measuring instead and whether it can be measured from the pixels we have rather than from a description somebody writes.

**Candidate answers.** None is recommended; the choice is the reader's.

- **The face and shoulders dominate**. Perceived facing is carried mostly by the head's orientation and the apparent width of the shoulders, and outline asymmetry below the waist contributes little.
  - For: It would explain the three plates that measure turned and read straight, since their heads and shoulders are close to square while their legs are angled.
  - Against: It would mean our gate measures the least informative part of the figure, and we would need a way to measure head orientation from pixels, which we currently take from a written label.
- **The outline is a sound proxy**. Outline asymmetry does track perceived facing well enough to gate on, and the three plates in question are genuinely turned in a way a viewer would accept once the figure is in a chair rather than isolated on a sheet.
  - For: It is what we already measure, and an isolated figure on a white field is not the condition under which anyone will see it.
  - Against: It asks us to discount the one direct observation we have, on the grounds that the viewing conditions were wrong, without testing the better conditions.

- art/qa/p71/source_intake_dispositions.json, the written descriptions of the same plates — It disagrees with the measurement on two of its four seated plates, calling one close to square that measures strongly turned and calling another turned the opposite way. Two disagreeing judgements do not establish which is right; they establish that the question is real.
- The contact sheet of all twenty-two candidate bodies, looked at directly — Four plates read clearly turned and three read close to straight-on, while all seven measure turned in the same direction. One person's impression of seven pictures is not evidence about perception, which is why this is a research question rather than a second opinion.

> The direction convention itself is settled and recorded as a decision; this question is about whether the quantity it is built on is the right quantity, not about which sign means which way.

The refusals were right and cost four states their playability until found; the assertion was wrong and quoted a stale date at the player for months of game time; the guess was the worst, because it disabled the guard silently and nothing would have announced it. This is not a question about one rule. Every registry the game is about to fill for fifty-one jurisdictions will meet it, and the answer decides what happens in the far more common case where a row is simply absent.

**A usable answer.** A short policy with a test somebody can apply at a call site without knowing this history. Ideally: the question to ask of a missing fact, the answer that sends it to each of the three outcomes, and what a refusal owes the player — whether naming the gap, offering the action anyway with a marker, or something else. It is the machinery for whatever answer this question produces; it does not choose the policy, and it currently exists in only one corner of the game.

> It is not asking what any particular rule should be. It is asking for the policy that decides, so the next fifty cases are decided once rather than one at a time by whoever meets them.

> An answer that says 'it depends, and here is what it depends on' is the best possible outcome. A single blanket rule is almost certainly wrong, because refusing a legal requirement we cannot source and refusing a modder's content pack are not the same act.

### What a legislator's bargaining manner should actually come from

**P2** · asked by modular legislation (modular legislation thread) · filed 2026-09-22 · `bargaining-manner-source`

**The question.** What should decide how a legislator behaves at a bargaining table? The build derives it from how firmly they have stated past commitments. Is a record of past commitments the right source at all, or should a bargaining manner come from something else — who they owe, what they have survived, how safe their seat is, who is watching — and if past behaviour is right, is the firmness of what they said the most telling part of it?

**Why it matters.** Bargaining is the deepest negotiation surface in the game and it is live in the played client. Read from the wrong source, every member in the chamber acquires a manner that is internally consistent and wrong, and it will not look wrong in any test — the seam works either way. It is also the first legislative trait, so whatever it reads from sets the pattern for every one after it.

**A usable answer.** A position on whether past behaviour is the right source, and if so which behaviour: firmness of stated commitments, whether those commitments were kept, the pattern of refusals and deferrals, or something outside the record entirely such as seat safety or who the member owes. If more than one, a rough sense of which dominates. A worked sketch of two contrasting members and how each should read would be ideal, since the distinction that matters is between members the current reading cannot tell apart.

**Already checked.**

- src/simulation/legislature-manner.ts, the derivation as built — Establishes that a manner can be derived from records alone, explain itself in the player's own terms, and leave an unobserved member genuinely unknown rather than middling. It has nothing to say about whether commitment firmness is the behaviour that most decides how somebody bargains.
- src/simulation/types.ts, LegislativeCommitmentRecord and LegislativeNegotiationRecord — Shows what the world already records about a member's dealings: the firmness and stance of every commitment, and the disposition of every approach including refused, deferred, countered and withdrawn. It establishes that other sources are available; it cannot say which of them a legislator's manner actually rests on.
- The owner's instruction of 2026-09-22: 'i want the trait system itself. not those 5 hardwired. built the connectors for later traits and effects etc. that goes with all systems.' — Settles that a legislative trait belongs in a pack rather than hardwired, which is how it is built. It says nothing about what the trait should be, or what in a member's life should confer it.
- The coordinator's decision of 2026-09-22, taken as technical lead rather than by the owner — Chose the member's own negotiating record as the source, on the same principle the resistance work rests on: the game does not assert something about a person it never observed. That principle rules out inventing a value; it does not choose between the several observed things that could be read instead.

> The mechanism is built and shipped inert-safe, so this question is not blocking anything. A member with fewer than three recorded commitments reads as unknown and contributes nothing, which means an answer that replaces the source changes a derivation rather than unpicking saved data.

> The build currently derives manner from LegislativeCommitmentFirmness alone: explicit counts as showing their hand, noncommittal and provisional as keeping it open, qualified on neither side. That choice is defensible because it is the game's own record of what the member said, but it is one reading of a member's behaviour and not obviously the most telling one.

> Deliberately not used, and worth challenging: whether the member kept the commitments they made. The world records that too, and a member who promises plainly and then does not deliver is a different person from one who promises plainly and delivers, while the current reading cannot tell them apart.

### What an American school in a given state and era is actually called

**P2** · asked by playtest (playtest thread) · filed 2026-09-22 · `school-name-plausibility-by-state-and-era`

**The question.** What is an American public school in a given state and era actually called? Specifically: how the mix of naming shapes (a person, the town, a compass point, a piece of landscape, a county) differs by state, by region and by the decade the school was named; whether the level of the school moves that mix the way the build assumes it does; and which figures are in genuine wide use on school buildings today, including whether leaving out Confederate figures is the right call or an overcorrection.

**Why it matters.** Every character whose earlier life the game summarizes goes to three of these schools, and the player reads them on the Personal screen as their own past. Named against the wrong convention they will all be plausible one at a time and wrong as a set, which is the failure no test in the repository can see: the generator works either way. It is also the first generated institution name, so whatever convention it settles on is the pattern the next one copies.

**A usable answer.** A rough mix — what share of American public schools carry a person's name, the town's, a compass point, a landscape feature or a county — with any variation by region and by the decade the school was named, and whether the level of the school moves it. Plus a short read on which figures are in wide use on school buildings now, and whether the exclusion of Confederate names is correct. Approximate is fine; the build needs weights, not a census.

**Already checked.**

- src/education/catalog.ts and src/education/types.ts, the shipped education directory — Settles that the directory cannot be used for a historical attendance: institutionDateReason refuses any date outside its one academic year, and foundingDate is typed null. That is why the names are generated. It carries real school names for one recent year, so it could in principle answer what schools are called today — but reading a naming convention off it is a research pass over a corpus, not a lookup, and nothing in the tree has done it.
- The owner's approval of 2026-09-22: 'I am completely fine with the generated schools. You can do a county one or you know, some famous person like I'm just making it up like a Andrew Johnson High School or Davy Crockett Middle School, anything like that, or Daniel Boone, or John Brown, anything like that. Also, counties, etc. As long as they're realistic.' — Settles that generated names are wanted and names four shapes he finds realistic. It does not say what proportion of schools carries each shape, and his four examples are all figures with Kentucky and Tennessee ties, which may be a regional pattern he is reading off his own state or may be coincidence.
- docs/playtest/generated-school-names-2026-09-22.md, the walk that produced this — Records what the surface said before and after, across twelve towns. It establishes that the names read as schools rather than placeholders. It cannot establish that the mix matches any real state's.

> The generator is shipped and inert-safe: it replaces three hardcoded placeholders, it draws through the same seeded stream every other generated name uses, and the organizations it names carry generated provenance. An answer changes a corpus and a set of weights, not saved data — a world rebuilt from the same seed would rename its schools, which is the cost of acting on an answer and worth stating.

> Shipped shape: four patterns — a historical figure, a landscape or neighbourhood stem, a compass point on the town, or the town itself — weighted so high schools lean on the town and elementary schools on people and landscape. The weights are a guess dressed as a convention. Nothing was read to set them.

> The figure list is 73 public historical Americans chosen because they are in wide use on American public schools. Confederate figures were deliberately left out, on the reasoning that schools carrying those names are being renamed rather than added. That is a judgement about the present, not a measurement, and it is exactly the kind of thing that should be checked rather than assumed.

> Era is not modelled at all. A character born in 1962 and one born in 2012 draw from the same list, and the naming conventions of American schools plainly moved between those dates. The generator has the birth date available and does nothing with it.

> Region is not modelled either, beyond the town's own name. Whether a school in Montana should draw from a different pool than one in Georgia is unanswered.

### What a player should see in place of a person whose picture cannot be drawn

**P2** · asked by character art and scenes (character rendering triage thread) · filed 2026-09-22 · `person-with-no-artwork-on-screen`

**The question.** When the game knows who somebody is but cannot draw them, because the artwork for their body or face is not present in this build, what should occupy their place on screen — and how, if at all, should the game convey that this is an absence rather than how that character looks?

**Why it matters.** Two standing rules pull in opposite directions here and a portrait is where they meet. Unknown content is supposed to be skipped with a stated reason and never silently, which argues for the player being told something. Player-facing surfaces are supposed to carry no source or provenance references, which forbids the only thing the game actually knows to say, since the reason it has is the name of a missing asset. Today the game draws the person's initials and says nothing, which satisfies the second rule and arguably fails the first. Until this is settled, every surface that composes a person is guessing.

**A usable answer.** A decision about what a player sees and whether they are told anything — for example initials in silence, a plain unmistakably generic figure, a marked placeholder that reads as deliberately empty, or the person being omitted from the scene entirely — with the reasoning that makes it consistent with skipping unknown content out loud rather than in silence.

**Candidate answers.** None is recommended; the choice is the reader's.

- **Initials, in silence**. A monogram in place of the portrait, with nothing said. The person is still present, named and interactable; only their likeness is absent.
  - For: It keeps the player in the fiction, carries no provenance, and reads as a deliberate design rather than a fault.
  - Against: It is indistinguishable from a character the game intends to look like a monogram, so an absence is silently presented as a choice.
- **A generic figure that is plainly a stand-in**. Draw an unmistakably neutral body or face that no real character would have, so the player reads it as a placeholder without being told anything.
  - For: It says 'not drawn yet' without a word of provenance, which satisfies both rules at once.
  - Against: It needs artwork that does not exist, and a stand-in that appears often enough starts reading as a character in its own right.
- **Omit the person from the visual entirely**. If a person cannot be drawn, do not place them in the scene; they remain in the fiction through text.
  - For: Nothing false is shown, and a room with fewer people is honest about what it can render.
  - Against: A room is first person and meant to be populated; removing people to avoid an imperfect likeness empties the very surface this is all for.

**Already checked.**

- The portrait resolution path and the component that renders it, read directly — It establishes what happens today and that the reason is preserved rather than discarded: the refusal names which layers are missing and reaches the rendered markup, where a developer can read it. It does not establish what a player ought to see, which no amount of reading the code can decide.
- The two standing rules themselves, that content is skipped with a stated reason and never silently, and that player-facing surfaces carry no provenance — Each is clear on its own and they were written for different situations. Neither says what to do when the stated reason is itself provenance, which is precisely the portrait case.
- The current behaviour in the game, where a person with no artwork appears as their initials — It is a reasonable default and it is not obviously wrong, but it was never chosen. It is what the component did before anybody asked the question.

> This is a product judgement, so every candidate is filed unrecommended on purpose. The engineering is already able to do any of them.

> The situation is not hypothetical: a set of people whose images were deliberately deleted still have records that several live surfaces are built on, so they render as initials today.
