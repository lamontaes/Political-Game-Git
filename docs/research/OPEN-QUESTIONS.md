### What should decide which figure paints in front of which

**A usable answer.** A position per jurisdiction, for DC and for Puerto Rico separately. - **Treat both as genuinely unknown rather than generated**. DC and Puerto Rico stay outside the range. Neither gets interpolated rules.   - For: The owner's three states for a rule are sourced law, generated-and-playable, and genuinely unknown. - **Each gets its own instrument**. The two are decided separately, not as one class. DC is ranged or read against a comparison group it actually belongs to; Puerto Rico gets its own, drawn from a different group again or read from its real law. Neither inherits the state range.
  - For: They are not alike. DC has a Mayor and a Council and sends a Delegate; Puerto Rico has a bicameral legislature that does not resemble the state ones and sends a Resident Commissioner. Deciding them together is the same error as ranging them against states, one level up. This is also the only option that ends with both playable.
  - Against: It is two instruments to build, maintain and explain, for two jurisdictions, and each needs a named comparison group that somebody has to justify. It risks a bespoke rule path per jurisdiction, which is the thing the range exists to avoid.

How far apart do two experiences have to be before the second is genuinely a second one — a month, a season, a year? And how much should accumulated experience ever be able to add, given that a single formative event is worth three times a passing one?

**Why it matters.** These numbers are what a player actually feels, and the shape above cannot save them. Set too loose, characters reinvent themselves every few months and stop being people. Set too tight, nothing anyone does to anyone ever matters and the system is decoration. Both failures look identical in tests, which pass either way, so nothing in this repository will ever catch a wrong answer here.

**A usable answer.** Rough ranges rather than exact numbers, worked against a named example: how many separate experiences, over how long, before somebody who is strongly a certain way plausibly stops being that way; and the same for somebody who barely leans. A position on whether the settling period should differ between a way of thinking and a way of getting on with people would settle five numbers at once. Any answer replaces numbers in a pack file and touches no code.

**Already checked.**

- src/simulation/people-trait-pack.ts and src/simulation/legislature-trait-pack.ts, the movability numbers as shipped — They are the whole of the question. Every one was chosen to be plausible rather than researched, and nothing in the repository checks them against anything.

**The question.** When a room's seat is drawn at an angle to the camera and a person is composed onto it from a separate flat plate, what has to agree between the two for the result to read as that person sitting in that chair rather than near it — how closely must the figure's own turn match the seat's drawn angle, how much mismatch is tolerable before a viewer notices, and what should a seat and a figure each record so a room can decide this by rule instead of somebody deciding by eye?

**Why it matters.** Every seated placement in the project is currently settled by looking at it. We can measure a figure's turn and the point where it meets a seat, and we do, but nothing tells us whether a figure turned one way belongs in a seat drawn another way, so the last step before a person appears in a chair is a judgement nobody can check or repeat. That makes seated coverage un-plannable: we cannot say which seats our existing people can fill, or what to ask for when they cannot.

**A usable answer.** A stated rule with a number in it — for example, that a figure reads as seated when its turn is within some stated angle of the seat's drawn angle, or that turn agreement matters less than a named cue such as where the near knee falls — together with which properties a seat and a figure each have to carry for a room to apply that rule without a person looking.

**Candidate answers.** None is recommended; the choice is the reader's.

- **Angle agreement within a tolerance**. A seat records the angle it is drawn at, a figure records the angle it is drawn at, and a figure reads as seated when the two are within some stated number of degrees.
  - For: It is computable, it extends to seats nobody has drawn yet, and it turns the hand-written permission lists into something derived.
  - Against: It assumes apparent turn is a single angle, which may be false: a figure can read as turned because of its face and shoulders while its body is nearly square.
- **Contact and occlusion carry it, not angle**. What sells a seated figure is that its hips meet the seat surface and its near leg reads in front of the chair's near edge. Turn agreement is secondary and a wide mismatch passes unnoticed.
  - For: It would explain why square figures have looked acceptable in angled chairs so far, and we already measure the contact point.
  - Against: If it is wrong we would be filling angled seats with square figures and only find out when somebody looks at a finished room.

Nothing says how far apart the two may be, so the list is an assertion rather than a derivation.
- The turn measurement in scripts/art-asset-factory/seated-contact.ts, and the twelve seated plates measured through it — It gives each figure a signed turn offset, so we can compare figures to each other. It says nothing about what a viewer accepts, because there is no measurement of a chair's drawn angle to compare it against and no evidence about tolerance.
- src/environment/scenes/residence-apartment-living-production.ts, the apartment club chair — The chair is drawn turned towards the room and permits one facing. ### What a player should see in place of a person whose picture cannot be drawn

**P2** · asked by character art and scenes (character rendering triage thread) · filed 2026-09-22 · `person-with-no-artwork-on-screen`

**The question.** When the game knows who somebody is but cannot draw them, because the artwork for their body or face is not present in this build, what should occupy their place on screen — and how, if at all, should the game convey that this is an absence rather than how that character looks?

**Why it matters.** Two standing rules pull in opposite directions here and a portrait is where they meet. Unknown content is supposed to be skipped with a stated reason and never silently, which argues for the player being told something. Player-facing surfaces are supposed to carry no source or provenance references, which forbids the only thing the game actually knows to say, since the reason it has is the name of a missing asset. Today the game draws the person's initials and says nothing, which satisfies the second rule and arguably fails the first. Until this is settled, every surface that composes a person is guessing.

**A usable answer.** A decision about what a player sees and whether they are told anything — for example initials in silence, a plain unmistakably generic figure, a marked placeholder that reads as deliberately empty, or the person being omitted from the scene entirely — with the reasoning that makes it consistent with skipping unknown content out loud rather than in silence.

**Candidate answers.** None is recommended; the choice is the reader's.

- **Initials, in silence**. A monogram in place of the portrait, with nothing said. The person is still present, named and interactable; only their likeness is absent.
  - For: It keeps the player in the fiction, carries no provenance, and reads as a deliberate design rather than a fault.
  - Against: It is indistinguishable from a character the game intends to look like a monogram, so an absence is silently presented as a choice.
- **A generic figure that is plainly a stand-in**. Draw an unmistakably neutral body or face that no real character would have, so the player reads it as a placeholder without being told anything.
  - For: It says 'not drawn yet' without a word of provenance, which satisfies both rules at once.
  - Against: It needs artwork that does not exist, and a stand-in that appears often enough starts reading as a character in its own right.
- **Omit the person from the visual entirely**. If a person cannot be drawn, do not place them in the scene; they remain in the fiction through text.
  - For: Nothing false is shown, and a room with fewer people is honest about what it can render.
  - Against: A room is first person and meant to be populated; removing people to avoid an imperfect likeness empties the very surface this is all for.

**Already checked.**

- The portrait resolution path and the component that renders it, read directly — It establishes what happens today and that the reason is preserved rather than discarded: the refusal names which layers are missing and reaches the rendered markup, where a developer can read it. It does not establish what a player ought to see, which no amount of reading the code can decide.
- The two standing rules themselves, that content is skipped with a stated reason and never silently, and that player-facing surfaces carry no provenance — Each is clear on its own and they were written for different situations. Neither says what to do when the stated reason is itself provenance, which is precisely the portrait case.
- The current behaviour in the game, where a person with no artwork appears as their initials — It is a reasonable default and it is not obviously wrong, but it was never chosen. It is what the component did before anybody asked the question.

> This is a product judgement, so every candidate is filed unrecommended on purpose. The engineering is already able to do any of them.

> The situation is not hypothetical: a set of people whose images were deliberately deleted still have records that several live surfaces are built on, so they render as initials today.

## For whoever is answering these

A question belongs here if no explicit research has been done on it. That is
the owner's instruction and it is wider than the test this queue used to
apply, which asked whether a question was bulky enough to be worth handing
over. So expect this document to grow, and expect more of it to be judgement
than citation: design questions belong here too, not only factual surveys.

Two kinds of answer are worth as much as a filled-in table. "It depends, and
here is what it depends on" is a real answer to a design question. So is "I
checked and could not establish this", with what was checked — which beats a
plausible guess, because a guess silently disables the refusal that would
otherwise have told somebody the fact was missing.

Replies go in **CHATGPT REPLIES TO CLAUDE — CURRENT**, or the Art Bench
exchange when the subject is art. A reply left as a comment on this document
never arrives: our Drive connector cannot read comments at all, on any
document, so the channel reads as silent while you are answering.

Superseded renders of this document are renamed and archived rather than
binned, so anything left on an older copy survives.
