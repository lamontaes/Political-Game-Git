### What should decide which figure paints in front of which

**P1** · asked by hardcoded-content audit (hardcoded-content audit thread) · filed 2026-09-22 · `scene-depth-ordering`

**The question.** In a layered 2D scene where figures stand on a floor drawn in perspective, what rule should decide which figure or object paints in front of which — and what must a scene and a figure each record for that rule to be computable rather than asserted by whoever authored the room?

**Why it matters.** The owner wants a graphics skeleton in this release, and the part he has named as an assumption is depth. The pieces are built — contact lines, perspective scale, occluders, hitboxes — and the ordering on top of them is a number an author writes per anchor. That holds while one person places every figure in a room and stops holding the moment a figure moves within a scene or a room is reused, which is exactly what an interactive environment requires. Getting this wrong is not subtle on screen: a person stands inside the furniture.

**A usable answer.** A rule stated so it could be implemented, plus what it needs recorded. For example, and only as an example of the shape: order by the y of the contact point with the floor, tie-broken by something named, with objects that a figure can stand behind carrying their own contact line so they enter the same order. Where the rule breaks — a figure partly behind and partly in front of a long object, something hung on a wall, a figure lying down — say what the scene has to declare instead. Saying that some cases cannot be ordered by a rule and must stay authored is useful, if it names which.

**Already checked.**

- src/presentation/scene-registry.ts, the runtime scene contract, read on main 273fd2b8 — The depth model exists and is more complete than the question implies, which is why the question is narrow. Each anchor carries a zOrder, described in the file's own comment as paint order, with the note that perspective depth is derived from contacts rather than from it. Each anchor also carries contactFloorYPercent, the floor line it stands or sits on, and that is what perspective scale is interpolated from. Occluders are first-class, with their own zOrder, a region and a plate clip. What it does not establish is where a figure belongs in the order when it is not standing at an authored anchor: zOrder is a number the author of the room wrote down.
- src/presentation/scene-placement.ts, which places a body at an anchor — Computes scale from the contact floor line, checks the sprite against the anchor's footprint and foot spread, and returns the placement carrying the anchor's zOrder through unchanged. So scale is computed from geometry and order is copied from authoring. That asymmetry is the whole of this question. It cannot say what the order should be, because nothing asks it to.
- src/presentation/scene-occlusion.ts and the seat-contact work in the character rendering triage lane — Occluders can be released and plate clips applied, and seat contact is recorded per anchor, so a seated figure meets its chair at a known line. That is the nearest measured ground the project has and it is cited as context, not as an answer: it establishes that the game knows where a figure touches the world, and says nothing about ordering two figures against each other.
- The shipped rooms themselves — They look right, which is evidence that the authored orders are correct for the figures currently placed in them. It is not evidence that the rule generalises, and it is why this reads as an assumption rather than a bug.

> Filed from the owner's own words on 2026-09-22: the game cannot figure out the z space, the depth, and that is an assumption. The second half is the precise part. There is a depth model; what is authored rather than computed is the ordering.

> It is worth saying plainly that the question is narrower than 'how does 2.5D depth work', because the repository already implements the usual answer and an answer that re-derives it would cost a round trip. What is wanted is the rule that turns a figure's own position into its place in the order, and what has to be recorded for that rule to be applicable.

> The honest failure today is not visible breakage in the shipped rooms — one author places each anchor and gets it right. It appears the moment a figure moves within a room, or a room is authored by somebody who did not also place every figure in it.

> An answer that says the authored order is correct and should stay, with the condition under which it stops being correct, is a real answer and would close this.

### What a believable pace of personality change looks like

**P1** · asked by people and life (people and life thread) · filed 2026-09-22 · `personality-change-pace`

**The question.** How much has to happen to somebody before a disposition actually shifts? Specifically: how much should a single formative event be able to do on its own against how much accumulated repetition should be needed, roughly how long a value has to have stood before it counts as settled, and whether a person who has already changed once becomes easier or harder to change again.

**Why it matters.** These numbers are what a player actually feels. Set too loose, characters reinvent themselves every few months and stop being people. Set too tight, nothing anyone does to anyone ever matters and the system is decoration. Both failures look identical in tests, which all pass either way, so nothing in the repository will ever catch a wrong answer here.

**A usable answer.** Rough scales rather than exact numbers: how many significant events, over how long, before a disposition plausibly moves; whether one severe event should ever be enough on its own; and a position on whether prior change makes the next change easier, harder, or both depending on how recent it was. Worked against any of the five current traits would be ideal, since they differ and should.

**Already checked.**

- src/simulation/people-trait-pack.ts, the movability numbers currently shipped — Each trait declares what a settled value resists, how long it takes to settle, and what each prior change adds. Deliberation settles hardest over fifteen years, reliability soonest over eight, the rest between. Every one of those numbers was chosen to be plausible rather than researched, and nothing checks them against anything.
- src/simulation/trait-resistance.ts and its tests — Establishes that resistance can be read from records alone, that an unobserved person is not movable at all, and that repeated pressure eventually carries where one attempt does not. It proves the mechanism behaves; it has nothing to say about whether the pace it produces resembles a life.
- The owner's instruction of 2026-09-22: 'every character should be able to change with varying levels of resistance.' — Settles that resistance must vary between people, which the record-chain reading delivers. It gives no sense of scale — whether a disposition should shift once in a decade or several times in a year.

> The mechanism is built and is not in question: resistance is read from the person's own chain of records, and failed attempts are recorded so pressure accumulates rather than each attempt being an independent roll. How it is computed is engineering and stays in the code.

> What is deferred is only the numbers, which decide how a life feels. They are authored placeholders and are labelled as such in the file, so an answer replaces three numbers per trait and touches no code.

> The build currently asserts both directions at once and that is the part most worth challenging: somebody recently shaken is easier to shift again, while every change already made adds a permanent cost, so each shift leaves them harder to move than the last time they settled.

### When a missing fact should refuse, assert or be re-derived

**P2** · asked by hardcoded-content audit (hardcoded-content audit thread) · filed 2026-09-22 · `missing-fact-refuse-assert-rederive`

**The question.** When the game needs a fact it does not have, when should it refuse to act, when should it assert a default and carry on, and when should it re-derive the fact from material it already holds? What distinguishes the three cases, and what must a refusal tell the player?

**Why it matters.** Four cases in one evening across four independent lanes decided this differently, and none of them decided it on purpose. The refusals were right and cost four states their playability until found; the assertion was wrong and quoted a stale date at the player for months of game time; the guess was the worst, because it disabled the guard silently and nothing would have announced it. This is not a question about one rule. Every registry the game is about to fill for fifty-one jurisdictions will meet it, and the answer decides what happens in the far more common case where a row is simply absent.

**A usable answer.** A short policy with a test somebody can apply at a call site without knowing this history. Ideally: the question to ask of a missing fact, the answer that sends it to each of the three outcomes, and what a refusal owes the player — whether naming the gap, offering the action anyway with a marker, or something else. Worked against the cases in the checked list would be ideal, and disagreeing with how any of them was decided is a useful answer, not an awkward one.

**Already checked.**

- The four states unblocked on 2026-09-22 — Alaska, Nebraska, Minnesota and Ohio — and the candidate-qualification refusal path they all ran through — All four refused, and all four refused for a reason about the repository rather than about the candidate: a missing commencement date, a requirement stated in months the code read in years, a compiler token the parser did not know. That establishes the refusal discipline is worth its cost for legal requirements. It says nothing about where else the same discipline applies, and three of the four looked like ordinary bugs from the screen.
- The same evening's counter-examples, in the same repository — An unanswered job offer asserted a start date that had gone stale and quoted it months later. An education release label was guessed, which disabled the refusal that would otherwise have fired. Nebraska and Minnesota were settled by re-deriving from pages already held, in under an hour. Three different answers to the same question, none of them chosen deliberately, and each defensible in isolation.
- The owner's own ruling on content packs, 2026-09-22: 'ignore it and say so. that shouldnt hold the game back.' — Settles the case where the missing thing is a modder's work: load what is understood, skip the rest, and state what was skipped. It cannot be generalised as it stands, because the cost of being wrong about a modder's field is an afternoon, while the cost of being wrong about a state's eligibility rule is the game asserting something false about the real world.
- src/simulation/candidate-qualification.ts and the four-state research typing it implements — Implements KNOWN, UNKNOWN, NO_REQUIREMENT_FOUND and NOT_APPLICABLE, and holds the line that unknown is not permission. It is the machinery for whatever answer this question produces; it does not choose the policy, and it currently exists in only one corner of the game.

> This is a design question rather than a factual survey, filed here because no explicit research has been done on it and the owner's instruction is to defer such things rather than hold them.

> It is not asking what any particular rule should be. It is asking for the policy that decides, so the next fifty cases are decided once rather than one at a time by whoever meets them.

> An answer that says 'it depends, and here is what it depends on' is the best possible outcome. A single blanket rule is almost certainly wrong, because refusing a legal requirement we cannot source and refusing a modder's content pack are not the same act.
