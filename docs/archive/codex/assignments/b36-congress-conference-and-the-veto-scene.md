## What the player experiences

You are a senator and your bill passed the Senate, but the House changed it. Nothing happens by magic: the House sponsor's staff calls your office, and the two chambers name members to sit down together. You are one of them, or you lobby the ones who are. In a committee room off the Capitol rotunda you and three others trade away parts of the bill; each of you knows what your side will accept because you have counted. A report comes out, both chambers vote on it, and the clerk enrolls the one agreed text and carries it across the street. If the President is not you, her chief of staff phones the sponsor the day before to say what the President will do and why, and the people the bill helps or hurts write, call or show up. Then the signing happens in a room with the sponsor, the affected people and cameras, or the veto message goes up the Hill. A veto comes back to the chamber where the bill began. Leaders count; some members who voted yes now say they were only voting for the bill, not against the President; some switch because of what the President or their district told them. The override roll call is a played moment with the same members who voted before, each for their own reasons, and the outcome (law, or sustained) is on the record. If you are the President, the same scene runs from the other chair.

Where a body has no conference or no veto, the scene is simply absent; where it has them, the same scene plays in a statehouse (governor, joint conference) and a council (mayor, no conference). Computer-run chambers do all of it in the background and the news reports it.
