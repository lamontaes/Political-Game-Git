## What the player experiences

You walk into the county clerk's office (or the town clerk's, or the secretary of state's page if that is where the place files). A clerk behind the counter tells you what this seat needs: your age against the office's age, how long you must have lived there, the fee or signatures, the deadline, and which party label you may file under. If you are too young for the Senate in Kentucky (30), she says so and names the offices you can file for now. Ask "who has filed so far?" and she reads the list from the file: names, dates filed, party label, and independents or no-party candidates on a separate line, because they file differently and skip the primary.

If your state holds partisan primaries, filing puts you in a party's field. You meet the precinct committeeman and the county party chair, who decide whether to back you. Primary night is a scene like election night: precincts report, you see your share against named opponents, and where the state has a runoff you see the second date and decide whether to ask for it (North Carolina) or hear that you must. For President the same ladder runs bigger: state primaries and caucuses feed delegates, who are people with their own views, and at the national convention the delegates choose the nominee and the nominee chooses a running mate. Nominees are no longer invented for the cycle; they are people who climbed.
