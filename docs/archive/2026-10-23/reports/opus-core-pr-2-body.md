## What changed for the player

- Games open on January 20, 2025 (your October 14 ruling), and the disasters FEMA declared for the town's county or state happen on their real dates until FEMA's records end on October 9, 2026. Measured from FEMA's records: 1,610 of 3,282 counties had one begin in that span, including every parish in Louisiana and every county in Kentucky. Each one is news, with how many homes FEMA found damaged. When it's declared, federal aid reaches the town's government and its households, and households pay the town's builders to repair their homes.
- Social Security claims during play follow the law in force, so if Congress raises the full retirement age, people claim later. The evening build opens the laws engine (`src/bench/world.ts:300`), so this applies there.
- Residents' relatives who live in other towns now spend most of their income instead of keeping all of it, so far less money piles up in the background population.
- Families with children in private school split their tuition among the town's private schools by size, instead of each family being assigned to one.
- Town prices no longer run away. Over a year in Woodmere, New York, the average business's price rose 3.1%, where it had risen 21%; the price index the game reads also rose 3.1%. A business also raises its prices when its own workers get a raise, such as a new minimum wage.
- Families who move into town get family names from the generator that names invented officials. It draws from the state's name records and refuses a real officeholder's name (`src/core2/officials-identity.ts:72`).

## Why each change works

**Disasters come from FEMA's records.** What the code does now (inferred from the code): a disaster happens only where and when FEMA declared one for the town's county or its whole state, from 50,883 declarations since 2000 (`src/core2/modules/disasters.ts:343`). On the day it begins, it's a public news event. On the day it's declared, the U.S. Treasury pays the town's government the money FEMA committed to rebuilding its public property, and the town's households the housing aid FEMA approved for them. FEMA's award to the county's government is also paid when the county government is part of the game. In most game towns, including Gonzales below, the county government is kept outside the game, like the Treasury, so that award moves no money in play. The households then pay for repairing the damage FEMA inspected in owners' homes, split among the town's builders by how many people each employs (`src/core2/modules/disasters.ts:268`).

A game town can hold fewer people than the real one. So household amounts are cut to the game town's population divided by the Census Bureau's estimate for the real town. The town's government is the real one, with its real budget, so it gets FEMA's full award, unscaled.

Measured over 2021 in Gonzales, Louisiana, where the game town has 1,500 people and the real town 13,930 (a share of 0.108). FEMA found 1,297 homes damaged by Hurricane Ida, which becomes 139 in the game town (rounded down). Its households received $1.02 million of FEMA's housing aid. Of that, they paid $495,626 to the town's three builders for repairs; the rest is aid for rent and other needs, which they keep. The town's government received $2.23 million for public property. Money balanced.

**Retirement follows the law in force.** What the code does now (inferred from the code): where the laws engine runs, a claim during play is dated from the government team's Social Security ages, not the core's own table (`src/core2/modules/retirement-claims.ts:88`). Each person's claim age is set from their full retirement age, earlier with less saved or less patience, so it moves with the full age. Measured in the test in Teaticket, Massachusetts, on October 13, when it passed; it now fails on main's date change (see "Checks"). The game opens on January 1, 2021, and one adult is set up so that their award would start two months later. With the full retirement age raised to 68 on the opening day, their full age moves from 66 years 10 months to 68, so their claim moves about 14 months later. They had no award by June 2021 and had claimed by the end of 2022. The same rule applies to every claimant; the test follows this one.

**A faster year.** Three fixes. Measured back to back on the evening of October 13, before the October 14 main merge, they take a Woodmere year from 112.2 seconds (the commit before them) to 96.6 (the commit with all three). Single runs vary too much to size each fix on its own:

- **Filling a household in** (inferred from the code): each seller's sales to the counted population were kept as one list, with a part for each of about 24,500 counted households. That count includes the town's households and the households of residents' relatives who live elsewhere. Each time a household was filled in as full people, about 200 of those lists were copied whole, and that happened 33 times in the first 60 days. Now each list is copied once and then changed in place (`src/core2/modules/residents.ts:1193`).
- **Dating Social Security claims** (inferred from the code): each new year, every counted person 60 or older had a claim dated through the law engine, including people long past 70. Now people past the last claim age are skipped, and the law's ages are reused for each birth year within a day (`src/core2/modules/retirement-claims.ts:145`). Measured: in a 60-day profile, claim dating fell from about 1 second to too little to list.
- **Storing module records** (inferred from the code): with bookkeeping off, each record an engine stores is now a plain copy, without the checks and freezing that tests keep (`src/core2/module-data.ts:24`).

**Congress's vote day.** What the code did (inferred from the code): when an event names many people, such as all the members voting on a bill, everyone who hears of it checked their tie to each named member one by one. Now each person checks whichever list is shorter, their own ties or the named members, with the same result (`src/core2/modules/life.ts:345`). Measured in Woodmere (seed m701-a), one run each: timing that one day before and after the fix, December 15, 2026, went from 4.71 to 3.02 seconds (the 4.96 under "Known problems" is the same day inside a whole-year run). The whole year went from 97.6 to 90.8 seconds.

**Choice tests after the personality weight change (resolved).** The owner's October 14 ruling raised the personality weight from 0.2 to 1 (#4152), and three core choice tests in `src/core2/life.test.ts` failed on main from that merge. Each gives a worker a single trait, the strongest effort, and checks that stress, wages or that trait's direction turns the worker's choice. Main has since fixed them at the played weight with a milder effort trait, and this branch now carries that fix from the October 14 merge. I first reported that at weight 1 stress no longer turned that worker's choice. That came from the one-trait worker (inferred from the code): a person's traits share the push on each choice, so one trait alone pushes as hard as the game allows, and real people carry many traits (`src/core2/choice.ts:157`). Measured in played towns two days in, for every full person with a job, one run each: in Woodmere, New York (seed m701-a), high stress changed 2 of 19 choices at weight 1 and none at 0.2. In Cross Plains, Tennessee (seed 30e3de23771e), it changed 1 of 23 at weight 1 and 3 of 23 at 0.2. So stress turns a few choices at either weight, and there is no call for you here. A new test checks stress at the weight the game plays at: forty people with personalities from the people team's generator each play the test worker. Measured in the test, stress turned 3 of 40 from work to rest and none the other way (`src/core2/life.test.ts:389`, passing).

**Relatives elsewhere spend.** What the code did (inferred from the code): the counted population keeps one account for the town and one for each place where residents' relatives live. Only accounts with purchase flows ever spent, and the relatives' accounts have none, since they have no local stores. Now an account with income and nothing to buy locally spends 84% of its income, the share of personal income Americans spend (BEA), with sellers outside the town at each month's close (`src/core2/modules/residents.ts:702`). Measured over 2021 in Duncan Ranch Colony, Montana (seed 58b6bb74f3a80de69433f1d29cdc1851): the town's and the relatives' accounts together ended with $36.3 million instead of $56.9 million, against $6.9 million at the opening, and money balanced. The rest of that growth is the 16% of income that stands for taxes and saving. These accounts take in about $190 million a year in wages and Social Security, and 16% of that is about $30 million, close to the $29 million rise. Counted residents pay no taxes yet, and I've asked the government team about it.

**Prices follow the town's real income.** What the code did (inferred from the code): each business judges demand by comparing the town's income each month with its income at the opening. The opening figure counted only residents' pay from the town's own businesses; the monthly figure also counts pay from jobs elsewhere and retirement income (`src/core2/finance-state.ts:1047`). Measured in Woodmere (seed m701-a, one run): the monthly figure read 5.6 times the opening's from the first month. By year end the income businesses read was 2.33 times the opening's, demand was 1.41 times what they could serve, and prices had risen about 17% beyond the price index. Why those three differ (inferred from the code): the income a business reads moves only partway toward each month's figure, and demand grows more slowly than income (`src/core2/business-books.ts:258`). Now each business takes the first full month it reads as its starting point and reacts to changes from there (inferred from the code, `src/core2/finance-state.ts:1053`). Same town, seed and year, one run, with this fix and the two in the next paragraph: the income businesses read rose 2.4%, and the average business's price rose 3.1%, the same as the price index.

**Two more fixes kept prices from trailing inflation.** Measured in the same Woodmere run setup (seed m701-a, one run) with the first fix alone: prices rose 1.9% against the 3.1% index. What the code did (inferred from the code): spending rose only about a third as fast as the town's income in dollars. So when prices and incomes both rose 3%, spending rose about 1% and households bought about 2% less. Now spending in dollars rises with prices, and only income growth beyond inflation changes how much households buy (`src/core2/business-books.ts:268`). A test (`src/core2/business-books.test.ts:175`, passing) doubles prices and incomes together and checks that what households buy is unchanged. Second (inferred from the code): the stand-in pay of residents who work at jobs the game doesn't model, 77% of Woodmere's resident income, stayed the same amount forever. It now rises with the place's pay level, as modeled jobs' pay does (`src/core2/modules/residents.ts:440`); in the year above, that level rose 3.1%.

**A raise reaches prices** (the economy team asked for this). What the code does now (inferred from the code): at each monthly review a business records each worker's hourly pay. At the next review it compares those same workers' pay now with then, weighted by their hours, and raises its price by that change times pay's share of its costs. A worker hired since then isn't in the comparison, so hiring someone at higher pay doesn't raise prices (`src/core2/finance-state.ts:1176`). Measured in the test (`src/core2/finance-writer-contract.test.ts`, passing): doubling the pay of a firm whose costs are 83% pay raised its price to 1.83 times an identical firm's.

**No dice in private schools or newcomers' names.** What the code did (inferred from the code): a hash of a family's id picked which private school it paid, and a hash of a household's id picked a family name from the town's names. Now tuition is split by each school's staff (`src/core2/opening-private-tuition.ts:71`), and family names come from the officials' name generator (`src/core2/modules/counted-drift.ts:436`). The no-dice check flags no new seeded pick on this branch.
