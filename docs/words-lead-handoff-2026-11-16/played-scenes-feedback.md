### Rule 110. A scene opens on what just happened, in words a person would use

His words: "I'm not sure why it's saying the roll... is that finishing an event or something? I need more context, at least what it thinks the context will be. That will also help scene generation."

- The opening says the event that caused the scene, in plain words, from the cause record: "The council just voted down the free fares ordinance. You voted no." Not "The roll on {bill} is recorded."
- It says why this person is here only when that is not plain from who they are (a reporter after a vote needs none; a stranger at your office does).
- It still never says where you are (owner, Oct 27) and never restates the screen.
- The same facts are the scene's context for generation: every scene type names its cause record, and a scene whose cause cannot be said in one plain sentence is not ready.
- Covered before: rule 107 (no office jargon) in part. New: the opening must state the cause. "The roll" joins the jargon list: say "the vote".

### Rule 111. A "why" is answered with a reason

His words: "Put this down. I'm against the ordinance... She knows that. She asked why."

- When the other person asks why, how or what for, every reply offered either gives a reason or plainly declines. A reply that repeats what the asker just said is not offered.
- The reasons offered are ones the record can back for this player and this bill: who it helps or costs, what it costs the town, a promise made, how the player's voters lean, the player's own stance on the question. The player picks among true reasons; that is the choice.
- "Put this down" and its kind (telling a reporter how to write) are not answers and come out as answers.
- Covered before: rule 108 (a question is answered from the record) covered what other people say. New: it now binds the player's own replies.

### Rule 112. A sharp reply has to be earned

His words: "Read the ordinance before you write about it. That doesn't make any sense. Why is she so combative already?"

- A reply that attacks, scolds or brushes off is offered only when something on the record or in this talk earned it: this person wrote or said something against the player, the tie is bad, or they have pressed a second time. A first plain question never earns it.
- A refusal to talk ("I'm not doing this in a hallway.") is the same: offered only with a reason the record holds (bad tie with this outlet, a story running against the player), and it says the reason or is the plain "Not now."
- The player's own traits may make the sharp reply available sooner, never at the first plain question.
- Covered before: bribes and hard news slide with the tie. New for replies in general.

### Rule 113. No reply says what the record contradicts, unless it is a marked lie

His words: "I never had a problem with the ordinance." (offered after "You voted against it.")

- A reply that states the player's position, vote or past act is offered only when the record agrees.
- A false statement may be offered only as a lie: marked as one to the player, and only where the game reads lies (someone can have heard otherwise and it costs something when caught). Never as an ordinary answer, and never one that contradicts what was said one line earlier in the same talk.
- Covered before: "a line may not state a fact no record holds". New: applied to the player's replies and to the last thing said.

### Rule 114. The player always knows which lines are theirs to choose

His words: "Oh, are these my choices?... I don't understand how this is working." and "let me, quote unquote, play the game by choosing the dialogue."

- In the game, in the sampler and in every played file, the player's choices are set apart, labeled as the player's ("You say:"), and are pressed one at a time; what the other person says next follows the press.
- The sampler shows no scene in the old read-only form. A scene not yet playable is left out, not shown with a label like "old".
- A played file for a stamp lists the replies offered at each turn under "You say:" and marks the one pressed.

### Rules, for every scene

Rule 115. No word points at nothing. "It", "this", "that", "of it" must have a named thing said in this talk before it. "I'll keep every word of it." with no oath spoken, "If this doesn't go my way" with no "this", "a story about the water rates" with no story told: each fails. (Sharpens rule 110: the cause is said in the scene, and so is the thing every later line leans on.)

Rule 116. A scene says the specific thing at stake, from the record. A bribe names what the person wants done (the vote, the permit, the contract) before the envelope means anything. A reporter says what the story says the player did, not only its subject. A scene whose record cannot supply the specific thing does not play. (My bribe ruling of Nov 14, "what is wanted first, the money second", already says this for bribes; it now binds every ask, offer and accusation.)

Rule 117. A ceremony or form follows its real form. An oath is words said after the person giving it, then "I do"; nobody thanks anyone for trust in the middle of it, and the player's replies are the oath's own steps. The same for a roll call, a verdict, a vow, a concession call. Use the real form's order and plain words.

Rule 118. A reply does one thing and anyone can tell which. "I shouldn't. But fine. Put it down." refuses, gives in and accepts in three breaths; the player cannot tell what pressing it does. Each reply is a clear take, refuse, report, ask or leave, in words that say so. (Extends rule 113 and my desk ruling that a reply that names the act decides.)

Rule 119. Replies belong to the person and the moment. Two different people in two different scenes are not offered the same menu, and a scene has many different responses ("Everything needs to have a bunch of different responses and stuff. So that way I'm able to grade a lot."). Different means a different thing to do or a different reason, placed by the record and the tie, not a second wording of the same move. This is rule 111 to 113's placing plus the "many" floors, and it sets the order of the story queue.

Rule 120. Scenes do not all have one shape. "It's always narrator, person says one thing, person says another." A scene's shape comes from what is happening: some open on the person speaking, some on the player's own choice, some have the other person ask back, some run several turns before a choice. Narration is used when there is something to see that the lines do not say.

Wording notes he gave, to apply wherever the pattern appears: lines that pile clauses are clunky ("Whatever has gone wrong, this isn't the way out of it." Keep "Sit down." and say one plain thing after it); "I can see what this is costing you." is awkward; say the short form ("one that doesn't need an envelope" is fine but wordy).

- Rule 121. A line a person says to or about themselves is something people say ("I need to gather myself.", "I need a minute to take this in."). "I need to say it to myself first." is the class of stiff self-talk; it is reworded wherever it occurs, not only there.
- Rule 122. Someone speaking for their own bill says what the bill does, in a few sentences, without being asked. The player never has to ask "What does it do, exactly?" of its sponsor. This is a longer turn, and scenes may have them.
- Rule 123. The narration makes plain where the scene is and what kind of talk it is, and the lines fit it. A floor debate is on the floor, in the register of the floor; "I'm with you on this one." is for two people talking alone. One scene has one setting.
- Rule 124. An opener fits how the two came to be talking (caught on the stairs, arrived for an interview) and whether the player knows the person. "James recognizes you" is right when the player does not know James; "You're Angela, aren't you?" only from a stranger.
- Rule 125. A reply never needs context the player does not have. "Let's see where the amendments land." plays only when amendments are on the record and the player took part.
- Rule 126. What happens around the talk follows what was just said. After the player refuses a quote, no "Two more cameras swing toward you."
- A scene about another state's law said by someone who then says it does not apply: he does not understand it at all. It is not reworded; it is not staged (director class I).

## Words lead: what these rule (Nov 15 20:50Z), for every scene

- Rule 136. The narrator says who a person is the first time the player meets them at a story point: full name and what they are to the player or the place, from the record ("Jose Salgado, the council's clerk", "Erin Sinclair, who lives in your district"). A first name alone is used only where the record says the player knows the person. This holds at every level and in every scene; it is one rule in the director (the same read the desk thread's met record gives), never one per scene. A narration line that names a stranger and does not say what they are is a fault.
- Rule 137. A line never presumes something the record does not hold. "They have your case already. I sent it." and "I already called them about your case." say the player acted before this person ever spoke. A lie is a reply the player picks, the page and the game mark it as a lie, and its content must be possible at that moment; a line about an earlier act plays only when the record shows the earlier meeting or act.
- Rule 138. Every office, department or body a line names is one the place's records hold, said by its own name there. A made-up name ("the workforce office") is a stand-in. Where the place has no such body, the line says the act (what the official does and who reads it), not an office.
- Rule 139. Replies under one choice are different things to say, not the same refusal twice, and each stands on its own: a no says what is being refused and why ("Not this one. My hands are full." fails both).
- Rule 140. Plain phrasing people use. "What I saved is almost gone" is deleted; his picks stand ("I've been out of work a while, and my savings are almost gone.").
- Rule 141. The time words at the top of a scene come from the hour the event happens on the calendar. If every scene of a kind says "First thing on", the event has no hour of its own and is given one from the record (a ceremony's hour, office hours' hours).
- Rule 142. The Scenes page shows what a player would read: the office title of the seat the sample is for (never "Congress member" for a council seat), sample names that are one person, and conditions in plain English ("you have not met this person", never "name first, stranger").
- Rule 143. People already in the player's life carry context and news: a friend calls after a big event (his example: a college friend, coffee a few days after the president's speech) and the talk is how the player learns about the world. The example is a generality: any major public event can be the cause of such a talk with someone the player's record ties them to.
- Rule 144. Laws and ordinances that can pass are material for scenes at every level.
- Rule 145. Scenes are conditional by who the player is: sets that play only for a state legislator, sets for a legislator or an executive at any level, sets for a person not in office. The scene record says who it can play for.
- Rule 146. Sessions may ask him questions. The repo and his rulings are searched first (rule 132); a real design question left after that goes to the Account lead in one line with what was found.
