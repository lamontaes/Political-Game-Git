# Episode: the vote that switched (a close vote, a colleague or leader asks for one more member)

Slug `the-vote-that-switched`. Types: `floor-vote`, `vote-asked`. Season: holding office. Calibration only: nothing here ships as player text; no real name or quoted line is in any bank. Owner model (Oct 14, `docs/design/scenes/owner-scene-examples.md`): a tense floor vote where the goal is to convince one member to vote no on the amendment (the owner's example: McCain and the Obamacare repeal). That is federal context only; the cases below are local and state.

## Sources read (for calibration)

1. **Opened in full.** Laramie, Wyoming city council, March 23, 2022 (Laramie Reporter, "Councilors nixed civilian oversight board for fear of police resignations", https://laramiereporter.substack.com/p/councilors-nixed-civilian-oversight). During a seven-minute recess of a public meeting a councilor phoned a colleague and asked what it would take to change his vote; he added a point he had not made in public (police resignations, "upheaval"). The colleague had pushed back, then said he was already on the fence, and asked for reconsideration. The 5-4 passage became a failure. He did not tell the room about the calls. The mayor called lobbying during meetings "problematic." Cost: an almost two-year effort ended; advocates were disappointed.
2. **Opened in full.** New Jersey Senate, Sept. 27, 2018 (New Jersey Globe, https://newjerseyglobe.com/?p=12249). A corporate tax cleanup bill failed 17-16 (21 needed). The Senate president said the governor lobbied members in the roughly 20 minutes before a second vote; one senator then changed a yes to an abstention (reported source: a senator the president had removed as a committee chair that year; he would not name her). A recording error was fixed and the bill passed 21-16.
3. **Search summary only.** Youngstown, Ohio council, Oct. 1, 2014 (Vindicator, https://vindyarchives.com/news/2014/oct/01/youngstown-council-rejects-measure-remove-mayors-a): on a 4-3 vote a councilor who had voted with the majority twice switched and defeated the ordinance, saying only that he changed his mind.
4. **Search summary only.** Seattle city council, April 2010 (Seattle Met, https://www.seattlemet.com/news-and-city-life/2010/04/obrien-rumored-to-be-considering-switching-vote-on-panhandling; page returned 403): the mayor was reported pressing a councilor to switch on a panhandling ordinance, with a veto threat behind it. Rumor of a switch, not a confirmed one.
5. **Search summary only.** Louisiana House (710keel.com, "LA Legislator Changes His Votes"): members may change a vote after it is cast so long as the result does not flip. A rule that shapes when a switch is allowed.

## Fact packet (what happens, in substance)

- **Who asks:** a fellow member on the body (Laramie), the governor's office through a chamber leader (New Jersey), or the mayor with a veto behind it (Seattle).
- **Trigger:** a close vote is held or about to be: 5-4, 17-16, 4-3. One member decides the result.
- **When:** in a recess, in the minutes between two roll calls, or at the member's desk before the call.
- **What the asker offers or argues:** a cost the member had not weighed (a department in upheaval, a veto), or simply "what would it take." Not a speech to the room.
- **What the member weighs:** the case already made to them in public, the people they answer to, what the asker can do to them next (a committee seat, a veto, the next vote on their own item).
- **What the member answers:** four stances: switch (Laramie), hold, bargain (name a price), or ask for a few minutes (the member "on the fence" who wants the recess).
- **The vote:** the roll is called again or a motion to reconsider is made; the result flips, or an abstention drops the total under the line (New Jersey).
- **What it costs or earns:** the other side's trust is spent (advocates in Laramie); a leader remembers who held and who moved (the New Jersey committee chair story); the press learns it from the calls, later.
- **The record:** the member's recorded vote changes, and the public does not see why.

## Lines the bank needs

- **The asker**, by trait and tie: a peer with a long working history (warm, plain, a cost named), a rival (guarded, a price named), a party leader or the mayor (formal, a consequence named but not said), a constituent in the gallery (anxious, one reason). Each opens on the count ("one vote either way"), not on the bill's title, and says what they can offer or what the member would be spared.
- **The member's four replies (player and NPC):** switch ("I've heard enough to move."), hold ("I said how I'd vote and I'll stand there."), bargain ("What do I get for the bill I carried?"), ask for time ("Give me ten minutes."). Each varies by trait (steady, anxious, proud, guarded) and by tie to the asker.
