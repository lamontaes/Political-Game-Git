# Game Constitution

Status: **AUTHORITATIVE**

These principles govern product design, simulation design, content, UX, and implementation. Specifications and code must be interpreted consistently with them. If implementation exposes a conflict, record and resolve it explicitly rather than silently changing a principle.

Any amendment must explicitly identify the affected principle and preserve an auditable decision history.

1. The game is a political-life simulation. Life exists before, during, and after office. Politics is not required.

2. The world exists independently of the player. NPCs pursue careers, relationships, political activity, governing, failure, retirement, and death without player involvement.

3. Meaningful history is durable. Preserve what happened, when, where, context, participants, witnesses/knowledge, and later claims about events.

4. The player interacts with political circumstances rather than visible formulas. Hidden mathematical models are allowed. Do not expose persuasion-point requirements, relationship meters, political-capital currencies, or deterministic support thresholds as normal gameplay.

5. Consequences are contextual. An action does not automatically produce a universal bonus or penalty.

6. Characters are persistent histories, not simple stat blocks. Characters may have factual history, memories, beliefs, knowledge/expertise, personality tendencies, goals, relationships, perceptions, and public reputation.

7. Private belief, public position, campaign promise, and actual behavior are separate concepts. People may lie, compromise, evolve, contradict themselves, and change their minds.

8. Political beliefs are issue-specific and expandable. Characters form opinions sparsely rather than having predetermined opinions about every issue. Broad ideological principles may influence reasoning but do not force uniform ideology.

9. Government institutions and rules should be data-driven rather than unnecessarily hard-coded. Different jurisdictions can operate differently and rules may change during a save through appropriate mechanisms.

10. Legislation is composed of provisions. Support, opposition, amendments, negotiation, delay, agenda control, and procedural obstruction must be possible for substantive reasons. Passing legislation must not reduce to accumulating persuasion points.

11. Time and attention are fundamental player resources. Routine work is delegated or summarized. Greater responsibility normally produces more competing demands and potentially more meaningful gameplay per simulated period, not more clerical clicking.

12. Staff are persistent characters, not equipment bonuses. They may improve, decline, leave, disagree, develop expertise, build careers, and become politically important themselves.

13. Information is imperfect. Polls, rumors, advisers, politicians, and the player may all be wrong.

14. Expertise creates knowledge, options, questions, and arguments; it does not reveal objectively correct political answers.

15. Geography matters. Familiarity, reputation, coalitions, political relationships, and political appeal vary by place.

16. Populations, political coalitions, factions, parties, demographics, and institutions may evolve over decades.

17. Major events should generally arise from conditions, previous events, people, and decisions. Events may have prerequisites, blockers, modifiers, follow-ons, and consequences.

18. Randomness creates plausible variation rather than arbitrary nonsense. A world seed must make stochastic behavior reproducible where practical.

19. Failure remains gameplay. Losing elections, failing legislation, losing jobs, resigning, leaving office, or losing confirmation does not produce a generic game over.

20. Former officeholders remain people in the world and may retain informal political power.

21. Families can accumulate political history, wealth, relationships, reputation, expectations, enemies, and recognizable names across generations.

22. The simulation must be explainable after the fact. Histories must allow a player to understand how people, parties, places, institutions, and political conditions arrived at their present state.

23. Complexity should be explained through contextual UI, advisers, tooltips, reports, and archives rather than removed merely for accessibility.

24. “Holy shit, the game remembered that” is an explicit design objective. Specific old events should be capable of becoming relevant years or decades later when context makes them relevant.

25. Real-world starting data and simulated save-world history are distinct data domains. Real data must have source and as-of metadata. Once a save starts, simulated events become authoritative for that save.

26. Simulation complexity must not require computing every possible characteristic for every person every turn. Characters and geography may use progressive resolution, sparse beliefs, selective activation, and other scalable techniques.

27. NPC decisions should arise from beliefs, goals, personality, expertise, relationships, memories, perceived circumstances, institutional constraints, political incentives, information, and bounded randomness. NPCs are not globally optimal and may make mistakes.

28. NPCs and players should not have omniscient access to underlying probabilities or true support.

29. Relationships are histories between people rather than a single friendship score. Specific favors, betrayals, loyalties, disagreements, introductions, shared experiences, and third-party relationships matter.

30. The same simulation systems should operate in Observer Mode without a player character. A player may later take control of a persistent character and may branch the world.
