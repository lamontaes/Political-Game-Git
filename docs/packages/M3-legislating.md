# M3-legislating: write a bill, negotiate, pass or fail (golden path steps 6–8)

**Specs:** M3-01, M3-02 and M3-03.
**Built by:** OPUS-GOV, with OPUS-PEOPLE (members' lives, stakes and words) and OPUS-PLAY (the bill paper and meeting screens).
**Systems model:** *The Political Process*:
- 80+ kinds of legislation;
- committee hearings, amendments and floor votes, with members voting by ideology;
- the executive signs or vetoes;
- chairs decide which bills get a hearing, and leaders sway votes.

Our version adds members with real lives, real deals and played meetings.

## What you'll do

**Write a bill on paper.**
- Pick a subject from your accepted bill catalog: the 18 subject menus, each with its topics and deeper branches. Then pick what to do: raise, lower, expand, restrict, replace or repeal.
- The bill then shows only the sections that apply: current rules and programs, who's covered, the benefit or rate, funding, who runs it, enforcement and appeals, reporting, and timing.
- Set the real amounts, starting from the law in force, and phase them in if you want. You can also have your staff draft from a goal you give them.
- A bill can cover more than one subject.
- A cost estimate appears as you draft. The bill gets a real bill number.
- What you can pass depends on your level's real powers. A council can't pass what only a state can, and the city attorney warns you when the authority is disputed.

**Negotiate.**
- Each member's position comes from their own life and stakes. You only learn a stake the way your character could: a conversation, a staffer, the news or their voting record.
- Everything is tradeable:
  - changing the bill;
  - backing their item;
  - favors and endorsements;
  - threats and public pressure;
  - money and worse.
- A deal changes real terms, not a relationship number.
- Favors and promises are remembered and show up in your journal. There's no "you owe her" menu, and not every favor gets repaid. You can lie.

**The body votes.**
- The bill goes to committee, then amendments, then a floor vote, then the mayor, governor or president signs or vetoes it, and it takes effect on its date.
- Each body follows its real rules, and members can vote to change those rules.
- Meetings are played scenes. The items that matter play out and the rest are summarized.
- You vote one item at a time, in batches, or by standing instructions.
- Committee seats, chairs and leadership are earned through relationships and seniority, not points.
- The minority can delay, amend or filibuster where the rules allow. Poison pills and riders are possible.

**One engine** runs city councils, state legislatures and Congress.

## What gets built

- The bill paper:
  - the catalog in `docs/design/legislative-content-menus-v1.md` (owner-accepted Sept 26): the 18 subject menus and their drill-downs, the shared sections, one identity per policy with cross-links, the operations inside each subject, and no outcomes in the drafting controls;
  - which drill-downs and sections appear, and their wording patterns, come from the 222-law research in `data/research/bill-samples/`;
  - the existing law families are mapped into the catalog;
  - amounts from current law, and phase-ins;
  - staff drafts and multi-subject bills;
  - the cost estimate;
  - real numbering;
  - authority checks by level.
- Negotiation:
  - stakes learned through real routes;
  - trades that change terms;
  - favors and promises on the record;
  - lies;
  - money and corruption as reasons.
- One legislature engine:
  - committees, amendments, floor votes and executive action;
  - each chamber's real rules from official sources, changeable by vote;
  - seats and leadership earned through relationships and seniority;
  - minority tools;
  - ties broken by lot only where the law requires it.
- Meetings as played scenes, with the rest summarized.

## How you'll know it works

In a random place, from your council seat:
1. You write an ordinance with real amounts.
2. You learn two members' stakes and trade for their votes.
3. The council meets in a played scene and votes, each member for recorded reasons.
4. The mayor signs or vetoes it, and it takes effect on its date.
5. A bill the council can't legally pass gets the city attorney's warning.
