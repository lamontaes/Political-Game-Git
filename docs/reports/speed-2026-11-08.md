## What costs the time in year 4 (CPU profile, 163 s sampled)

- Campaigns engine: 62 s (38%). Inside it, town halls (`holdEvent`) 24.8 s, the day's campaign choice 24.2 s, statements to an audience (`makeStatements`) 20.7 s.
- A speech's event fan-out is the largest single piece: `deliverSpeech` 14.9 s, of which `emit` 10.9 s (each listener observes the event, then the views engine moves their stance, the drives engine decides a response, the life engine appraises).
- Garbage collection: 15.5 s (9.5%). Module-table `get`/`put`/`insert` and their copies: about 23 s self time (14%).
- Volume was counted with temporary counters that were not committed. Year 1 held 3 town halls (98 listeners). Year 2 held 1,281 (64,700 listeners). Year 3 held 6. Year 4 held 1,134, with 326 people checked per event, about 62 listeners per event and 78,700 listener reaches in all.

So the election year is cost per listener (about 240 microseconds each, most of it the shared event fan-out) times 79,000 listeners. Earlier speed work already cached and tuned the code on that path. Parameters are read once and table facades are reused. A few percent remains in single functions, but a 21 percent cut needs a change in how much is done, not how fast.

## What is still over the bar

Smackover and Gunnison election years are still over 120 seconds. The Gunnison election-year profile (165 s sampled) shows where the rest goes:

- Campaigns: 40 s, mostly each candidate's daily turn.
- Press: 25 s, mostly weekly interviews (arranging them 8 s, scoring the questions 10 s).
- News: 14.5 s, of which 13.6 s rebuilds the counted groups' audience whenever any member of a group has a birthday.
- Garbage collection: 13 s. Voting: 13 s.

## Fast-forward: what exists and what it costs

A development switch (its file was deleted with the fast clock, part 4; registered stopgap SG-SPEED-fast-forward-campaigns) takes each world campaign's turns four times as far apart. The first version skipped campaign turns outright, and a twenty-year run showed that left almost every race with no votes tallied, so it was replaced.

Twenty years of Upper Witter Gulch, Colorado, same place and seed, stride against full:

- Time: 1,596 seconds against 2,266 (the stride run shared the machine for part of it), about 1.4 times faster. Seconds per year grow in the full run, with election years climbing from 85 seconds in year 4 to 234 in year 18; the stride run's election years go from 76 to 116.
- People alive after twenty years: 9,292 against 9,284.
- Elections: the same races in the same years, none with zero candidates. Of 127 races both runs held, 85 have the same winner, and state and federal top vote shares differ by 7 points on average. That is drift between twin worlds, which is what a drift test wants, but it is not the same result race by race.

So the owner's twenty years in seconds is not met. The rest of the world is a flat 45 to 55 seconds a year spread over many engines, and nearly all of the town's people already sit in the calendar tier that plans only scheduled work. Seconds need the engines themselves (legislatures, press, voting, money) to step in months for what nobody is watching, and that is open.

A twenty-year run found and fixed a crash in 2032 (a relative living elsewhere who held a job and moved), which stopped every long run of that town before it reached year 8.

For the Dev Tester: `node --import tsx scripts/speed-years/fast-forward.ts --place <key> --seed <s> --years 20 --fast 1 --out <file>` runs a world and writes the people alive, every table's size and each race the town votes in, year by year. A small town takes about 25 minutes for twenty years that way; a town of Montpelier's size takes hours and its memory grows by about 600 MB a year, so it does not yet fit in 8 GB.

## Is local campaign volume too high? Researched, and no

Montpelier's election year holds 483 events by 31 candidates, 11,612 door rounds and 763 statement rounds. Before cutting any of it, the volume was checked against real campaigns.

- Spending per vote: the model's municipal candidates spend a median of $5,574 and $5.16 a vote in Montpelier, and $5,390 and $3.81 in Smackover. Real figures follow. Rockville, Maryland candidates in 2019 "spent between $3 to $10 per vote" (maxforrockville.com/2023/08/11/can-you-buy-your-way-onto-city-council/). The average Massachusetts mayoral candidate in 33 cities in 2023 spent $13.42 a vote (ocpf2.blob.core.windows.net/pdf/studies/2023mayoralstudy.pdf). Four small California cities in 2016 had a median of about $6,000 raised per council race (League of Women Voters of the Santa Clara Valley, local_election_finance_study.pdf).
- Doors: canvassing reached "between 14 and 45 percent of households" (Green, Gerber and Nickerson 2003, povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/getting-out-vote-us-local-elections). The model's municipal candidates in Montpelier knock a median of 2,930 doors in a city of about 3,500 households.
- Volunteers: 3.5 percent of adults worked for a party or candidate in 2020 (ANES, sda.berkeley.edu/sdaweb/docs/nes2020full/DOC/hcbk0027.htm), and 5 percent of adults in the year before the 2018 midterm (Pew, pewresearch.org/politics/2018/04/26/10-political-engagement-knowledge-and-the-midterms/). In Smackover 93 distinct people volunteer for some campaign, about 6 percent of its adults.

So money, doors and volunteers per local campaign are inside the real ranges, and no cut to local volume is justified. Real counts of events per candidate and volunteers per campaign were not found. The election-year cost in Montpelier is the cost of each action plus memory, not too much happening.

## Memory and long runs (Memory session, Nov 8)

Montpelier, Vermont (key 5046000, seed mont1) can now finish its election year inside memory: year 4 ends at 2.8 GB of heap instead of 7.7 GB, and takes 504 seconds instead of 1,012. Twenty years do not yet fit under 8 GB; election years still add about 1 GB each.

What grew, and what I changed:

- **Group ties, not coworker ties.** The brief blamed hires meeting every coworker. In Montpelier the `coworker` kind stayed flat at about 18,300. Year 2's growth was 22,978 `met-in-group` ties. They are made when a person joins a group and meets every member. The fix, #7455, lets a join or a hire meet only as many people as a group that size lets one know. That is a power law of its size, never over 150, estimated, with the spread stated in `parameters.json`. Year-2 relationships fell from 61,884 to 44,215, the durable log from 82,667 to 38,637 rows (the player's circle is the people the player's people know), and year-3 heap from 2,720 to 2,043 MB. This is a behavior change: the records proof differs widely.
- **Folded causes for the circle.** 72 circle voters held 6,674 folded campaign causes at year 2, and 38,608 by year 3 on main. #7540 drops those older than three months (the circle's act window); its records proof shows one differing table.

Still growing, per election year (cap branch, years 3 to 7): impressions 115,000 rows, folded causes 78,000 (before #7540), cause summaries 44,000, campaign money 36,000, `candidacy.decisions` 27,000, the voting `places` table 40,000, and the durable log's campaign gifts and endorsements (public records). Those need agreed windows with the campaigns owner.

By estimated bytes at year 6 the biggest is each person's knowledge (`core.knowledgeByPerson`, about 880 MB for 10,700 people, 2.2 million facts), mostly heard promises (`promise:<candidate>:<speech>:<question>`, one per listener per statement), `candidate:` facts and the cause-group `known` facts.

Why no fold here is exact: each of these tables is read by a later election, a screen or a scene (a voter's heard promises are weighed at the candidate's next election, `voting.ts` line 813; `impressions` carry a voter's warmth into the next run). Dropping or summarizing them changes what voters remember, so each fold needs its reader windows agreed with the campaigns, voting and record owners, and some change play (a lost candidate who runs again would start cold). These are the owner's calls through the Coordinator.

What would reach twenty years in seconds and under 8 GB is not another fold but what the owner said at 1:58 a.m.: simulate the years nobody is watching coarsely, with places and offices as aggregates, and build detail when someone looks. That is the Speed session's fast-forward.

## Night's end: a year got slower, and why (Nov 9)

Gunnison, Utah (seed n8f6, key 4932660), four years, one process: year 4 took 171.7 seconds on main d88e748d91 and 225.7 on d0b476a228. A search through the 78 merges between them, one at a time, found one merge that adds more than 5 percent: pull request 7831, cabinet candidates drawn from residents (cf68cf8b23). Years 2 to 4 went from 119.5, 134.2 and 171.1 seconds on its parent to 149.7, 166.9 and 235.0; year 1 did not move. The cabinet code itself costs about 1 second; the rest is spread over campaigns, medical billing, table writes and garbage collection, which fits more full people. It is with the Legislation session. The door path was measured as a possible cut and is not one: a full person's answered doors cost 6.7 seconds of a 215-second year 4 (the vote answer 4.2, the contact tie 1.5, the impression 0.65, the retelling 0.33).

Montpelier, Vermont (seed mont1), twenty years with the development fast-forward on, main d0b476a228, stopped in year 6 when the container ran out of memory at 7.8 GB, with a second heavy run beside it. Years 2 to 4 took 288, 297 and 521 seconds, and year 1 is not a clean time. Full people alive grew from 1,626 to 3,851. Process memory reached 7.0 GB by year 4 against an 8 GB limit, so twenty years in Montpelier do not fit. Still over the bar: Gunnison years 3 and 4, Montpelier years 2 to 4, Washington, D.C. year 4. The best twenty-year run is Upper Witter Gulch's, far from the owner's five seconds.
