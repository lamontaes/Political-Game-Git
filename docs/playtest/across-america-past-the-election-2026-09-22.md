# Running for office across America, and what happens after you win

**Walked** 2026-09-22 between 16:00Z and 17:00Z on `main` at `1c4992e8`, in
**Chromium 141 from the container's own browser bundle, not the version CI
runs**. Twenty-six lives: sixteen towns read on their Campaigns screen, and ten
carried from filing past election day. Every life was made through the ordinary
creator — normal start, age 40, a drawn name, no fixtures, no injected records,
no developer menu. Screenshots were taken at each decisive moment and are handed
over with this record; they are deliberately not in the repository.

This picks up where `running-for-office-across-america-2026-09-22.md` stopped.
That walk covered fifteen towns and stopped at election night. This one goes to
the places it did not — the one unicameral legislature, towns split across
districts, consolidated city-counties, Washington D.C., a second Puerto Rico
town — and then carries lives past the result into what follows.

---

## The short version

1. **Winning gives you a seat in Illinois and nothing at all in Kentucky.** Two
   lives won the same seat on the same day with the same share. One walked into
   a legislature. The other was told she holds no office in this life yet.
2. **A player can lose.** The first four lives that reached a result all lost.
   The earlier record that a player wins every time was right about its own
   method and wrong as a statement about the game.
3. **The campaign is decided by how many days you pressed.** Nothing: 40 percent.
   Four door-knocking sessions: a coin flip. Twenty-seven: 81 percent. Four
   sessions on the phones is worse than doing nothing at all.
4. **Nebraska is closed — governor included — because the game's own research
   was carried out eight months after the game begins.**
5. **Ohio is closed in both chambers** by a rule nobody can ever satisfy.
6. **Washington, D.C. calls itself "City of Washington Dc"** and refuses
   candidacy in the same words as an unread state.
7. **Louisville has no government.** Nor does San Juan, Ponce or Honolulu.
8. **Columbus's home screen adds up to 101 percent** — the same rounding defect
   fixed on election night this morning, in a second place.

9. **A won governorship seats you, if you press "Qualify for the term".**
   Walked later the same day on `4965f63c` in Reno and Springfield: qualify
   in November and you are Governor on 4 January 2027. Skip it and the term
   is lost for good. See section 10.

## 1. What happens after you win depends on which state you are in

This is the finding with the most behind it, and it is two screens.

Two lives, both at 81 percent, both deciding on **2 February 2026**, both a seat
in their state's house of representatives, both after twenty-seven sessions
knocking on doors.

**Dawn Ortega, Galena, Illinois** — the result reads:

> Dawn Ortega won. The seat is theirs, and so is everything that came before it.
>
> Dawn Ortega (them) — 81.4%
> Martin Terry — 18.6%

Her office screen, opened straight after, is a working legislature:

> Dawn works for the Illinois legislature, so what is in front of the chamber is
> in front of them too.
>
> **How this office works** — Illinois · house. Preferences stay with this
> office term.
>
> **Voting workflow** — Leave instructions, then handle exceptions (suggested) /
> Review votes in a batch / Handle each vote yourself
>
> **Constituent casework** — Staff handle routine requests; bring exceptions to
> me / I will handle constituent work myself / Staff handle the casework and
> brief me

**Jasmine Carlson, Lexington, Kentucky** — the result reads:

> Jasmine Carlson won. **The supported term begins 2027-01-01; the result itself
> grants no current office authority.**
>
> Jasmine Carlson (them) — 81.2%
> John Palmer — 18.8%

Her office screen, opened straight after, and again ten weeks later:

> **You hold no office in this life yet.** Running for one is under Campaigns
> when the game supports it here.

She won. The screen tells her she has never run for anything, in a state where
the game plainly does support it, and will keep telling her that for eleven
months of game time she has no way to spend.

One of these two is wrong and the game does not know which. Kentucky's gap is
the closer match to how American legislatures work; Illinois seating a winner
the same afternoon is not. But an eleven-month gap in which the player holds
nothing, is told they hold nothing, and is given no word for what they are, is
not the right answer either. **Filed as research:
`legislative-winner-between-election-and-seat`** — what a winner holds between
election night and the seat, whether there is a real status such as member-elect,
and what that status can do.

Worth adding: the Illinois office, reached and real, is also empty. "No workflow
is recorded yet." "No staff are recorded for this office." "Nothing has been
filed yet." That is the pattern already recorded in
`reachable-and-empty-2026-09-22.md`, now confirmed one layer deeper than anyone
had reached before.

## 2. A player can lose, and the campaign is what decides it

The earlier record says a player won eleven of eleven, 71.3 to 90.5 percent.
That is correct about that walk. It is not a property of the game. On the same
head, in the same towns, ten lives produced this:

| Town            | Sessions taken | What they were | Result                 |
| --------------- | -------------- | -------------- | ---------------------- |
| Louisville, KY  | 0              | —              | **lost** 40.6% – 59.4% |
| Minneapolis, MN | 0              | —              | **lost** 44.1% – 55.9% |
| Galena, IL      | 4              | on the phones  | **lost** 40.7% – 59.3% |
| Louisville, KY  | 4              | on the phones  | **lost** 38.4% – 61.6% |
| Lexington, KY   | 4              | on the doors   | **lost** 48.8% – 51.2% |
| Louisville, KY  | 4              | on the doors   | **won** 50.8% – 49.2%  |
| Galena, IL      | 4              | on the doors   | **won** 50.0% – 50.0%  |
| Galena, IL      | 27             | on the doors   | **won** 81.4% – 18.6%  |
| Lexington, KY   | 27             | on the doors   | **won** 81.2% – 18.8%  |

Three things fall out of that table.

**The dose is the whole game.** Between doing nothing and knocking on doors
every day there is a forty-point swing, and the twenty-seven-session lives land
exactly where the earlier walk's eleven lives landed. Nothing regressed; that
walk pressed the day control and this one pressed the week, and the week control
only lets a candidate do one thing with it.

**Four sessions on the phones is worse than doing nothing.** 38.4 percent
against 40.6 percent, same town, same seat. The committee holds USD 0.00 at
filing and still holds it after four sessions of fundraising, and the screen
never says that a session spent on the phones is a session not spent on doors.
A player who reads "Spend a session on the phones" as sensible campaign work is
being punished for it silently.

**Galena printed a tie and declared a winner.** 50.0 percent against 50.0
percent, with "Evelyn Flynn won. The seat is theirs" above it. Whatever broke
the tie is not on the screen, and a player who adds the two numbers is owed an
explanation.

## 3. Nothing at all happens after the election

Every life was carried between six and sixteen weeks past election day. In every
one of them, the campaign screen froze on the day it was decided and stayed
there:

> WRIGHT FOR THE HOUSE OF REPRESENTATIVES · DECIDED FEBRUARY 2, 2026
> **Running against Aiko Knight.**

That present tense is still on the screen on 27 April, twelve weeks after the
race ended. "Running against" a person nobody is running against any more.

The front page, read on 27 April in Louisville after a contested state house
election the player had personally lost, across all four outlets:

> CIVIC LEDGER — The governments in the fishing-rights talks announced an
> interim arrangement. April 26, 2026
> THE MERIDIAN DISPATCH — Several governments opened talks over fishing rights
> in shared waters. April 21, 2026
> LONGWIRE PUBLIC AFFAIRS — Several governments opened talks over fishing rights
> in shared waters. April 21, 2026
> CIVIC LEDGER — The national unemployment rate for March 2026 was reported at
> 4.9 percent. April 1, 2026

Fishing rights, unemployment, shipping delays. **The player's own election is not
news, in the state it was held in, in any outlet, at any point.** Two of those
outlets print one sentence word for word on the same day, which is the
already-recorded newspaper behaviour showing up again.

So the plain answer to "carry a life past a first election into the events that
follow" is that outside Illinois there are none. The race ends, the screen
stops, and the world does not notice.

## 4. Nebraska is closed, governor and all, by a date

Omaha and Chadron — the biggest city in the state and a town of five thousand —
both read, under Running for office:

> You can't run for office in Nebraska this early. The game knows Nebraska's
> rules for who may stand, but not whether they were already in force this far
> back, and it won't apply a rule to a time it can't place it in. A life that
> starts later may be able to run here.

The same sentence appears under The state's top office. There, "Put your name
in" is on the screen — and **disabled**. Nebraska is the first state measured
where the governor's route is closed too.

The cause is not a gap in research. It is the opposite. Every one of Nebraska's
thirteen qualification rows in `src/simulation/office-qualifications.generated.ts`
carries `CURRENT_OBSERVATION` observed on **2026-09-09**. The game opens on
**2026-01-05**. `qualificationTemporalApplicability` in
`src/simulation/office-qualification-rules.ts` correctly refuses to apply a rule
to a date before the date its evidence was observed — so the research closes the
state it researched, eight months before it was carried out.

**Minnesota has the same defect, halfway.** All six Minnesota rows are observed
2026-09-09 too, and three of them are LOWER_CHAMBER rows. So in Minneapolis and
Moorhead the House seat refuses with that sentence and **the Senate seat files
normally** — because the only Minnesota upper-chamber row is a term length, and
the Senate passes precisely because the game knows less about it. More research
closed the seat.

This is engineering, not law, and the law half is already filed as
`mn-ne-constitutional-provision-commencement` (P1, blocks-work). What this walk
adds is that it closes a whole state including its governorship, that it is
mechanical rather than particular to Nebraska, and that **every state researched
from here on will close the same way on day one** unless the opening date and
the observation date are reconciled. Five of the seven researched states escape
only because their rows happen to carry `EXACT_INTERVAL` dates from the 1950s
to the 1990s.

## 5. Ohio is closed by a rule nobody can satisfy

Columbus and Athens, both read on screen:

> This office requires that the candidate is a qualified elector. The game does
> not record that about a character, so it neither grants nor refuses on it.

No office browser renders under it. Ohio has a shipped pack with both chambers
and a player cannot stand for either, in a big city or a small town.

Athens is the control that makes this the elector rule rather than anything
else: Columbus is split across three districts and refuses on that ground first;
Athens is whole, and refuses on the elector requirement alone.

The rule is real and correctly sourced — Ohio Const. art. XV, § 4, an exact
interval from 1953-11-03, applied to both chambers and to the three statewide
executives. The gap is that no fact about a person exists for it to be applied
to. **Filed as research: `elector-requirement-what-establishes-it`** — what the
phrase decomposes into, and which of its conditions a life simulation already
knows about a person.

One thing to fix without research, on the same screen: the elector sentence is a
**refusal** under Running for office, where it removes the office browser, and a
**caveat** under The state's top office, where "Put your name in" is right below
it and enabled, and the governor's race files. One sentence doing two opposite
jobs on one screen.

## 6. Being split across districts only matters where a state has a residence rule

Worth writing down because two earlier claims looked contradictory. Baltimore,
Chicago and Kansas City are all split across both chambers' districts in
`src/districts/place-membership.generated.json`, and all three filed and won in
the earlier walk. Columbus, split the same way, refuses:

> This office requires 1 year of residence for the district. This character's
> town lies across more than one district, so the game cannot say which one they
> live in, and it will not pick one to answer for them.

The split is not what refuses. **The split plus a district-residence rule is.**
Ohio carries `DISTRICT_RESIDENCE = RESIDENT_1_YEAR`; Illinois, Maryland and
Missouri carry no district-residence row at all, so their split cities file
freely. Alaska's rule is in its legislature pack, which is why Anchorage refuses
and Sitka does not. Nebraska's unicameral chamber carries one too — so even with
the date problem fixed, Omaha and Lincoln, the two largest cities in the state,
would still refuse.

A town's own screen says nothing about this. Columbus lists its three counties
with land shares and never mentions districts until the player tries to file.

## 7. Washington, D.C.

D.C. has exactly one town in the game, and this is the first walk to enter it.
Anna Vincent, 40, Washington, District of Columbia. Her home screen reads:

> Who governs where you live
> **City of Washington Dc**
> The Census Government Units listing records no county government for this
> city's county area.

"City of Washington Dc" is not a thing anybody calls anything. The
`residentPlaceName` repair that fixed "Nashville-Davidson metropolitan
government (balance)" strips a trailing lowercase unit type; D.C.'s Census row
survives it and comes out title-cased into a name no resident would recognise.

Under Running for office she gets the unread-state sentence, word for word:

> The game has not read this state's elected offices yet, so there is nothing to
> stand for here. It will not borrow another state's rules to fill the gap.

That is now **seven jurisdictions** confirmed on that sentence — Maine, Georgia,
Arizona, Tennessee, Montana, Puerto Rico and D.C. — and D.C. is the one where it
is most obviously wrong, because the District has a Council, a Mayor and a
delegate, none of which are "another state's rules".

Under The state's top office she gets the correct one: "This life is not set in
one of the fifty states, so there is no state executive office to stand for."

Puerto Rico behaves identically in **both** San Juan and Ponce, which makes it a
property of the jurisdiction rather than of the capital. Both already have an
open question, `national-range-instrument-for-non-states`.

## 8. Four of the largest consolidated governments in America have no government

Louisville, San Juan, Ponce and Urban Honolulu all read, on the screen headed
"Who governs where you live":

> No city or town government is recorded for this place.
> No county government is recorded for this place's county areas as the Census
> described them on [date].

Louisville has a metro government and six hundred thousand people. Honolulu has
a consolidated city and county. Both come up blank on the one screen whose whole
job is to say who governs where the player lives — and Louisville then goes on
to offer both Kentucky chambers and file a candidacy normally, so the blank is
cosmetic rather than a refusal, which is arguably worse: the player is told
nobody governs them and then runs for office anyway.

The other consolidated city-counties walked all render correctly: "City and
County of Denver", "City and County of San Francisco", "City of Philadelphia",
"City of New Orleans", "City of Indianapolis". So this is a handful of rows, not
the mechanism.

## 9. Columbus adds up to 101 percent

Columbus, Ohio, on the same home screen:

> City of Columbus
> County of Franklin — 98 percent of this place's land
> County of Delaware — 2 percent of this place's land
> County of Fairfield — 1 percent of this place's land

98 + 2 + 1 = 101. The cause is one line,
`src/player/NationwideCandidacyWorkspace.tsx:98`:

```tsx
? ` — ${Math.round(landShare * 100)} percent of this place's land`
```

Each share is rounded on its own. This is **exactly** the defect fixed on
election night this morning and merged as #378, where 71.95 and 28.05 both
rounded up and the screen printed 100.1 percent. The fix that shipped there —
`displayedSharePercents`, rounding by largest remainder — is a general-purpose
helper and applies here unchanged.

## Third-person: how many places can you actually run in

Nine states ship a legislature rule pack: Kentucky, Nebraska, Alaska, Minnesota,
Illinois, Maryland, Missouri, Nevada and Ohio
(`src/simulation/legislature-rule-packs.ts`). The other forty-three jurisdictions
of fifty-two give the unread-state refusal. That is the real denominator behind
"six states confirmed, forty-five unchecked" — the unchecked ones are not a
mystery, they are the forty-three without a pack.

Of the nine that do ship one, this walk closes two: **Nebraska entirely**
(dates) and **Ohio entirely** (elector), and half-closes a third, **Minnesota's
House** (dates), with Alaska's and Nebraska's split cities refusing on district
residence on top of that. What is left is **seven jurisdictions out of
fifty-two** where a player can stand for a legislative seat at all — and the
states most likely to refuse are the ones that have been researched most
carefully.

## 10. Winning a governorship, walked to the office (added 17:50Z)

Walked 2026-09-22 between 17:25Z and 17:50Z on `main` at **`4965f63c`**, the
same container browser as the rest of this record. Three lives, ordinary
creator, normal start, age 40, a drawn name. The question left open earlier in
the day was whether a player who wins a governorship can ever hold it. An
earlier Reno life, Ariana Moore, had won and then read "The term that ran from
January 4, 2027 to January 6, 2031 is over or was never entered". **The answer
is yes, and that earlier life had skipped one button.**

**Ariana Moore, Reno, Nevada** (seed `gov-reno-nv`). She filed with "Put your
name in" under _The state's top office_ on Campaigns, then did one session on
the doors and pressed Week, every week. The Campaigns screen ran through three
states:

> You are on the ballot. The campaign itself is run from your office and campaigns.

and then, the week after the 3 November election:

> You won. The term runs from January 4, 2027 to January 6, 2031, and you must
> qualify before it begins.

Under that sentence is **one enabled button, "Qualify for the term"**
(`qualify-state-executive`). The top of the same screen says "You do not hold a
job or an office right now. An offer of work as Governor is waiting for your
answer." The answer to that offer is this button, on Campaigns. The offer line
does not say so, and the Jobs screen has no Accept for Governor. Pressing it on
Monday 9 November 2026 changed the status at once to:

> You have qualified. The term begins January 4, 2027.

On **Monday 4 January 2027**, with nothing else pressed, the status read "You
hold this office until January 6, 2031", and the Office tab opened a working
governorship: "Your role: Governor", "Governor of Nevada — Your term runs until
January 6, 2031. No chief of staff yet."

**Emma Villanueva, Springfield, Illinois** (seed `gov-springfield-il`), did the
same things and got the same result on the same dates: qualified on 9 November,
Governor of Illinois on 4 January 2027. **So in Illinois a governorship seats
you the same way the legislature does, and Nevada now behaves like Illinois,
not like Kentucky.** That was the question in the handoff, and the difference
between the states is not in the governorship.

Of the three things the code needs before it seats a governor
(`activeElectedExecutiveTermEvidence`, `src/simulation/executive-work-context.ts:262`),
**none refused**. The qualification is the button. The work status and the
term's entry step are handled by the clock on the start date
(`electedExecutiveTermTransitionHandler`, `src/simulation/executive-work-entry.ts`),
with no second press. "Term over or never entered" happens only when the start
date passes without a qualification, and `stateExecutiveEntryStatus` documents
that as intended.

**What a new governor finds is empty.** Both offices say, on the first
morning:

> Needs you — Nothing is waiting on you right now.
> No bill reached Governor of Nevada this session: the game has not compiled
> NV's legislature, so it files no measures.
> What this office is answerable for — No program of this government has a
> record here yet — no service capacity, no appropriation and no commitment.
> Who works here — Nobody is recorded as working for this office.

The Illinois office reads the same, with "the game has not compiled IL's
legislature", and it has an "Office work — Work for 30 minutes" control.
The only recent entry under "What came of it" is the outgoing governor's
December budget request.
The same shape as the empty legislature in section 1:
**you can reach the office, but on day one there is nothing to do in it.**

**The legislative seat, rechecked on `4965f63c`.** Paola Evans, Reno (seed
`leg-reno-nv`), filed for the Assembly, did one session on the doors and pressed
Day, 27 sessions in all. The election was decided on 2 February 2026; the
field memo put her "somewhere around 74 percent". On election day the Office tab
already read "Paola works for the Nevada legislature, so what is in front of the
chamber is in front of them too", with the voting-workflow and casework
choices. **Same-day seating still holds on current main.** A second run of the
same seed, pressing Week (four sessions), had no office on 23 February. This
walk did not capture that race's result line. It fits the dose curve in
section 2 but proves nothing about it.

## What this walk did not establish

- **Whether the Illinois legislature does anything.** The office is reachable and
  its surfaces are empty on arrival. Nobody has taken a bill through it from a
  seat won in play.
- **Whether a seated member ever faces re-election.** No life was carried far
  enough.
- **Forty-three jurisdictions.** The refusal sentence was read in seven. The
  other thirty-six are inferred from the pack list, not walked.
- **Whether the tie in Galena is reproducible**, or a one-seed coincidence.
  One occurrence, one seed.
- **Anything about money.** Every committee in every life held USD 0.00 from
  filing to election day, in ten lives, including four that spent sessions
  fundraising. That is consistent with
  `what-campaign-sessions-are-missing-2026-09-22.md` and was not investigated
  further here.

## How this was run

Two Playwright walks of my own, written outside the suite and not added to it,
for the same reason the last one was not: browser shard duration is the binding
constraint on how often anything merges here, and a reporting walk is a measuring
instrument rather than a gate. Each case is a fresh browser with storage cleared.
The walks assert almost nothing — they press the controls a player presses, read
the text off the screen, and write it down, so a town behaving unexpectedly is
recorded rather than failing the run. Per-town JSON and screenshots are written
outside the repository.

One thing had to be written by hand rather than reused. `tests/e2e/support/creator.ts`
refuses any town whose name is also a state's — `resolveExplicitCreatorHometown`
throws "a state name is not a hometown" — which makes **Washington, D.C.
unreachable through the shared helper**. The game has no such rule; a player
types "Washington" into the town box and presses the town. Any future spec that
wants D.C., or Nevada, Missouri, or Kansas City, Kansas, will hit the same wall.
