# A morning's play, on main at 453b6893

Walked 2026-09-22 from 07:11Z, on `main` at `453b6893` (#321). Chromium 141
locally, which is not the version CI runs. Every life below was made through
the ordinary creator — no fixtures, no injected records, no developer menu.
Where a route does not exist, that is the finding, not an invitation to force
it.

## The finding

**Three things are wrong in the first two minutes of play, and all three are
sentences rather than systems.** The Politics menu says it is about jobs and
study. Alaska refuses a new character with a sentence about recorded intervals.
And one line about fishing rights sits on top of every screen there is.

None of the three is a missing feature, a broken system or a hard problem. All
three are words on a surface, and all three are the first words a new player
reads. You can check every one of them yourself in about two minutes.

Underneath them the systems are in better shape than the surfaces suggest, and
that gap — between how much is built and how much a player would ever find — is
the largest single thing this walk measured.

---

Three lists, kept apart on purpose, because tonight turned on the difference
four separate times:

- **Broken** — a player does the ordinary thing and gets a wrong or unusable result.
- **Built but unreachable** — it works, and there is no way in from the game.
- **Reachable but unfindable** — it works, you can get there, and nothing tells you it exists.

---

## Broken

### Alaska refuses an ordinary life, and the sentence it refuses with is machine language

This is the measurement that had not been taken. A forty-year-old made in
Sitka, Alaska through the creator, on day one, reaches Politics → Campaigns and
is told:

> The recorded district-residence interval does not yet satisfy the sourced
> 1-year requirement.

There is no office browser at all. Nothing can be filed for.

Two separate problems in one sentence.

The first is that it refuses — and the apparent disagreement about this turns
out not to be a disagreement at all.

A resolver-side report was read as saying Alaska's house and senate had moved
from refused to admitted. `docs/systems/nationwide-rule-coverage.md` on this
head says, in the Alaska row, "lower: refused; upper: refused" under
qualifications, and 2/2 under a different column, "seats with candidacy
admitted". Those are two columns about two things.

And the report's own fourth line says what it means:

> Rule admission only. An admitted field is a compiled, dated rule the resolver
> will apply; it is not proof that a player can reach the action, that an office
> or contest exists in a save, or that every clause of local law was read.

So the document disclaims the exact inference that was drawn from it. It never
said a player could file in Alaska; it said a rule had been compiled and dated.
The screen and the report agree. What disagreed was a claim built on top of the
report, which has now been retracted three times tonight and can stop being
retested.

The second is the sentence itself. "The recorded district-residence interval
does not yet satisfy the sourced 1-year requirement" is the machinery
describing its own bookkeeping to a player. A person would be told they have
not lived in the district long enough yet, and when they will have.

There is a passing test called "a district seat is filed for through the
district screen" that walks this exact Alaska route and goes green. It builds
its character by injecting seven hundred days of recorded Sitka residence
straight into the save store before it starts. It proves the fixture path. Its
title does not say so, and anyone reading the suite would reasonably conclude
Alaska filing works for a player.

### The Politics menu entry still describes the wrong thing

The menu reads:

> **Politics** — Jobs and study

Behind it are your office, campaigns, the government where you live, parties,
and the budget. A player looking for any of those has been told the entry is
about jobs and study. The fix is written and is sitting in an unmerged pull
request, so it is still broken for anyone playing this build.

### The starting place still defaults to Kentucky in code

`DEFAULT_NEW_GAME_SETUP` in `src/presentation/new-game.ts` carries
`placeKey: "kentucky"`. Its own comment says a fresh creator uses an empty place
key instead and that the default is there for old callers and encoded replays,
which is a reasonable design. The effect is still that everything built on that
default — helpers, tests, any caller that does not name a place — starts in
Kentucky. A player choosing a place is unaffected. Anyone reading the code, or
running a check that does not name a place, is looking at Lexington.

There is a second half to this, found while taking the seating counts below.
`lifePlaces()` returns ten authored places — Kentucky, Nebraska, Alaska,
Lexington-Fayette and six bare state entries. The rest of the country is
reachable only through `searchLifePlaces`, which reads the nationwide
catalogue. So a caller that lists places sees ten, and a caller that names no
place sees Kentucky. Those two together explain most of why this game keeps
looking like it is set in one state.

### The same sentence is printed at the top of every screen in the game

This one appears to be new and unreported, and after the menu label it is the
most visible thing in this walk.

> Several governments opened talks over fishing rights in shared waters.

That line sits at the top of the content on every screen reached, in a
thirty-four-year-old's first session in Augusta, Maine. Named, in the order they
were walked:

1. **Who you are** — above her name, age, household, schooling and work.
2. **People** — above the four people she knows.
3. **News** — above the front page, where the same sentence then appears again
   as an actual story, so a player reads it twice on one screen.
4. **Parties** — above the county chapters.
5. **The Guide** — above the civics definitions.
6. **Campaigns** — above the refusal about standing for office.

It is the identical sentence every time, and in a second life started in
Columbus, Ohio it was again the identical sentence on Government, Budget and
Campaigns. So it is not following the player's own situation; it is one world
event pinned to every surface.

**It is the television in the room.** Measured afterwards by walking a fourth
life, in Baltimore, Maryland, and reading the page itself rather than guessing:
the sentence lives in
`[data-testid="scene-surface-living-room-television"]`, inside the scene
backdrop. It is not a banner and not a ticker. It is a working television,
standing in the living room, and the room stays behind every screen the shell
opens — so the television is visible while the player reads People, or Parties,
or the Guide.

What it shows is the front page's lead story. `projectDynamicSurfaces` in
`src/presentation/surface-projection.ts` fills a `headline` surface fact from
`projectPublicInformationHeadline`, which takes `items[0]` of the public
information digest. That is deliberate and the code says so, in a comment
worth quoting because it tells you the design is honest:

> Empty means no explicit publication has occurred; rendering must not
> manufacture one to make a television busy.

So nothing here is inventing news. The television is correct. Two things about
it are still wrong from a chair in front of the game.

The first is that a full-screen workspace does not hide the room, so one
sentence appears pinned above every screen in the game. The second is that on
a first day the lead does not move: there is one story, so the television
shows that one story for as long as it stays the lead. On the News screen you
then read it twice at once — once on the television and once as the front
page's own lead, which the DOM confirms, the second copy sitting in
`[data-testid="news-lead"]`.

**Qualified 2026-09-22 09:40Z.** A later walk let ten weeks run in Baltimore
and the line changed five times, on a one-to-three-week rhythm, telling a
story rather than shuffling — shipping delays, the delays continuing, a
January unemployment figure, shipping recovering, a February figure. So the
complaint that survives is the spatial one: one line visible everywhere at
once. "It never changes" is a property of a first day, not of the game.
`ten-weeks-in-baltimore-2026-09-22.md` has the table.

That makes it a presentation fix rather than a simulation one, and it is a
smaller fix than the walk first suggested: either the room stops showing
through a workspace, or the television stops being a news repeater when the
news is already on the screen.

### Three papers still print one sentence, except when they do not

The front page after sixty days, with a party founding driven through the
simulation's own writers, carries thirteen stories from three outlets. Five of
them are word-for-word duplicates of another story on the same page.

What works, and it works well:

| Civic Arc Broadcasting | Two organizers decided to form The Commons Party. |
| The Evening Compass | Hailey Harper and Vincent Hicks decided to form The Commons Party. |

That is two papers telling the same news in their own voice, and the difference
has a reason behind it rather than being noise — a broadcaster has no room to
read two names aloud. It is a real improvement on what this lane measured a few
hours ago, which was "2 organizers publicly decided to form The Commons Party"
printed identically by everyone.

What does not work is everything that is not a party story:

| Civic Arc Broadcasting | Talks over fishing rights in shared waters continued without an agreement. |
| The Evening Compass | Talks over fishing rights in shared waters continued without an agreement. |
| Civic Ledger | Talks over fishing rights in shared waters continued without an agreement. |

Identical, all three. The same for the interim arrangement, three times, and for
February unemployment, twice.

So the machinery for giving a paper its own voice exists, is proven, and is
wired to one kind of story. Every other kind still prints the record's own note
to itself. This is the more useful version of a finding this lane reported
earlier as a flat "three papers print one sentence" — that was true then and is
now only two-thirds true, and which third was fixed is the part worth knowing.

### The world only has so much to say

Underneath the repetition is a smaller world than the front page implies. The
bank of things that can happen beyond your own life is four local subjects —
opening hours at a public facility, a road repair schedule, reserving a park
shelter, a recycling drop-off site — and two international storylines, each of
which runs in three stages: reported, continued, then eased.

So a paper covering "talks opened", "talks continued" and "an interim
arrangement" is not repeating itself. It is following the only story there is,
faithfully, to its end. The repetition a player sees is two separate things
stacked: the same sentence from several papers, and only six things in the
world to write about.

That second one is content rather than a defect, and it is the single largest
lever on whether the newspaper feels like a newspaper.

## Personality: the system landed, the screen did not

Two gaps here, both real, both narrow, and neither is a break. They are worth
separating because from a player's chair they look identical to "it was never
built", and it was built.

### A trait added to a pack in the build works, and nobody will ever see it

**Corrected 2026-09-22 08:25Z.** This section first said traits were
"pack-driven" in the modding sense, which overstated it. The pack _shape_ is
real and the simulation honours it: `loadedTraitRegistry()` composes
`peopleTraitPack()` and `legislatureTraitPack()`, and `people-contact` and
`people-trait-change` both read the result, so a trait added to one of those
packs genuinely affects how somebody can be approached and how they change.

But both packs are compiled into the build. `loadTraitPacks` is never called
with anything else anywhere in the tree, and a runtime content pack — the only
thing a mod can install — carries durations and scenes and nothing else. So a
**mod** cannot add a trait today. See
`docs/playtest/policy-catalogue-2026-09-22.md` for the measurement.

The one screen that shows a person's temperament does not read it. `PersonCard`
calls `observedTraitLabels`, which walks a five-element list written into the
code — deliberation, sociability, conflict, reliability, risk. A sixth trait
from a pack is loaded, is doing work in the simulation, and cannot appear on
the only surface that would show it.

So the modding story is half-connected: the engine takes the content, the game
does not show it.

### You cannot see what you are like

`PersonCard` carries an explicit exclusion: "Temperament is shown for other
people only, never for the one played." A player can read a temperament on
anybody in the world except themselves.

There is a stated reason underneath it — the played character's temperament
never decides anything for them, because the player decides — and that is
coherent. But the owner's own framing is that the player has traits "because it
is how they are portrayed to others", and a player who cannot see how they are
portrayed cannot act on it. Whether that exclusion is right is a design
question, not a defect, and it belongs to him rather than to this lane.

What is not in question is that neither of these is a missing system. The
system is there.

## Reachable but unfindable

### The Guide

Twenty-eight plain-language definitions — adjournment, appropriation, caucus,
committee referral, concurrence — and it opens with "What the words on the
other screens mean. Reading an entry does nothing in the world." It is good and
it is honest. It sits behind a menu group called "Guide and options", next to
settings and the build number, which reads as a place where preferences live
rather than the game's own civics primer. Nothing on any screen that uses these
words points at it.

### Party chapters, and what they lead to

The parties screen offers real things: two county chapters with named
organizers, an organizing meeting, a door canvass, a phone shift, a community
town hall, "Talk about running for office", and "Join as a volunteer". It says
plainly that coming to any of it is not joining, endorsing or voting. All of
that is reachable in three presses and none of it is mentioned anywhere else.

## What a first day actually looks like

A thirty-four-year-old in Augusta, Maine. The world knows her schooling from
1997, her job at a neighbourhood market from 2008, who she lives with, and four
people she knows — her mother among them. Money and the local economy both say
plainly that nothing is on record yet, which is the right answer rather than a
zero.

The paper on day one carries two stories: the fishing-rights sentence, and
"City of Augusta posted a proposal about operating hours at a public facility
and opened a public comment period." Both from the Civic Ledger.

People can be asked to meet. Asking costs no time; the meeting will. A meeting
can be arranged between two dates the game names. Cynthia Schmidt was last in
touch on January 1, 2002, and the game says "It has been a long while."

### The one seated body is the one you are not told you are in

The Government screen works and is one of the better ones: Who governs, a Map,
Local meetings and records, and a "Represented by · your districts" panel that
says plainly it follows where you live rather than where you are browsing.

In Columbus, Ohio it reads:

> U.S. House District not recorded

The US Congress is the only body in this game with real people sitting in it,
measured below rather than carried. It is also the one body the player is shown
no connection to. The
screen is being honest rather than guessing, which is right, but the effect is
that the only populated legislature in the world is the one you cannot see
yourself inside.

Budget and economy is the same shape and gets it right: "No public service
records are kept for Here: Columbus, Ohio in this game." An absence stated as
an absence.

## How much of the country is actually playable

From `docs/systems/nationwide-rule-coverage.md` on this head, which is generated
rather than written, and whose numbers are about compiled rules rather than
about what a player can reach:

- 50 states. **9** have a compiled legislative pack at all.
- **2** states where a legislator's qualifications are admitted for at least
  one chamber. That is the number in the by-state heading; the Totals block
  counts a stricter threshold, standing for a seat, and gets the same 2 on this
  head. Both are rule admission, and neither means a player can file — Alaska is
  one of the two, and Alaska refuses.
- **1** state with an admitted legislative term rule.
- 38,704 general-purpose governments in the catalogue. **3** have a single
  admitted local action between them.
- Of 19,489 municipalities, **3** have a compiled enacted instrument. Of those
  three, **1** can pass an ordinance and **2** can pass an appropriation. Two of
  the three are in Virginia and one in Nevada; the report does not name units,
  and the research-audit lane reads them as Charlottesville, Richmond and Carson
  City.
- Everything else is thinner than that. **38,381** of the 38,704 are known by
  identity only — a name and a place, no compiled rules at all — and **320**
  carry nothing but an inherited default.
- `body.seats` is the first missing mandatory field for passing an ordinance in
  **38,701** of them. That is a missing compiled rule — the record of how many
  seats the body has — and not a count of empty chairs. Whether anybody is
  sitting in those offices is a different measurement, and this walk did not
  take it.

So "38,701 of 38,704 cannot pass an ordinance" and "one government in 38,704
can" are answers to two different questions, and both want their threshold said
out loud. The shape of the gap is the same either way: there is machinery to
decide what a body may do, and almost nothing compiled for it to decide about.

## Who is actually seated

Taken after the rest of this walk, because the coverage report measures
compiled rules and says so, and nobody had counted people in a save. Five
lives, each made through the ordinary creator on this head, in Augusta
Arkansas, Columbus Georgia, Tucson Arizona, Billings Missouri and Sitka
Alaska. Counts are from the game's own Congress reader and from the save's
organisation records, not from the coverage report.

**Congress is genuinely seated, in every one of the five.** 435 House seats and
100 Senate seats, every one of them carrying either a person or an explicitly
recorded vacancy. Members came out at 432, 435, 433, 433 and 435 in the House
and 99 in the Senate every time, with the remaining seats held as stated
vacancies rather than as blanks. So the figure that has been passed between
lanes as "535 people" is 535 **seats**; the people are 531 to 535 of them, and
which seats are empty varies by seed. That is the right shape — a real chamber
has vacancies — and it is worth having the number be the number.

**Nothing else in the world has anybody in it.** A save holds 23 or 24
organisations in total. Counting the people its records tie to each one:

| Organisation in the Augusta save                                  | People its records tie to it |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------: |
| United States House of Representatives                            |                          432 |
| United States Senate                                              |                           99 |
| Presidency of the United States                                   |                            1 |
| Supreme Court of the United States                                |                            1 |
| Governor of Arkansas                                              |                            1 |
| City of Augusta                                                   |                            0 |
| County of Woodruff                                                |                            0 |
| three local schools, a youth service club, a neighbourhood market |                       0 each |

That is the Augusta save and the other four differ only in their place names.
The two chambers, a president, a chief justice and a governor. The city and the
county exist as one record each with nobody attached.

**There is no state legislature at all.** Not an empty one — absent. No save in
the five carries an organisation for one, which is a stronger statement than
the coverage report's "no compiled pack" and consistent with it.

The whole population of a save is 555 to 559 people. Subtract Congress and
between twenty and twenty-five people are left: the player, their household,
the four or five people they know, a governor, a president, a chief justice and
the handful of press bylines. That is the world outside the one seated
building.

One limit on the method, stated because it cuts against a number above. The
per-organisation count reads people an event ties to an organisation directly,
and the party chapters come out at zero — yet the walk saw named organizers on
the chapters screen. So the chapters record their people by a route this count
does not see, and their zero is a limit of the measurement rather than a
finding. The Congress figures come from the game's own reader and do not depend
on it.

## What this walk did not establish

- **When Alaska would open.** The refusal is about a recorded residence
  interval, so presumably a player who lives in Sitka for a year and then
  stands would be admitted. I did not play a year. That is the measurement
  worth taking next, and it is the one that would actually tell him Alaska
  works.
- **Whether the headline fix is meant to cover more than party stories.** It may
  be a deliberate first slice. The measurement says what it covers today; it
  does not say what was intended.
- **Anything about the policy catalogue, art registration, or the state
  legislatures beyond candidacy.** They were on the list and I did not reach
  them. Not a verdict on them — simply unwalked.
- **How the game behaves over a long life.** Everything here is a first day or
  sixty simulated days. Nothing in this document is evidence about year ten.

## What is in good shape

Worth saying plainly, because the list above is all defects and the balance is
not what it implies.

Traits come from packs the simulation honours, even if only packs inside the
build so far. Papers can hold their
own voice and one story family proves it. Party chapters have real things to
attend, with named organizers. The civics Guide is twenty-eight honest
definitions and says outright that reading one does nothing in the world.

And the screens that do not know something say so, rather than guessing or
printing a zero: no personal balance on record, no supported economic
observations for this place and date, no public service records kept here, US
House district not recorded. That is the hardest habit to build in a simulation
and this one has it.
