# What candidates do in a campaign

The [action catalog](action-catalog.json) lists 42 concrete activities for Team F's campaign menu. Each record names the people, setting, purpose, time driver, cash driver, and source. A player can spend scarce hours in a conversation, canvass, forum, or fundraiser instead of buying an abstract score.

## Source boundary

The sources support the existence and purpose of these activities. The National Democratic Institute manuals are campaign-practice guides, including contexts outside the United States. Federal Election Commission pages establish federal finance treatment for several activities. The League of Women Voters materials establish candidate forums and voter-guide formats. Those sources do **not** set universal minutes, dollars, contact rates, or electoral effects. The catalog leaves those quantities unknown. A state or local campaign must use its own compliance rules; the federal examples do not authorize conduct elsewhere.

## For Team F

- Select actions by their identity, name, participants, setting, and purpose to assemble scenes or choices.
- Use the recorded time and cash drivers to show why the same action can take different hours or money. An unknown value is not a free or instantaneous action.
- Resolve actual campaign funds, work hours, people, location, and election law through the game's canonical systems before charging an action or writing its result.
- Put any chosen hours, prices, and effects in a separately labeled gameplay calibration. They are not measured facts in this catalog.
- Keep sources in data and developer review. No source URLs or citations belong on the player screen.

## Evidence gaps

The [campaign evidence packet](campaign-evidence.json) records bounded observations rather than a universal cost table. In Massachusetts's 2022 election, 274 state House candidates spent an average of $32,665 and 76 state Senate candidates spent an average of $84,782, according to the [state campaign finance office](https://ocpf2.blob.core.windows.net/pdf/studies/2022legislativestudy.pdf). Those are state means, including unopposed races. A [2008 survey of contested state House candidates in 18 states](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/2053168015605105) found roughly 47 to 49 campaign hours a week in several reported groups, while many candidates also worked outside jobs. These are observations from a sample, not a schedule for every character.

A [U.S. field-experiment paper](https://sites.temple.edu/nickerson/files/2017/07/Nickerson.APR2005.pdf) describes volunteer contact ranges of 3 to 8 people an hour at doors and 15 to 20 by phone. Those are contacts, not knocks or dials. The [FEC's 2024 table](https://www.fec.gov/resources/campaign-finance-statistics/2024/tables/congressional/ConCand4_2024_21m.pdf) shows why candidate status matters: median receipts through September 2024 were $1.73 million for Democratic House incumbents and $67,362 for Democratic House challengers. Receipts are not spending. New York City's [2025 City Council program limit](https://nyccfb.info/candidate-services/limits-thresholds/2025) was $228,000 for a primary, but a legal cap is not actual or national spending.

No comparable national median spending, staffing, volunteer count, or candidate-week measure was established for every requested office level. The packet leaves those fields null and names the gaps. The existing candidate-qualification corpus also leaves filing fees and petition requirements unestablished. Team F can use the catalog as a sourced action vocabulary now; hours, prices, and electoral effects still need a separate, labeled gameplay calibration.
