# Fertility intentions are plans, not birth probabilities

U.S. survey evidence distinguishes wanting a child, intending one, timing and certainty. Age, existing children, partners and resources differ across these measures. The evidence supports recorded household circumstances for Team 3’s design. It does not supply a universal intention-to-birth conversion, monthly birth chance or causal income weight. The bounded packet is partial; no simulation result or calibrated writer is claimed.

## 2. Research

Measured descriptive findings from Badolato, Hayford and Guzzo, [Multiple dimensions of uncertainty in fertility goals: recent trends and patterns in the United States](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12130082/), 2025:

- NSFG repeated cross-sections, 2002–2019; analytical sample 41,492 women ages 15–44. Weighted period estimates represent 2002, 2008, 2012, 2014, 2016 and 2018. This is not a panel following these women to births.
- Among women intending more children, Table 2 gives 7 percent “not at all sure,” 36 percent “somewhat sure” and 57 percent “very sure.” These are certainty responses, not observed conversion rates.
- Women ages 30–44 intending more children: more than half were uncertain about realizing their intentions; “not at all sure” reached 17 percent in 2008. About one-third of intending women ages 15–29 were uncertain.
- Women at or above 200 percent of the poverty level were slightly more certain than those below it. The narrative supplies direction, not a numeric causal income effect or a discontinuity at 200 percent. Exact income-specific confidence intervals were not extracted.
- Among intending women with a bachelor’s degree, “very sure” declined from 65 percent in 2014 to 54 percent in 2018. This is a descriptive time comparison, not an education-policy effect.
- The survey asks intended timing in less than two versus at least two years. Partnered women answer about joint intentions; unpartnered women answer about their own intentions. Do not treat a joint response as independently measured agreement from both partners.

Measured descriptive findings from Badolato and Hayford, [Trends and Levels in Men’s and Women’s Fertility Goals in the United States](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12827432/), 2026:

- In the 2018 period, among ages 35–44 who intended a child, 77 percent of women and 54 percent of men intended one within two years. This is a sex-specific timing comparison conditional on positive intentions.
- Retrospective wantedness analysis covers 4,433 men and women ages 15–44 who had a child in the two years before interview, using NSFG 2011–2019. In 2018, 55 percent of women and 61 percent of men described the birth as occurring at the right time. Earlier-than-wanted births were 21 percent for women and 18 percent for men.
- These retrospective birth reports do not estimate prospective conversion among everyone who intended a child. Different respondents and denominators cannot be joined into a transition rate.

Primary abstract evidence from [Fertility Intentions, Career Considerations and Subsequent Births: The Moderating Effects of Women’s Work Hours](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10834-012-9331-2), 2013: panel analysis of 2,411 National Survey of Families and Households respondents finds career considerations mediate the work-hours/fertility association. Intentions and career considerations predicted births more strongly with greater work hours. Only the primary abstract was available in this pass; coefficients, confidence intervals, follow-up duration and detailed sample restrictions remain unverified. Do not implement numeric weights from this abstract.

## 3. Breadth, ranges and limits

Research inference: keep age, parity, both partners’ recorded intentions, intended timing, certainty and resource constraints distinct. Income associations do not justify a universal income-to-birth multiplier. Desired or intended timing is not a biological guarantee or a deadline after which birth happens automatically.

Observed descriptive spans above are comparisons across groups or years, not research uncertainty ranges for effects links. Neither the 54–77 percent timing comparison nor the 54–65 percent education/time comparison is an admissible drawnLinkSize range. Confidence intervals must be extracted for the relevant statistic before recommending a range; no invented ± spread is supplied here.

The NSFG frame and questionnaire exclude or incompletely represent institutionalized people and some partner/gender configurations. National U.S. samples do not establish all 56 jurisdiction-specific patterns or territorial effects. Male parity measures have birth underreporting concerns; the uncertainty analysis excludes men because unpartnered men were not asked the necessary certainty questions.

The source narrative and pooled table contain differently summarized overall positive-intention proportions. This packet deliberately does not recommend an overall intention prevalence until the denominator discrepancy is reconciled.
